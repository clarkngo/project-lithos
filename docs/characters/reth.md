# Chairman Reth

**Role:** The Mining Guild's ruling Board, in a plain grey coat — Book Three's central antagonist, and the series' clearest case that an opponent can be right about the problem and still dangerous about the solution.

## Physical description

Confirmed on the page (Book Three, Act One, first appearance):

- Small, thin, plain. Wears a grey coat cut for use rather than display; hair gone white in a way that suggests he stopped caring about its color long ago.
- Carries no papers into the hall — a clerk brings a slim leather ledger when needed.
- Keeps a second, private ledger at home, cracked-spined, forty years old, with a column of small crosses beside names of the drifting who died unattended.

## Voice / dialogue notes

Quiet, dry, very clear. Never raises his voice; never needs to. Speaks in numbers before he speaks in appeals — a death count delivered plainly, meant to be remembered rather than wept over.

Not cruel. Not greedy. The style guide's rule against cartoon antagonists is load-bearing for him specifically: his argument is sound, and that is what makes him dangerous rather than simply villainous.

## Relationships

- **Caelen** — respects him on sight ("the finest argument for my petition that could be devised... the one in eleven hundred who lived"); their final exchange on the hall's steps is read as genuine mutual understanding rather than defeat.
- **Director Kell** — his subordinate on the Board; demotes and later dismisses her for testifying against his own petition, without personal malice.
- **Captain Roshe** — the Warden captain whose drift, discovered live in the hearing, finally moves Reth to write a name in the "Survived" column of his private ledger for the first time in forty years.

## Arc summary

<details>
<summary>Contains Book Three outline spoilers</summary>

Introduced via his petition for a single, enforceable, Guild-funded standard governing all Lithoi remains and Resonance use — framed as mercy, built from forty years of a private ledger recording every unlicensed death four institutions refused to take responsibility for. His case is never dishonest: the territory's fractured doctrine genuinely kills people no single authority answers for.

Loses the vote on his own petition (22–19, for Maren's amended finding) when Captain Roshe begins drifting live in the hearing — proof that even the Wardens' own licensed enforcement housings cause the harm his register was meant to prevent. Accepts the finding with genuine grace rather than retaliation, promises to "return, with names," and privately begins a column in his own ledger headed "Survived" — the first entry in forty years. Not defeated; changed, and still coming back.
