# Corwin

**Role:** Second son of House Vael's lower ring — the found family's first real foothold inside Spire politics.

## Physical description

Confirmed on the page (Book Three, Act One, first appearance):

- Perhaps Rook's age. Slight, dark-haired, a coat of good grey cloth mended at one cuff by someone who cared.
- Moves through the Spire's formal grammar with perfect friendliness and no deference — a performance that reads, on closer watch, as its own kind of defiance.

## Voice / dialogue notes

Light and wry on the surface, with a genuine rueful humor about his own uselessness. The lightness drops away entirely when his sister comes up.

Teaches Caelen and Rook the Spire's "language under the language" — courtesy as a vocabulary of orders and refusals — in the same practical, survival-instruction register Varrick once used to teach Caelen Resonance.

## Relationships

- **Lisbet** — his sister, fitted at fifteen with a licensed conduit their family thought would protect her; it slipped within weeks, and the Synod's own physicians called it "nerves" for two years before anyone acted. His reason for writing to the camp in the first place.
- **House Vael** — disowns him by letter, in a single line, once his hosting of Caelen's crew becomes known.
- **Caelen's crew** — hosts them at his mother's green-doored house in the lower ring, which becomes, by Book Three's end, the first of the hall's publicly funded houses of treatment.

## Arc summary

<details>
<summary>Contains Book Three outline spoilers</summary>

Introduced at the Spire's outer gate, having written to the camp a month earlier about his drifting sister Lisbet. Teaches the found family how to survive Spire court grammar, and feeds Malakar's network intelligence on Chairman Reth's petition through his own clerk's connections. Disowned by House Vael, in a single unsigned line, once his hosting of the party becomes public — a price he says he decided to pay "knowingly."

Chooses, with Caelen, to answer his father's writ of guardianship over Lisbet by bringing her into the hall's public protection rather than letting her be used as either a locked-ward object lesson or a rhetorical weapon — refusing, on principle, to let anyone else's cause spend her life for her. By the book's end, his mother's house is the first house of treatment the hall funds, and Lisbet runs it.
