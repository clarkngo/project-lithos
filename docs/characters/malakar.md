## Voice / dialogue notes

Warden Enforcers speak in procedural, clause-stacked sentences — the grammar of people who have learned that precise language is what keeps them blameless later.

Malakar specifically: clinical precision that frays, almost imperceptibly, whenever doctrine and conscience collide — a stalled clause, a word chosen and then replaced. Do not underline the fraying. Let it sit in the syntax.

Avoid making him cartoonishly cruel. He does violence through paperwork and doctrine.

## Relationships

- **Caelen** — assigned the strata-child case; the hunt is professional before it is personal. By Book Two an ally; by Book Three, the man he promised Orell he would see safely through to the end.
- **The Warden Enforcers** — his order and his language. Formally renounces it in Book Two; by Book Three has built a sympathizer network inside it instead.
- **The Synod** — Wardens enforce; the Synod adjudicates moral status. The exact reporting line between them is undetermined.
- **Captain Orell** — once his superior at Kesh Hollow, assigns him the strata-child case in Book One. Hunts him in Book Two, then defects alongside him after reading what the order did at Greyfen. By Book Three, his closest partner, running a house of treatment for drifting Wardens at Kesh Hollow.
- **Petra Halsey** — a garrison clerk recruited into his Book Two sympathizer network; her case becomes public testimony in Book Three's hearing.

## Arc summary

<details>
<summary>Contains Book One–Three outline spoilers</summary>

Book One, Act One: first appearance as a controlled outside POV insert — procedural, closing in on Hollow Cut, authorized for the raid that burns it.

Book One, Act Two: senior within the Wardens, still on Resonance cases. Ordered to suppress Vault knowledge rather than enforce law evenly, which frays him further. He defects from open pursuit into a wary, transactional alliance with Caelen's crew — echoing, structurally, Varrick's own transactional taking-in of Caelen. Trust is negotiated term by term. At the climax he has to decide which side of his doctrine he actually believes.

Book Two: formally renounced by the order. Builds a sympathizer network one careful name at a time — a clerk, a line Warden, a garrison surgeon — starting with nothing more than quiet refusals to report. Confronted by Captain Orell, his own former colleague, sent to investigate the network; instead of arresting him, Orell asks whether the order he serves is the one he actually believes in, and eventually defects alongside him after a Warden containment operation kills a mother and son over an unlicensed rescue Shift (Greyfen).

Book Three: puts his own name on a public statement of testimony for the first time in his career — "I am here in my own name," with no doctrinal clause to hide behind. Helps expose that Warden enforcement-housings themselves cause Drift in their wearers after a decade's use, discovered when Captain Roshe — who signed the Greyfen order — begins drifting in open hearing. Runs, by the book's end, a house of treatment for Wardens at Kesh Hollow alongside Orell.
