# Chapter One — The Glass Wastes

Nine years teaches a body its own arithmetic, whether or not it ever wanted the lesson.

Caelen ate before first light now, always, a fixed measured portion regardless of hunger, because hunger had long since stopped being a reliable narrator of what his body actually needed and started being, instead, a thing to be managed the way a man manages a debt he already knows he can't fully pay off — steadily, on schedule, never trusting the creditor's mood. He counted the ration out by weight in his palm without needing to look at it anymore, dense traveling bread and dried strip-meat and a hard curl of rendered fat that Varrick still called, from some habit twenty years older than either of them, *runner's tallow*, and ate it standing at the mouth of the shelter, watching the Glass Wastes go from black to bruised grey to the flat hard silver of a sun that never seemed to warm the ground it fell on.

He was not the boy who'd come over the ridge nine years ago. He had stopped, some years back, expecting to feel that fact as a loss, the way it had felt in the first months after Hollow Cut. It simply was, now, the way a scar simply is — visible if you knew to look, unremarkable if you didn't. He stood taller than Varrick these days, leaner through the shoulders than any grown man ought to be who ate as much as he did, his hands scarred faint and permanent at the nail-beds, his jaw carrying, still, the architecture of something that had needed, more than a few times now, to be considerably more than a boy's. He wore, strapped to his left forearm beneath his sleeve, a conduit housing that had never once held a core — brass, dulled deliberately with the same acid-wash Varrick used to age forged papers, worn openly in any settlement they passed through, because a boy with no visible conduit and an unlicensed Resonance nobody could explain was still, after nine years, the single most dangerous thing either of them could be caught being in daylight. The empty housing was Varrick's idea, three years old now, and it had worked exactly as well as every other lie Varrick had ever built to keep them both breathing.

Rook was already awake behind him, crouched over the morning's gear with the particular unhurried thoroughness that had replaced, somewhere in the years since Hollow Cut, the restless motion of his hands. He still talked fast when he talked at all — that much the years hadn't touched — but the talking came in shorter bursts now, spent more carefully, the way Caelen spent his own hunger. He had left them once, in the third year, for the better part of a season — no argument, no scene, only a morning where his bedroll simply wasn't rolled up with the others and a note, badly spelled, that said he needed *some air that didn't smell like you two*. He had come back before the season turned, thinner, quieter, and neither Caelen nor Varrick had ever asked him what he'd gone looking for out there alone, because some questions, Caelen had learned from a dead man's example, were better left in the ground they'd come from. He had simply resumed his place at the fire, and the place had simply let him back in, and that had been the whole of the reckoning either of them required.

"Two crates," Rook said now, not looking up, checking lashings with fingers gone permanently sure despite the crooked arm that still ached him every wet season, exactly as Varrick had once promised it would. "Salvage broker in Underlow's paying triple weight if we bring them through the Ashfall stretch instead of round it. Says the long way's got Guild survey crews thick as flies this month."

"And the short way's got what," Varrick said, easing himself down onto an overturned crate with the particular careful grunt of a man whose knees had started keeping their own ledger of every year spent walking scarred ground. He had gone properly grey since Hollow Cut, thinner in the face, slower rising in the mornings — not frail, not yet, but no longer a man who could out-walk trouble the way he once had, a fact all three of them managed around without ever once discussing it plainly. "Nobody offers triple weight for a stretch of ground that's merely inconvenient, Rook. Triple weight means somebody else already priced in the dying."

"Ashfall's not that bad anymore." Rook shrugged, though something in the set of his shoulders suggested he didn't entirely believe his own reassurance. "Runoff's mostly settled since the spring rains. Caelen's crossed worse."

Caelen had crossed worse. That, too, had become simple arithmetic over nine years — a body that could spend itself in ways no licensed Resonant would ever survive spending, provided the spending was managed, rationed, chosen rather than seized. He had learned, in the slow grinding years since the weigh-station, to hold the coal behind his sternum unlit for weeks at a stretch when the work didn't call for it, and to light only as much of it as any given crossing actually required — a half-Shift now, when the old undivided fullness of it wasn't needed, enough reach and enough strength to haul a stuck cart free of glass-fused ground without ever tipping past the careful line into anything the old hungry undertow could use against him. He still felt it there, always, patient beneath the discipline the way a banked coal is always patient beneath its own ash. He had simply gotten very good, over nine years, at never handing it more than the day's work could afford to pay for.

"Ashfall, then," Varrick said, after a moment's consideration that both of them understood as a formality — Varrick's opinion carried weight in this trio, but he had long since stopped pretending the final say on a route through poisoned ground belonged to anyone but the one of them who could survive the ground being wrong about its own dangers. "But we go in light, and we go in watching, and if either of you sees so much as a survey stake that shouldn't be there, we turn around and eat the loss on the triple weight rather than argue with a Guild patrol on their own contested ground."

---

The Wastes at full light were a country of their own particular violence, one Caelen had come, in nine years, to understand rather than merely fear. The great cracked plates of fused glass-stone stretched out flat and silver-dark toward a horizon broken here and there by the rusted ribs of abandoned extraction rigs, and underfoot the ground shifted texture without warning — solid fused rock giving way, sometimes within a single stride, to ash-fine drift deep enough to swallow a leg to the knee, or to pockets of ground gone soft and poisonous-pale where some old extraction fire had burned so hot and so long that nothing, not even the patient scavenger-grass that had reclaimed the Wastes' gentler edges, had ever found purchase there again.

He walked it the way he'd once walked the flooding gallery beneath the mountain, low attention held loose and total at once, reading the ground the way Rook read a lane's crowd for trouble before it arrived. The old service-shaft instinct that had carried him blind through stone as a child had never fully left him; it had simply matured, over the years, into something closer to a craft than an accident, a body that still, at the marrow, knew the shape of wounded ground better than it had any right to.

Ashfall lay two days in, a stretch where an old extraction fire had burned so hot it had fused the very air above the ground into something that shimmered, permanently, with heat that hadn't been real for a generation — a mirage laid over poisoned earth, beautiful in the specific terrible way the Wastes were sometimes beautiful, and Caelen had learned, crossing it now for perhaps the ninth or tenth time in his life, not to trust a single thing his own eyes told him about the distance between one safe footing and the next.

"Cart's caught," Rook called back, from where he'd gone ahead to scout the crossing's narrowest point, and Caelen was already moving before Varrick had finished the low curse that answered the news, because a stuck cart on ground this unstable was not an inconvenience — it was a clock, ticking down toward whatever came looking, drawn by the noise of three people and one struggling animal standing still too long in open ground.

He put his shoulder to the wheel where it had sunk to the axle in a pocket of ash-drift he hadn't caught in time to steer around, and let the coal behind his sternum light — carefully, deliberately, only as much as the wheel required, the old wrenching heat arriving now familiar rather than terrifying, his skin drawing tight at the shoulder in a way that had long since stopped surprising him, his fingers lengthening just enough to find true purchase on the wheel's rim without splintering the wood it gripped. It cost him what it always cost him at this measure: a wave of cold once the cart broke free and the coal guttered back down to its bank, a moment's unsteadiness in his knees, nothing that a slightly larger portion at the evening meal wouldn't settle.

"Nine years ago that would've been half your body weight and a fortnight's recovery," Varrick observed, dry, hauling the cart's lead rope taut once the wheel came clear, the old humor arriving a half-beat slower than it used to, but arriving all the same. "I'll admit I did not, in my more honest moments back then, expect to live long enough to see you make it look boring."

"Boring's the goal," Caelen said, and found, saying it, that he meant it entirely — that the whole slow architecture of the last nine years, all of Varrick's hard lessons and all of Rook's stubborn returning and all of the discipline Caelen had built for himself out of nothing but a stone-cold coal and the memory of a forge-keeper's last words, had been aimed, the entire time, at exactly this: a Shift that cost a wave of cold instead of a body's whole reserve, a crisis that resolved itself in a sentence instead of a settlement burning.

They cleared Ashfall by evening, the shimmering false-heat mirage giving way at last to the flatter, honester silver of ordinary poisoned ground, and made camp in the lee of a collapsed extraction rig whose rusted ribs, in the failing light, still looked to Caelen exactly the way they had the first time he'd seen this country from the ridge above Hollow Cut — the remains of something enormous that had died standing up, and simply never been permitted to fall the rest of the way down.

It was there, crouched over the evening fire with the last of the day's careful ration in his hands, that Caelen heard, for the first time in longer than he could easily place, the particular sound of someone running — light, fast, stumbling, entirely unlike the patient unhurried tread of anything wearing Guild grey or Warden brass — closing on their fire out of the dark with the unmistakable desperation of a person who had run out of every other direction worth running in.
