# Chapter Six — The Inquisitor's Alliance

He came alone, which was the first thing that told Caelen something about this meeting was not going to run the way every prior meeting with grey-and-brass had run.

Caelen heard him a long while before the others did — the unhurried, deliberate tread of a man who wanted to be heard approaching, no attempt at the disciplined stealth the retrieval party had used, no dogs, no torchlight signaling a wider cordon closing from unseen angles. Just one set of boots, moving steady across the Sinking Fields' scarred crust, and Caelen was on his feet with the coal behind his sternum already answering before Varrick's low warning had finished leaving his mouth.

"Stand down," the man called, from twenty strides off, hands lifted open and empty, his voice carrying the same clause-stacked precision Caelen remembered from a burning lane nine years gone, though something in the delivery had worn thinner since, closer now to plain speech straining against an old habit than to doctrine recited whole. "I am not here under recovery authority. I want that established before anyone's hand does anything neither of us can walk back."

Varrick's pry-bar did not lower. Rook's knife did not lower either. But Caelen, watching the man's face in the flat grey afternoon light — older than he remembered, grey threading in at the temples now, the same careful stillness he'd caught once before at a treeline while a settlement burned — let the coal bank back down, because some instinct older than caution recognized, before his mind had fully caught up to the recognition, a man who had once chosen not to close a cordon he'd been ordered to hold.

"Malakar," Caelen said, and heard, in his own voice, the flat certainty of a name he'd never once spoken aloud before and had somehow, across nine years, never fully forgotten either.

"Inquisitor-Commander, technically, though I suspect the title will mean considerably less to all four of you than it used to mean to me." Malakar came no closer, stopped at a distance that Caelen understood, watching him, was deliberate — close enough to talk, far enough that no one's hand needed to feel crowded into a decision. "I have a proposal. I would ask that you hear the whole of it before any of you decide what to do with the man making it."

---

They did not lower their weapons fully, even once he'd sat, at Varrick's terse instruction, on the far side of the small shielded fire — a careful distance maintained the whole while, the old wariness of grey-and-brass too deeply set in all of them to shed for one man's open hands and quiet voice. Seraphina had gone very still at the fire's edge, the shard's oilcloth bundle drawn close against her chest, watching Malakar with the particular sharp attention of someone recalculating, yet again, the shape of a danger she'd thought she understood.

"I was ordered," Malakar said, without preamble, laying the words out the same precise way he had once, Caelen imagined, laid out a case file square against a desk's edge, "to recover a Synod-sealed item and ask nothing about its contents. I was ordered this by a Custodian whose authority I am not permitted to question, in language built specifically to survive any tribunal that might later ask why I obeyed it. I have spent eleven years building a career on the belief that precise doctrine kept my conscience clean of the order's worse instincts. I no longer believe that. I want to be direct about the fact that I no longer believe it, because I suspect all four of you have earned considerably better than another man's careful evasions."

"That's a fine speech," Varrick said, dry, though the dryness carried less ease than usual, "for a man who spent a night watching a settlement burn without once ordering his own line to stand down properly."

Something crossed Malakar's face at that — not defensiveness, Caelen noted, but something closer to a debt being acknowledged rather than disputed. "I gave the order for non-lethal containment," he said, quiet. "It was disobeyed by a man under my command who panicked, and a forge-keeper died protecting a boy who was never, by any doctrine I have ever sworn to, supposed to exist in the first place. I have carried that account since, in my own private ledger, considerably more honestly than I have carried most of what the order's official ledgers ask of me. I do not offer that as absolution. I offer it only because I suspect you would smell the lie instantly if I pretended the debt wasn't there."

Rook's knife had not lowered, but something in his stance had, minutely, eased. Caelen understood why; it was the same thing that had eased something in his own chest, hearing it — not forgiveness, nothing so whole as that, but the particular relief of hearing a hard true thing said plainly instead of armored.

"Here is my proposal," Malakar went on, "stated in terms I would be willing to defend before any tribunal that ever asked, because I have found, over considerable practice, that a proposal built to survive scrutiny is generally also the only kind worth offering honestly. I know the recovery orders currently active against you, all of them, Warden and otherwise, because I hold the desk that issues most of them. I can misdirect my own order's pursuit — reroute patrols, misfile reports, buy you considerably more room to move than you currently have. I can do this for as long as I remain in position to do it, which will not be indefinitely, because a Commander who consistently sends his own Wardens toward empty ground eventually draws attention even doctrine can't fully deflect."

"And what," Varrick said, flat, "does a man like you want, in trade for a gift like that. Because I've built a fair portion of my own life on the understanding that nobody offers a gift this size without a price attached, and I'd rather hear the price now than discover it later, at a worse hour than this one."

Malakar was quiet a long moment, and Caelen watched something in him weigh a decision that had, evidently, cost him considerably more to reach than the rest of the speech had.

"I want the truth," he said, finally, plain. "Not the Synod's version of it, sealed and doled out in careful thin-worded orders that ask me to trust an authority without ever earning that trust honestly. I have spent eleven years enforcing a doctrine I believed kept the peace. I have recently been asked to enforce a *silence* instead, dressed up in the same language, and I find I can no longer tell the two apart with the confidence my career has always required of me. I want to see, with my own eyes, whatever this item actually leads to — not to report it, not to recover it for anyone's ledger, Guild or Synod or otherwise, but because I am no longer willing to spend the rest of my service enforcing an order I do not understand the shape of. Call it a Warden's version of the same want that drove your archivist here to run in the first place." His eyes moved, briefly, to Seraphina, who held his gaze without flinching. "I imagine she'll recognize the shape of it better than any of the rest of you."

"That's not a small thing to ask," Caelen said, slow, and heard, saying it, an echo of a voice from nine years gone — a boy in a tree, weighing a stranger's open hand against every instinct that told him to run. "Trust, for information. That's the whole shape of it, isn't it. Not so different from a scavenger once deciding a starving boy was worth the trouble of carrying."

Varrick made a sound that was almost a laugh, dry and startled, and looked at Caelen with something that, in the firelight, might have been pride. "Boy's got a memory," he said, to Malakar, though the words carried the particular weight of a man measuring an old bargain against a new one and finding the shape uncomfortably familiar. "I didn't do charity either, when I found him. Told him plain: I keep you fed, you're worth something to me eventually, and we'd sort the terms out as we went rather than pretend either of us knew the whole shape of it in advance." He fed the fire a stick, watched it catch, unhurried despite everything. "Seems only fair we extend a Warden the same courtesy we once extended a strata child. Terms as we go. No promises further out than either side can actually see."

"That is," Malakar said, and something in his clause-stacked precision loosened, just slightly, into something that might, in a different man, have been the beginning of relief, "considerably more generous than most terms I've been offered in this line of work."

"Don't mistake it for generous," Rook said, flat, though he had, at last, lowered the knife. "Mistake it for careful. First sign you're steering us anywhere that ends with grey-and-brass closing a cordon around this fire, I will personally make sure you regret trusting your own doctrine over your own eyes." He held Malakar's gaze a long moment, the old fast bravado entirely absent from it, replaced by something considerably harder-won. "But I watched a man choose not to close a cordon once, years back, same as you're claiming now. I'm willing to spend a little trust finding out whether that choosing meant anything, or whether it was just a good man's luck running out at a convenient moment for the story he's telling himself."

Malakar inclined his head, slow, accepting the terms exactly as they'd been offered — provisional, wary, negotiated rather than given — and Caelen, watching the four of them settle into an uneasy new arithmetic around the small shielded fire, understood that the crew that would carry the shard the rest of the way toward whatever answer it held had just, cautiously, become five.

"Rest while you can," Malakar said, rising, the old procedural cadence settling back over him like a coat put on out of habit rather than conviction. "I've bought you perhaps four days before my own absence from garrison starts drawing questions I can't misfile as easily as a patrol report. I'd suggest we use them."
