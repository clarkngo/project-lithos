# Chapter Three — What the Synod Wants Sealed

Seraphina slept until well past the hour Caelen would normally have had them packed and moving, and none of them woke her for it, though Caelen noticed, watching Varrick tend the morning fire with one eye constantly on the flat silver horizon, that the man's dry stillness had gone considerably more watchful than usual. Four retrieval men driven off in the dark did not mean four retrieval men accounted for. It meant, at best, a delay Caelen understood better than most how quickly the Wastes could eat.

She woke with the particular disoriented panic of someone who had trained her whole life never to sleep past the hour she intended, and was on her feet with her back to the rig's rusted ribs before her eyes had fully cleared, until she placed the fire, and the three of them around it, and let herself, visibly, breathe again.

"You're still here," she said, and something in the observation carried a weight that suggested she had genuinely expected otherwise.

"Triple weight on the salvage run's already lost," Varrick said, dry, not looking up from the fire. "Figured we might as well see what else the morning's willing to cost us before we start totaling the damage." He glanced up then, and whatever humor had been in his voice thinned considerably going into the next part. "But I'll want the whole shape of it now, girl. Not the courtesy version you gave us in the dark with four men closing. We put three lives and one very tired body between you and your House's own retrieval party last night. That buys a fuller accounting than *I require a moment.*"

Seraphina was quiet a while, turning something over — Caelen could see the old formal architecture rebuilding itself behind her eyes, weighing exactly how much of the truth her training told her was safe to spend. Then, visibly, she set the rebuilding aside.

She reached into the roughened lining of her travel coat and drew out a shard wrapped in layered oilcloth and Synod-sealed wax, unwrapping it with hands that had gone, for the first time since the fire had found her, entirely steady.

It was marrow-glass, but unlike any Caelen had handled in nine years of runner's work. Most cut cores held their warmth quiet and even, a banked coal's patience. This one pulsed — faint, slow, a rhythm that struck something in Caelen's chest before his mind had finished registering the shard at all, a low answering ache behind his sternum that he had not felt, with anything like this intensity, since the last true memory he carried of the dark beneath the mountain.

"It's called, in what little Synod doctrine ever admits its existence at all, a Record-Core," Seraphina said, watching Caelen's reaction to it with an attention that told him she had expected exactly this. "Not fuel. Not a conduit component. Memory, vitrified the same way marrow is vitrified — a full accounting, laid down by Lithoi hands before the Culling ever began, of where the deepest and oldest of their remains were gathered and sealed, deliberately, by the Lithoi themselves, against exactly the kind of extraction that killed the rest of them."

"The Vault," Varrick said, low, and Caelen heard in it the particular flat carefulness of a man recognizing a word he'd only ever heard as rumor suddenly acquire the weight of fact.

"The Vault," Seraphina agreed. "Its location is recorded nowhere the Guild has ever managed to find, though God knows they've spent three generations and no small fortune trying. The Synod has always claimed, publicly, that the location was lost with the Lithoi themselves — an honest tragedy, a closed door, nothing to be done about it. That claim is a lie. The Synod has held this shard, and the location it carries, sealed in a private reliquary beneath the Spire's own halls, for longer than any living Synod official will admit to knowing about."

"Why," Caelen asked, and found his own voice had gone quieter than he intended, his hand — without his fully deciding it should — reaching toward the shard before some older, more careful instinct stopped it a hand's width short. "Why keep it, if they're only going to keep it sealed. Why not destroy it."

"Because destroying it is a different kind of confession than merely hiding it," Seraphina said, and here, for the first time, Caelen heard something harder enter her voice, something that had clearly cost her considerably more than the shard's physical theft had. "The Synod's whole authority rests on a single doctrine, repeated in every canon hall from here to the Spire's own chapel: that the Culling was an unrepeatable tragedy, a wound the world has finished healing from, that peace — enforced, doctrinal, unbreakable peace — is the only decent memorial left to build over that many dead. Peace is a debt the living pay on behalf of the dead, they say, and mean it as comfort. But peace built on a hidden Vault is not the same as peace built on an empty field. If the Guild ever proved the Vault real, and found it, the extraction that follows would not merely repeat the Culling. It would be worse, faster, and this time there would be nothing sealed away to survive it."

"So the Synod sits on the truth," Rook said, flat, arms crossed, the old fast rhythm of his voice gone hard and careful in a way Caelen recognized from every conversation Rook had ever had about authority that claimed to know better than the people it governed. "Calls it peace. Lets everybody go on believing the wound's closed, when really it's just — what, bandaged over something still bleeding underneath."

"Yes," Seraphina said, simply, and did not flinch from the bluntness of it. "And I would have gone on believing the bandage too, and been grateful for it, and married whoever my House selected to secure its next alliance, if I had not spent three years serving as junior archivist to the very reliquary that housed this shard, and read, in the process, what the Vault actually contains beyond a location." She stopped there, visibly, and Caelen understood, watching her, that whatever came next was a door she had decided, deliberately, not to open yet. "That part I'll tell you when I better understand which of you I'm trusting it to. For now, understand only this: my House learned three weeks ago that I had accessed the reliquary beyond my clearance. They do not yet know I took the shard itself rather than merely its record. When they learn that — and they will, soon, because a House does not send four retrieval men after a mere embarrassment — the Guild's own agents inside the Spire will hear of it within days, and then this stops being a Synod secret and a family's shame, and starts being every faction in this territory racing toward the same door at once."

The camp went quiet, the fire's low crackle the only sound against the Wastes' constant patient hiss of drifting ash.

"That's not a salvage job," Varrick said, finally, dry again, though the dryness this time carried none of its usual ease. "That's the kind of job that gets the word *legend* attached to whoever survives it, which in my considerable experience is a word reserved almost exclusively for the dead."

"I'm not asking you to carry it to the Vault itself," Seraphina said. "I don't yet know where the Vault is — the shard requires a proper reading, a ritual none of us here are equipped to perform, and I have neither the training nor, I suspect, the raw capacity that such a reading would demand of an ordinary Resonant." Her eyes went, deliberate, to Caelen, and he understood, with a lurch that felt less like surprise and more like a door he'd always half-expected finding him first. "I am asking only that you help me reach somewhere safe enough to read it properly, before my House's retrieval or the Guild's own reach catches up to us both. After that, what any of us choose to do with what the reading tells us — that decision belongs to all of us equally, and I will not pretend otherwise to secure your help now."

Caelen looked at the shard a long moment, its slow pulse answering something in his own chest that he did not yet have full words for — the same low ache that had lived in him since the amber first split, the same certainty about buried shapes that had once carried him blind through a flooding gallery, arriving now, after seven long careful years of holding himself apart from exactly this kind of pull, with the force of something that had simply been waiting, patiently, the whole time, for him to come close enough to answer it.

"It knows me," he said, quiet, and did not fully understand the words even as he said them, only that they were true. "Or I know it. I don't have the word yet for which."

Seraphina did not look surprised. She looked, instead, like someone whose private theory had just been confirmed at considerably higher stakes than she'd hoped for.

"There is a passage in the reliquary's own commentary," she said, carefully, "concerning what the Synod calls *the grown, not the touched* — children born, if that's even the right word for it, directly from strata pockets rather than shaped afterward by conduit and core. The commentary treats it as myth. A cautionary doctrine, meant to frighten Guild crews into abandoning deep digs rather than a real accounting of anything that's ever actually happened." She looked at him very directly then, and there was no performance left anywhere in her voice at all. "I did not expect, when I ran into the dark from my own House's men, to find the myth sitting at a stranger's fire, offering me bread."

"Well," Varrick said, into the long silence that followed, dry as ever, though something in his eyes, resting on Caelen, had gone entirely serious. "Seems the job's found us regardless of what any of us decide to call it." He rose, slow, joints cracking audibly in the cold morning air, and began, without further ceremony, to break camp. "Rook. Pack the gear we can carry fast and leave the rest for whoever finds it. Girl — Seraphina — you'll ride the second cart and you'll tell us, as we walk, every last piece of this you've been holding back, because I did not survive this long by walking blind into anything, legendary or otherwise." He looked, last, at Caelen, and for once did not reach for a joke to cover what he meant to say plainly instead. "And you. However this shard answers whatever's sitting in you — we find out together, same as we've found out everything else. That much hasn't changed in nine years, and I don't intend to let it start changing now."

Caelen wrapped the shard back in its oilcloth himself, careful, the slow warm pulse of it settling against his palm like something recognizing an old debt finally being called due, and helped break camp beside the only family he had left standing, toward a destination none of them yet had the full shape of, and all of them, in their own particular ways, had already decided to walk toward regardless.
