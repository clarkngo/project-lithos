# Chapter Seven — Seraphina's Price

The watch fell to the two of them on the second of Malakar's borrowed four days, an arrangement Caelen understood, watching Varrick arrange it with a dry offhandedness that fooled no one, had been engineered rather than fallen into by chance.

Seraphina sat with the shard's oilcloth bundle set, for once, a careful arm's length from her rather than clutched against her chest, and Caelen understood that small distance as its own kind of statement — a woman deciding, deliberately, not to let the thing she carried sit between herself and the conversation Varrick had clearly maneuvered her into.

"You've been watching me watch it," she said, without preamble, not looking up from the low fire. "The shard. Since the Sinking Fields. I assumed, initially, it was simple caution. I no longer assume that."

"It answers something in me I don't have full words for," Caelen said, honest, because he had learned, across nine years, that honesty cost him less in the long run than the careful evasions Varrick still practiced out of older habit. "I watch it because some part of me is afraid of how much I want to watch it."

Seraphina was quiet a long while, the fire's low crackle the only sound against the Wastes' patient hiss of drifting ash, and when she spoke again, the careful architecture of her voice had already begun, Caelen noticed, to loosen at its own edges — not collapsing outright, but easing, the way a held breath eases before it's fully let go.

"I had a brother," she said. "Tomas. Two years younger, which in a House like mine meant considerably lower in the succession, which meant, in turn, that he was afforded a freedom the rest of us were never permitted — free to travel, to study what interested him rather than what secured alliances, free, eventually, to fall in with the sort of company a Spire heir is meant to be protected from ever meeting." She looked at the shard, briefly, then away. "He came home, three years ago, wearing a conduit he'd acquired unlicensed from exactly the sort of trade your Varrick still runs. He had been Shifting for months before any of us knew. He was, I am told, remarkably gifted at it — a Talon Vector, an Ember Cadence, a combination that should have required years of careful training to hold safely even once. He held it dozens of times in a single season, chasing whatever feeling it gave him that the rest of his caged life never could."

"Drift," Caelen said, quiet, already understanding the shape the story was walking toward.

"Drift," Seraphina agreed. "Though my House did not use that word in front of me, not then. They called it a *decline*. A *private matter requiring discretion*. I was seventeen, and I was told my brother had taken ill, and I was not permitted to see him during what they called his convalescence, and when I finally forced my way past the physicians my father had hired specifically to keep the family from asking the questions I was asking—" She stopped there, and Caelen watched the last of her formal architecture come apart entirely, the contractions arriving now without any performance left to hold them back. "He didn't know me. That's the plainest way I can say it. He looked at me the way you'd look at furniture, or weather, something present but not worth the cost of recognizing, and three days later my House quietly arranged for him to be 'returned to the mountains for treatment,' which is the phrase we use, in Spire circles, for a thing none of us have ever once had the courage to name plainly."

"He didn't come back," Caelen said. Not a question.

"He didn't come back." Her voice, saying it, had gone very steady in the particular way Caelen recognized from his own worst nights — grief that had been carried so long it had stopped costing tears and started costing, instead, a terrible flat clarity. "I spent two years afterward believing there was simply nothing to be done, that Drift was exactly the inevitable, unstoppable tragedy the Synod's doctrine claims it to be, a wound with no possible treatment beyond quiet, discreet disposal dressed up as mercy. And then I was assigned, as part of my archival duties, to catalogue a section of the reliquary's older records — Lithoi-era fragments, mostly untranslated, mostly dismissed by the senior Custodians as too degraded to be worth the effort of full study. I found, in three separate fragments, references to something the old records call *the tethering rite.* A practice, apparently once common among the Lithoi themselves, for drawing a drifting mind back from the edge of losing itself entirely. Not a cure. The fragments are careful never to claim that much. But a treatment. A real one, with a real methodology, sealed away in the same reliquary that holds the Vault's location, in the same collection the Synod has spent generations insisting contains nothing but administrative record and unrecoverable ruin."

Caelen sat with that a long moment, the shard's slow pulse against the near dark answering something in his own chest that he understood, now, with considerably more weight than he had an hour before.

"You think the tethering rite is real," he said. "You think it's in the Vault, or connected to whatever's in the Vault. And you think it might work on someone like me."

"I think," Seraphina said, careful now, the full weight of the confession finally arriving at its actual point, "that I have spent three years telling myself, and everyone above me, that I acted to expose the Synod's hypocrisy, to prevent them concealing a truth that belongs to every family who has ever lost someone to Drift and been told there was nothing to be done. That is true. It is also not the whole truth, and I promised myself, coming out here, that I would not let you walk toward the Vault beside me without knowing the rest of it. I want the tethering rite for Tomas's sake, even though Tomas is three years dead and past any rite's reaching. I have wanted it for my own sake, some cowardly nights, wondering whether it might have saved him if I'd found it sooner. And since watching you nearly lose yourself entirely in the Sinking Fields, I have wanted it, considerably more urgently than either of those, for yours."

The admission sat between them a long while, honest and unarmored in a way Caelen understood, from his own hard-won experience, had cost her considerably more than the shard's theft ever had.

"That's not a small thing to want," he said, finally, careful. "Wanting something for yourself, in the middle of all this. Varrick once told me — a long time ago, before either of us fully understood what I was — that wanting things was useful. Proof there was still something in a person worth keeping fed." He looked at her directly. "I don't think you need to apologize for it."

"Don't you?" Something raw crossed her face, quick, before she mastered it back down to something steadier. "I have pulled all four of you into a hunt that could unravel three generations of careful, terrible peace, and part of my reason for doing it is a grief I have never once managed to set down. If the Vault holds what the fragments suggest it might, the Guild will not stop at recovering a location — they will use whatever's real inside it to justify extraction on a scale the Culling itself never reached, because a Synod-sealed truth, once broken open, does not stay contained to the single kind use I want it for. I know that. I have known it since the reliquary. And I took the shard anyway."

"So did I, in a way," Caelen said, quiet. "Every time I let myself Shift instead of finding some other way through. Every time I spend what I am instead of what I have. I don't think wanting something selfish and wanting something true are as separate as the Synod's doctrine likes to pretend they are. Ansel didn't die for a cause. He died because he wanted, plainly and entirely, for Rook to keep having a family. That's about as selfish a want as exists, and it's also the truest thing anyone's ever spent themselves on for my sake." He held her gaze, steady, the fire low and warm between them. "Whatever's in the Vault, we'll carry the weight of finding it together, same as everything else. That includes the parts of the weight that are just yours, and just mine, and not the whole territory's business at all."

Seraphina was quiet a long moment, and when she finally spoke again, her voice had settled into something Caelen had not yet heard from her — not the careful court formality, not the raw unguarded grief either, but a plain, tired honesty resting somewhere between the two.

"Thank you," she said, simply, no architecture left in it at all to soften or perform. "I don't think I've said that to anyone, properly, since Tomas."

They sat together the rest of the watch in a silence that carried none of the wariness of their first night at the fire, the shard's slow pulse a steady presence between them, and Caelen understood, watching the false dawn begin to grey the eastern edge of the Wastes, that whatever waited for them at the Vault's actual door, he would not be walking toward it as a stranger carrying someone else's cargo, but as one of two people who had each, in their own particular and costly ways, already decided exactly what they were willing to spend to find out.
