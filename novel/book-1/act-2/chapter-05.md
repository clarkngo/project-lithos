# Chapter Five — The Toll of the Wastes

The ground had a memory too, Caelen had learned over nine years, and the memory the Sinking Fields kept was of fire — old extraction burns gone so deep and so hot that the strata beneath had hollowed itself into a honeycomb no survey had ever fully mapped, ready, in places, to give way under nothing heavier than its own settling.

It gave way under the weight of four people and a laden cart considerably sooner than any of them wanted.

The first collapse took the ground from beneath the cart's rear wheel without warning, a whole stretch of crust dropping a body's length into darkness that breathed up cold and mineral-sharp, and it was only Rook's fast grab at the lead rope that kept the whole cart, and the supplies that meant the difference between crossing and starving partway through it, from going down after.

"Behind us," Varrick called, low and urgent, and Caelen didn't need to turn to know what he meant — the same disciplined, torch-shielded jog from the extraction rig, closer now than any of them had hoped, three days' hard travel having bought them considerably less lead than they'd counted on.

"We can't outrun them across ground that keeps opening under our own feet," Seraphina said, already bracing the cart's near wheel with her shoulder, the shard's oilcloth bundle riding, as it had for three days now, close against her chest where she refused to let it out of arm's reach.

Caelen felt it before she'd finished the sentence — the shard's slow pulse, closer now than it had been at any point since the fire where she'd first unwrapped it, answering something in his own chest with a clarity that had sharpened, day over day, the nearer they'd traveled to whatever pulled at both of them from some shared, buried source. It was not comfortable. It had not been comfortable for three days. But it had, until this crossing, stayed manageable — a background ache, easily banked beneath the discipline of seven careful years.

The next stretch of ground took that discipline and spent it faster than he had any say in.

He hauled the cart's sunk wheel clear with a half-Shift, controlled, precise, exactly as much as the task required — and the ground gave way again not twenty strides further, this time beneath Rook's own feet, a whole section of crust folding downward into a black mouth wide enough to take him whole, and Caelen was moving before thought finished forming, the coal behind his sternum lighting hard and fast because there was no measured half-reach available to a boy — no, a man, nine years removed from the boy he'd been, and it still did not stop his mind reaching for the old word — falling into ground that did not forgive falling.

He caught Rook's wrist a hand's width from the drop's ragged edge and hauled him back bodily, and the cost of that reach arrived immediate and total, no careful rationing left in it at all, his skin drawing tight at the shoulders with a violence he hadn't spent on a single Shift in years, his fingers lengthening past anything the moment strictly required because fear, old and absolute, had made the decision for him before discipline ever got a vote.

He had no time to let it bank back down before the ground opened a third time.

---

By the fourth Shift, called up faster than the last had guttered out, something in Caelen's chest began to feel less like a coal being lit and more like a door being held open against a wind that had stopped asking permission to blow through it.

He was dimly aware of the shape his body had taken — larger now than any measured half-Shift, larger than he'd let himself become since the weigh-station, his voice, when some part of him still trying to shout warnings across the collapsing ground came out of him, arriving low and wet and wrong the same way it had the night a man died at a weigh-station nine years gone. He hauled Varrick clear of a fourth collapse with a strength that no longer felt entirely borrowed, more like something native finally being permitted its full stride, and he understood, distant and sick, that the understanding itself was the danger — that the enormous patient hunger beneath his discipline had stopped waiting to be invited and started, instead, simply attending.

The shard's pulse, close against Seraphina's chest a few strides off, had stopped feeling like an ache and started feeling like a voice — wordless, ancient, calling something in him toward a shape that had nothing left of *Caelen* in it at all, only the vast buried certainty of stone that remembered being alive before it remembered being mined, and Caelen felt the small warm stone of his own name, carried steady for nine years now, thin suddenly to something he could barely close his hand around.

*Caelen.* He reached for it, and found less of it waiting than he had ever found before, even at the weigh-station, even in the worst of the drowning undertow that had first taught him the word for what he was fighting. *Caelen. Not this. Not all the way.*

The old anchor did not hold. Not fully. He felt himself thinning the way smoke thins into open air, his own thoughts arriving distant and strange, some vast patient hunger beginning, very quietly, to like the thinning the same way it always had — and it was not Varrick's voice that found him this time, because Varrick was down on one knee in the ash-drift, spent past reaching, and it was not the small stone of his own name either, because the stone had gone thin as paper and offered nothing further to hold onto.

It was Rook.

"CAELEN." Not a warning this time. Not fear either, though fear lived in it, close under the surface the way it always had — this time something else rode over the top of the fear, deliberate, chosen, the same choosing Caelen himself had spent nine years learning to make. Rook did not run. Caelen understood that, dimly, through the thinning — Rook's crooked arm, his ordinary human body, standing close in front of something that had, an hour ago, been a man he called family and had become, in the space of four consecutive Shifts, something the old fearful part of Rook had every reason left in the world to flee from.

He did not flee.

"You said you'd know it when it comes," Rook said, closer now, his voice cracking with the effort of keeping it steady, "the price, the cost, whatever debt this whole cursed thing's been running since the mountain — well, I'm telling you plain, right now, it doesn't get to come due like this. Not today. You hear me? Bread and a smoking fire and a boy who didn't ask you a single question you weren't ready to answer — that's what you're spending down to nothing right now, and I will not stand here and watch you trade it for a stone's memory that was never yours to carry in the first place."

Something in the vast patient hunger hesitated.

"Ansel didn't die so you could go feed yourself to a hole in the ground," Rook said, and his voice broke fully there, the fast rough cadence Caelen had loved since a smoking fire in Hollow Cut cracking wide open into something with no bravado left in it at all, nothing but plain, unguarded, terrified love. "He spent himself on you on purpose, Caelen, deliberate, same as you're doing right now except he meant it to buy you *more* life, not less. Don't you dare make his dying the down payment on you losing yourself anyway. Don't you *dare.*"

The word landed somewhere the shard's ancient pulse could not follow. Caelen felt it — not as the old small stone, thin and nearly spent, but as something new entirely, a second anchor thrown alongside the first, close enough now that his own hand, lengthened and wrong and still somehow his, found Rook's shoulder instead of Rook's throat, and held there, shaking, instead of closing.

The heat guttered out all at once, the way it always did, a door thrown open onto winter — except this time the winter did not merely arrive. It arrived total, and Caelen went down into the ash-drift bonelessly, every ounce of nine years' careful discipline spent in a single afternoon, his body smaller again, wrong in every direction at once, shaking so hard that Rook's arms around him, hauling him back from the collapse's ragged edge, could barely hold him steady.

---

He did not fully surface again until well after dark, wrapped in every scrap of cloth the crew had between them, a fire built small and shielded against whatever pursuit the Sinking Fields' collapsing ground had, mercifully, finally discouraged. Varrick sat close, grey-faced with an exhaustion that had nothing to do with his own aging knees. Seraphina sat apart, the shard rewrapped and pushed, deliberately, to the farthest edge of the cart she could manage without abandoning it outright, her face carrying an expression Caelen was too spent to fully read but understood, on some level, as guilt.

Rook had not moved from beside him the whole while.

"That's twice now," Caelen managed, his voice wrecked, hollow, costing him more than it should to make. "Twice you've talked me back from somewhere I couldn't find my own way out of alone."

"Once," Rook said, quiet, correcting him with a gentleness that had nothing to do with the correction's actual content. "First time, at the shelter with the waterskin, that wasn't me talking you back from anything. That was me being a scared kid who ran. This—" he gestured, small, at the two of them, at the whole wrecked shivering shape of the night, "—this is the first time I actually did the thing I told you, back at that fire, I didn't know if I could do. I said I didn't know how long it'd take before there was only the one part of me left — the part that wants to sit close instead of the part that's afraid." He was quiet a moment, and when he spoke again his voice had found, underneath the exhaustion, something steadier than Caelen had ever heard in it before. "Turns out the answer was nine years. Turns out I already knew which part had won, I just hadn't been handed the chance yet to prove it."

Caelen closed his hand, slow, around the small warm stone of his own name, and found it there still, worn thin from the day's spending but present, real, and understood, for the first time since the mountain had first taught him how much a self could cost to hold onto, that he was no longer the only one holding it.

"Thank you," he said, and meant it in a way that had no room left in it for anything sideways or armored at all.

Rook did not answer with a joke. He only nodded, once, and settled in closer to the small shielded fire, and none of them said anything further about the Vault, or the shard, or the pursuit still somewhere behind them in the dark, because for tonight, at least, all any of them had the strength left to hold was each other.
