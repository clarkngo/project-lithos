# Chapter Twelve — The Glass Wastes, After

They walked three weeks before Varrick's ribs allowed anything faster than a careful, deliberate pace, and Caelen found, matching his own stride to the old man's slower one, that he did not mind the slowness at all. There had been considerably too much of his life spent running toward things, or away from them, to begrudge a season spent simply, stubbornly walking.

The scar the binding apparatus had left along his forearm never fully faded. It sat pale and puckered beneath the dulled brass of the conduit he still wore, empty, the same careful lie it had always been, though he had stopped, somewhere in the walking, thinking of it as only a lie. It was armor too, now, in its own quiet way — a mark that let strangers see a licensed man where an unlicensed danger actually stood, the same sideways protection Varrick had once built for him out of acid-washed brass and dry, unshowy care.

They came back, in the end, to the same broken ridge above the Glass Wastes where Caelen had once stood as a boy leaner and harder and more marked than the child who'd fallen from a mountain nine years before that — the same threshold, the same scarred silver country stretching out below it, wound in the land that had never fully closed and never fully would.

He stood at the ridge's edge now with Rook close on one side, Varrick easing himself down onto a flat stone on the other, wincing but present, and understood, looking out over ground he had once crossed as a frightened, hollow boy, that the country below him had not changed at all in the time he'd spent becoming someone able to survive crossing it. It was still ugly. Still poisoned in patches. Still the one stretch of unowned ground that nobody wanted badly enough to watch too closely, and he understood, standing there, that this was not a flaw in the landscape to be mourned, but the whole reason it had ever been able to hold a found family together in the first place.

"Guild's not done," Varrick said, into the quiet, his dry voice carrying, even now, even wounded, its old unshowy certainty. "Director like that doesn't withdraw and call it finished. She'll spend the next year finding some other angle into that mountain, and the year after that if the first one fails her. Some debts don't close just because the ledger's owner walked away from the table once."

"And the Synod's still deciding what to make of me," Caelen said, quiet, watching the horizon. "The Custodian as good as said it plain. Nine years of doctrine just proved wrong doesn't rewrite itself overnight, however honestly she meant the saying of it."

"Malakar's not going back to Kesh Hollow either," Rook added, glancing back toward where the Inquisitor-Commander sat a short distance off, still uncertain, three weeks on, of exactly where he belonged among them, but present at every fire regardless, his precise clause-stacked cadence slowly, awkwardly loosening into something closer to plain conversation the longer he stayed. "Man burned every bridge he had back to that life in about the space of an afternoon. Doesn't strike me as the sort of debt that settles itself quietly either."

"None of it's settled," Caelen agreed. "Not the Guild, not the Synod, not whatever's still sealed inside that mountain waiting for somebody else, someday, to find a way past what I built to keep it shut. Not even what I am, really — I still don't have the whole word for it, after everything. Grown, not touched. That's still the truest thing anyone's ever managed to say about it, and it still isn't the whole answer."

He felt, saying it, the small warm stone of his own name settle steady in his chest, exactly as heavy as it had been the night he'd chosen the small shape of his life over the chamber's vast lonely completeness, exactly as entirely his own — and understood, for the first time since the mountain first split open beneath him, that the weight of it no longer frightened him the way an unanswered question once would have. Some things, he had learned across nine hard years of learning it, were not meant to resolve. They were only meant to be carried, honestly, by people who had chosen, deliberately, again and again, to keep carrying them together.

Seraphina came to stand at his other side, the tethering rite's fragile fragment folded now into a leather satchel she never let far from reach, her own particular unresolved debt carried forward into whatever came next. "I mean to find others," she said, quiet, watching the same scarred horizon. "Families like mine. People who were told there was nothing to be done, the same lie my House told me about Tomas. The rite deserves better than sitting sealed in one more private reliquary, mine this time instead of the Synod's. I don't yet know how far that finding will carry me, or what it will cost, but I intend to spend myself on it regardless."

"Then you'll want company that's crossed worse ground than most," Varrick said, dry, wincing as he shifted his weight on the flat stone. "Provided my ribs are willing to keep pace with wherever you're headed, which I'll grant is presently an open question."

Rook laughed, short and real, the old fast rhythm fully returned to him now, worn into something steadier than it had ever carried in Hollow Cut's crooked lane. "Found family reforming again," he said, echoing his own words from a fire three weeks gone. "Guess that's just the shape of it now. Every hard thing that happens, we come out the other side with one more person standing at the fire than we started with."

Caelen looked out over the Glass Wastes one last time — the rusted rig-bones, the fused glass-dark plate, the patient hiss of ash still drifting over ground that had been taken apart the same thorough way his own body had been taken apart and put back together, wrong and then wrong again and then, finally, entirely, deliberately his own. He thought of a treeline nine years gone, of a rain that had struck him like a blow before he understood it for what it actually was, of a boy who had walked into unfamiliar trees because the trees were the only direction left that wasn't stone.

He was not that boy anymore, and had not been for a long while. But something in him, standing at this same threshold with the whole hard-won weight of everyone still gathered around him, understood that the boy's choosing — to walk forward into ground that offered no promise of safety, only the bare possibility of surviving it on his own honest terms — had never actually stopped being the choice he made. Not at the shelter. Not at the weigh-station. Not in the chamber's vast lonely offering, nor at the mountain's resealed door.

Not now, either, looking out at whatever the Guild and the Synod and the sealed truth of an entire vanished people still had waiting for all of them, somewhere past the horizon's flat silver line.

"All right," he said, and felt Rook's shoulder settle against his own, and Varrick's dry, steady presence at his back, and Seraphina's quiet resolve beside him, and Malakar's uncertain, hard-won loyalty a few careful steps behind, the whole unresolved, unfinished shape of the only family the mountain had ever, in the end, actually managed to give him. "Then let's go find out what it costs."

He stepped down off the ridge, into the long grey hush of the Wastes, and did not look back to see whether they followed, because he had long since stopped needing to check.
