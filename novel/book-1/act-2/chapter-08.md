# Chapter Eight — The Guild Moves

Malakar's four days had worn down to one and a half by the time Seraphina finally found ground she judged suitable for the reading, and even then she judged it suitable only because there was no longer any honest argument left for waiting on something better.

"An old resonance well," she said, standing at the lip of a shallow stone bowl sunk into the Wastes' scarred crust, its walls fused glass-dark by heat older than the extraction fires that had ruined everything around it. "Pre-Culling. The reliquary's older fragments mention them scattered through this whole territory — places where the Lithoi themselves once gathered to work rites too large for a single conduit to carry alone. If the shard is going to answer properly to anyone, it will answer here."

"Answer to anyone," Varrick repeated, dry, though his eyes had already gone to Caelen with a wariness none of the last nine years' careful discipline had fully talked him out of. "You said *anyone* like the word was doing more work than it usually does."

"It is." Seraphina did not soften the admission. "The fragments are plain that an ordinary Resonant attempting a reading of this scale would spend past anything a licensed core and housing could recover from. I do not know, precisely, what it will cost someone who was never touched by Resonance in the ordinary sense at all. I only know that if anyone standing on this ground has a chance of surviving the asking, it is Caelen, and I would not have brought us this far to pretend otherwise to spare any of us the discomfort of saying it plainly."

Caelen looked down into the fused stone bowl, and felt, even from its edge, the same slow answering pulse that had lived behind his sternum since the shard first came into the firelight — stronger here, though, considerably stronger, the way a sound grows louder the nearer you walk toward its source.

"Then I'm the one standing in it," he said, and did not let himself weigh the decision any longer than that, because he had learned, across nine years, that some costs only grew heavier the longer a person stood outside them deciding whether to pay.

---

The reading did not feel like a Shift. That was the first thing Caelen understood, descending into the bowl with the shard unwrapped and warm in his bare palm, Seraphina's careful murmured guidance — half-remembered fragments of a rite none of them fully understood — settling over him like a second, colder amber. It felt, instead, like the mine. Like the pulse he had lost the morning the blast split the dark apart, arriving now not as memory but as presence, vast and patient and entirely uninterested in whether a nine-year-old boy, or the man he'd since become, was ready to receive it.

He saw, or understood, or simply *knew*, in the wordless total way his body had always known the shape of buried stone: a location, first, sharp and immediate, a seam in the old range north of the Wastes where the Lithoi themselves had sealed a chamber deeper and older than any strata the Guild had ever charted. And then, beneath the location, the rest of it arrived, and it arrived like a weight dropped without warning onto a body already spent.

Not myth. Not administrative record gone to ruin, the way three generations of Synod doctrine had insisted. A true accounting — cores beyond counting, marrow gathered and vitrified and sealed by Lithoi hands in the last desperate years before the Culling finished what it started, a reserve so vast that the whole present economy of licensed Resonance, the whole careful three-generation trickle of Guild extraction that fed every conduit and every housing from here to the Spire, would look, against it, like a single coal held up beside a forge fire banked for a century.

And beneath that, deeper still, something else — fragments of the tethering rite Seraphina had found in the reliquary, but whole here, entire, a methodology laid down by the same hands that had sealed the chamber, meant for exactly the wound Tomas had died of, exactly the wound Caelen carried banked behind his own sternum every hour of every day.

He came back to himself on his knees at the bowl's center, the shard's warmth gone entirely cold in his hand, his whole body shaking with a hypothermic violence that dwarfed even the Sinking Fields, and understood, before he'd fully caught his own breath back, that Rook's arms were already around him, and that he had been unconscious, by Varrick's grim accounting, for the better part of an hour.

"Say something," Rook said, low, urgent. "Anything. Your name. I don't care which of the old anchors it is, just give me something to know you're still in there."

"Caelen," he managed, wrecked, and felt Rook's whole body ease at the sound of it. "I'm here. I saw it. I saw the whole of it."

---

It was Malakar who broke the silence that followed his account of it, once Caelen had steadied enough to speak plainly, his precise clause-stacked cadence returning, but pointed now at something considerably more urgent than doctrine.

"An untapped reserve of that scale," he said, low, "is not a secret that survives contact with the Guild's own ledger for very long, once anyone with proper authority begins asking the right questions. I need all of you to understand the shape of what that means. The Guild has spent three generations mining strata that grow thinner and more contested every year. A reserve like the one you're describing would not merely restart extraction. It would justify, in the Guild's own accounting, a scale of operation that makes the Culling look, by comparison, like an opening bid."

"Which is exactly why the Synod's sealed it," Seraphina said, quiet. "Not only for the tethering rite, or whatever else the old fragments hold. For this. A reserve nobody sanctioned, nobody's ledger accounts for, sitting unclaimed and unwatched in ground neither the Guild nor the Wardens has ever fully mapped."

"Then we should assume," Malakar said, "that the Guild has spent considerably more effort than any of us credited them for trying to find it independently, reliquary theft or no. Which means—" He stopped there, listening, his whole body going suddenly, professionally still, the same stillness Caelen remembered from a burning lane nine years gone.

Caelen heard it a beat later, the sound carrying strange and flat across the Wastes' scarred silence — not boots this time, not dogs, but the low grinding rumble of heavy machinery, distant still, but moving with a scale and a purpose that no salvage cart or single survey crew could account for.

They climbed the bowl's rim together, and looked north, toward the old range Caelen's reading had just marked as the Vault's true door, and found the horizon there thick with dust and torchlight both, a column of Guild extraction machinery and armed escort stretching further than Caelen's sharp eyes could fully measure in the failing light — survey rigs, drilling platforms broken down for transport, and at the column's head, unmistakable even at this distance, the particular disciplined march of men who had not been sent merely to investigate a rumor.

"They're not scouting," Varrick said, flat, all the dry humor gone out of him entirely. "That's a mobilization. That's a Guild expedition that's been staged and ready to move for considerably longer than a stolen shard's had time to explain."

"Which means," Malakar said, grim, "either the Guild has independently located the Vault through channels none of us are aware of, or—" He stopped, and Caelen watched the stalled clause this time resolve fast, ugly, into a conclusion none of them wanted spoken aloud and all of them understood regardless. "Or my own order's careful, thin-worded silence has already been breached somewhere above my desk, and the Guild learned what the Synod's been sitting on considerably faster than any of us hoped they would."

Seraphina's hand had gone, without her seeming to notice it, to the shard at her chest, the last of her careful composure finally, entirely stripped away by the sight of that distant column.

"Then it isn't a race toward an answer anymore," she said, quiet, watching the dust and torchlight crawl steadily north toward a door that, an hour ago, only the five of them and a dead civilization had known the location of. "It's a race toward whoever reaches that door first deciding, for everyone, what the answer is even permitted to mean."

Caelen looked out at the column a long moment, the shard's cold weight still ghosted in his palm, the tethering rite's fragmented shape still settling somewhere behind his ribs alongside every other buried thing the reading had given him, and understood that whatever quiet, careful arithmetic had governed the last nine years of his life — the measured meals, the rationed Shifts, the discipline built stone by patient stone since a forge-keeper's death had taught him what a self actually cost to keep — was about to be spent, all at once, on ground that had been waiting, he understood now, considerably longer than any of them for someone to finally come and answer it.

"Then we move faster than they do," he said, and started down the bowl's far slope without waiting to see who followed, because for the first time since the mountain first split open beneath him, he understood exactly what he was walking toward, and exactly what it would cost him to arrive there first.
