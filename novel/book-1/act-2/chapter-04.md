# Chapter Four — Malakar, Still Hunting

*A note on point of view: as in Act One, Chapter Eight, this chapter departs deliberately from Caelen's perspective. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**MALAKAR**

Nine years had given Inquisitor-Commander Malakar a larger office, a heavier chain of rank across his shoulders, and precisely the same cold north wind finding its way in through shutters that no amount of seniority had ever persuaded the garrison's quartermaster to properly seal.

He had risen, in that time, exactly as far as precision could carry a man in the Warden order — which was, he had come to understand, considerably further than conviction ever could, and rather less far than the order's own recruiting broadsides implied. He commanded, now, the whole of the Resonance Enforcement desk for the eastern territories, three Inquisitors and a dozen line Wardens answering to his signature on their orders, and he had built that command the same way he had built everything else in his career: on files kept in exact, squared-off order, on force parameters documented before they were ever exercised, on the unbroken habit of never once letting doctrine and action drift far enough apart that a tribunal, someday, might find daylight between them.

He had believed, for most of those nine years, that the habit still amounted to a clean conscience.

The file on his desk this morning suggested otherwise, and had suggested it plainly enough that Malakar had read it three times before permitting himself to write a single word in response.

*Spire House Ilvane reports unauthorized removal of a sealed reliquary item by a junior archivist, since fled. Item's classification is Synod-restricted beyond standard Warden clearance. Warden involvement is authorized for recovery and CONTAINMENT ONLY. Investigation into the item's contents, provenance, or significance is expressly not authorized and will be treated, if pursued, as a breach of Synod confidence subject to tribunal review.*

He set the page down, precisely square with the desk's edge, and sat a long while with his hands folded in front of him, doing the particular clinical accounting he had built his entire professional self around: reading a sentence for what it actually required of him, rather than for what its authors hoped he'd assume it required.

*Investigation... is expressly not authorized.*

In eleven years before his promotion, and seven since, Malakar had never once received an order instructing him not to investigate something the order itself had flagged as dangerous enough to warrant a Commander's personal attention. Recovery orders came thick with detail — subject descriptions, last known movements, authorized force parameters down to the specific joint a restraint could be applied to without exceeding doctrine. This order came thin, deliberately thin, shaped with the particular precision of men who had learned, the same as he had, that the safest sentence was the one that left the fewest details behind for a tribunal to later hold against its author.

He was being asked, in language exact enough to survive any review, to recover an object and ask nothing at all about what it was.

---

The liaison arrived at midday, unannounced, the way Synod representatives always arrived — a formality Malakar had come to recognize, over the years, as its own kind of doctrine, a demonstration that a Synod Custodian's presence required no Warden courtesy to justify itself.

"Commander," the Custodian said, seating herself without invitation in the chair across his desk, her voice carrying the particular Synod cadence Malakar had encountered perhaps a dozen times in his career and never once grown fully accustomed to — soft, unhurried, every statement bent, almost imperceptibly, toward the shape of a question that did not actually require an answer. "You've read the file, I expect. Is there anything in it you find yourself uncertain about?"

"The scope of the containment order," Malakar said, precise, because precision was the only ground he had ever fully trusted beneath his own feet. "It instructs recovery without investigation. I am accustomed to orders that define a threat before they ask me to contain it."

"Is a threat only a threat once it has been fully defined?" the Custodian said, mild, and Malakar understood, hearing it, that this was the whole of the answer he was going to receive — not an answer at all, but a question shaped precisely enough to close the one he'd asked. "The item in question belongs to a category of Synod-held knowledge that has remained sealed for reasons predating your service, Commander, predating mine, predating the current configuration of every house and order presently arguing over its custody. I am not authorized to explain those reasons to you. I am authorized to tell you only that the Synod considers its continued sealing a matter of public peace, and that public peace, as I understand your own oath, falls rather squarely within your order's founding purpose."

"My oath," Malakar said, carefully, "is to enforce Warden doctrine evenly, and to document the reasoning behind any force I exercise, so that the reasoning may withstand review."

"Then document that you were instructed, by proper Synod authority, that the item's recovery serves public peace," the Custodian said, unbothered, rising. "That is reasoning, Commander. It withstands review considerably better than most reasoning I've encountered in your order's files, precisely because it asks nothing further of you than trust in an authority your own oath already recognizes."

She left without further pleasantry, the same as Orell had, years ago, in this same cold room, and Malakar sat a long while afterward with the stalled clause sitting heavy in his own throat — a sentence he had begun forming, silent, about the difference between *enforcing doctrine* and *enforcing an authority's preference for its own doctrine remaining unquestioned*, and a second sentence, older, more practiced, that moved to overwrite the first the way it always had.

This time, the overwriting did not fully take.

He noticed the failure with the same clinical attention he noticed everything, cataloguing it the way he might catalogue a new discrepancy in a witness's testimony: the sentence had not resolved. It sat there, unfinished, refusing the doctrinal ending that had always, reliably, closed every prior version of this same internal argument.

---

The second file crossed his desk three days later, and it was this one, not the Custodian's visit, that finally moved Malakar to act on the discrepancy rather than merely note it.

*Spire retrieval party, four men, reports engagement with fugitive archivist at an extraction rig south of the Ashfall stretch within the Glass Wastes. Fugitive was not traveling alone. Party's report describes third-party intervention consistent with unassisted Resonant transformation, subject young adult male, methodology matching unresolved Long Seam case file from nine years prior.*

Malakar read the paragraph twice, and felt something in his chest do a thing precision had no proper clause for — not quite recognition, not quite dread, some third thing that sat closer to relief than either, the relief of a stalled clause finally finding a sentence large enough to finish it.

The boy was alive. Had been alive the whole nine years, evidently, surviving in the one stretch of territory neither the Guild nor his own order had ever committed real numbers to searching, and had now, by whatever accident of circumstance, placed himself directly between a Synod-sealed reliquary item and the House trying to reclaim it.

He thought of the witness statement from a factor near Hollow Cut, nine years old now in his files but never fully filed away in the part of his mind that had once, briefly, let a sentence about a child's stolen nine years form all the way to completion before doctrine overwrote it. *Quiet. Watchful. Did not speak much, and when he did, spoke like someone relearning a language he'd once known and lost.*

He thought, too, of the burning lane at Hollow Cut, of standing motionless at the treeline while a boy — not yet a man, then, still mostly a child even in the shape of the enormous wrong-scaled thing his grief had made of him — tore through a cordon Malakar had personally ordered to hold non-lethal, and how he had, in that single unguarded instant, let the line scatter around him rather than command it to close.

He had filed that, too, at the time, as a tactical judgment. He understood now, nine years and one unfinished sentence later, that it had been something considerably less clean than tactics.

Malakar drew a fresh page toward him, and began, in his own exact and careful hand, to compose the orders that would send him back into the field for the first time in three years — not toward the Spire, where the Custodian's careful thin-worded authority expected him to look, but toward the Glass Wastes, toward a boy he had once, doctrine notwithstanding, chosen not to stop.

He did not yet know what he intended to do once he found him. He wrote the orders anyway, precise, square-edged, every clause defensible before any tribunal that might one day ask him to account for it — and understood, writing them, that for the first time in eleven years of service, he was building a paper trail not to protect his own conscience from the order's judgment, but to protect the order's judgment from ever fully understanding what his conscience had already, quietly, decided.
