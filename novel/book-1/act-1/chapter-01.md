# Chapter One — What the Deep Struck

The dark had a pulse, and Caelen had always lived inside it.

Not a heartbeat. Slower than that. A long, grinding throb that came up through whatever he was pressed into — warm, dense, the color of nothing, because there was no light down here to give it a color at all — and for as long as he could remember, which was not very long, the pulse had been the whole of the world. It came. It went. Somewhere above it, muffled the way a shout is muffled by a held hand, men's voices moved in metal rooms and metal boots struck metal floors, and none of it had ever reached him. He was folded in tight, knees to chest, and the warmth held him the way nothing else ever had, and he had no name for any of it because he had never needed one.

He had a name for himself, though. Caelen. He turned it over sometimes, the only thing that was purely his, a small stone worn smooth from handling.

The pulse missed a beat.

He felt it before he understood it — a hitch, a held breath, the amber-dark going taut around him like a drum skin about to split. Then the world above him screamed in a language made of iron and powder, and the drum split.

The blast came down through the strata in a single compressed shove, and the stone that had cradled him for nine years turned, in a tenth of a second, into a weapon.

Heat first. A wall of it, sudden and total, baking through the marrow-glass casing so fast that Caelen's skin remembered fire he had never touched. Then the sound arrived — not a boom, nothing so clean, but a tearing, a groan pulled up from underneath the world, rock shrieking against rock the way teeth shriek when they're ground in sleep, except this was the mountain's teeth, and the mountain did not sleep gently. The pulse that had been his whole life stuttered, raced, broke apart into a hundred smaller shudders running through the amber like cracks spidering across ice.

Caelen did not have the words *fracture* or *pressure differential* or *catastrophic strata failure.* He had only his body, curled tight in its warm dark cradle, and the sudden, animal certainty that the cradle was killing him.

He screamed. It came out of him before thought, a sound with no shape, and the amber around his mouth — thin there, thinner than anywhere else, worn by nine years of small movements he'd never known he was making — starred outward in a spiderweb of white fracture lines.

The second shockwave found that weakness and used it.

The world he had always lived in came apart in a single convulsive crack, and Caelen was born a second time, badly, into smoke.

---

Air hit him like a fist.

It was the wrongest thing he had ever felt — thin, and scalding cold where his lungs expected warm density, and full of grit that scraped the inside of his throat raw on the very first pull. He choked on his first breath of it, retched, breathed again because his body did not know how to stop, and every gasp brought more of the wrongness in: rock dust, and the acrid bite of blasting powder, and underneath both of those, something sweet and burnt that he would only later have a name for — scorched marrow-glass, the smell of his own cradle cooking in the heat that had killed it.

He could not see. His eyes streamed and stung and told him nothing but a red-orange smear, lantern light through smoke, and shapes moving in it that his mind could not yet sort into men.

He could hear, though, and hearing was worse.

Shouting, first — a ragged chorus of it, close and far at once, voices cracking high with something that was not the sound men make over ordinary damage. Somebody was praying. Somebody else was saying the same four words over and over, fast and flat, *not real not real not real,* and it took Caelen a long, swimming moment to understand — his mind thick and slow, unused to sound at this pitch, this volume, this violence — that the man was saying it about *him.*

He tried to uncurl. His body would not obey. Nine years folded into one shape had set him that way, and his knees screamed white-hot when he tried to straighten them, joints grinding like the mountain's own, tendons that had never once been asked to lengthen refusing the demand now. He fell instead of stood, out of the ruin of the amber and onto stone that had just, an instant before, been the bottom of the world he knew, and the impact knocked the last of his breath out of him in a wet, wordless bark.

Hands seized him. Rough, gloved, shaking — shaking harder than his own body was — and a voice above him, close enough now that its shape resolved out of the roar: a woman, or a man with a woman's pitch of terror, screaming for the foreman, screaming that there was *something in the seam,* that it was *alive,* that it had *eyes.*

Caelen understood, dimly, blinking smoke-tears down a face that had never before needed to make that particular motion, that the something was him.

The mountain did not give them time to decide what to do about it.

---

The second collapse came from below, not above — the deep strata folding in on the hollow the blast had punched through it, a chain of failures running up through the levels like a held breath finally let go all at once. The lantern light lurched sideways as the man holding him staggered, and Caelen, still held in arms that had gone rigid with panic, felt the floor drop a full foot beneath both of them before it caught.

It did not catch a second time.

The world tipped. Rock roared somewhere close enough to feel in his teeth, and the hands holding him opened — not thrown away, just opened, the reflex of a body given a choice between holding a child and holding a guide rope, and choosing, in that quarter-second, wrong for Caelen and right for itself. He fell into black water that had not existed nine years ago and now filled the lower gallery ankle-deep and rising, and the cold of it closed over his skin like a second, crueler amber, and this time nothing about it felt like a cradle at all.

He came up choking. Around him the shaft was a chaos of moving light — lanterns swinging on knocked-loose hooks, men wading and stumbling and shouting names that were not his — and Caelen, nine years old and newly born into a body two sizes too settled for the size of fear now flooding it, did the only thing left to any animal in that position.

He ran.

Or tried to. His legs did not run the way he somehow, in some buried and untouched part of him, knew legs were meant to run — they seized and dragged, unfamiliar with their own length, and twice he went down hard into the rising water and had to claw himself back up on hands that bled from stone he could not see to avoid. But some scrap of instinct in him that had nothing to do with the nine patient years in the dark took over where his unfamiliar limbs failed, and it was fast, and it was low, and it moved him up the flooding gallery on all fours faster than any of the grown men floundering upright around him.

He passed a man on his knees in the water, both hands pressed to a wound at his scalp, blood threading black through the flood in the lantern light. He passed a voice calling a name — *Dessa, Dessa, where's Dessa* — that broke apart into a sound with no words left in it at all. He did not stop for either. Some part of him, the part still curled tight around the small warm stone of his own name, understood without being told that stopping meant the water would keep rising and he would not.

The shaft narrowed. He knew this without knowing how he knew it — some sense not fully his own, a certainty about the shape of stone in the dark that felt less like memory and more like something laid into him at the marrow, the way the blood in a body knows, without being taught, which way is down. He turned where the turning was, into a service crawl too tight for the grown men behind him, and heard their shouting fall away, muffled, then gone, swallowed by rock the way his whole life had been swallowed by it an hour before.

The climb went on longer than his body had any right to survive. He would not remember most of it clearly — not then, and only in pieces later, the way a fever is remembered — but certain things stayed lodged in him like grit under a nail: the particular rasp of stone against his palms, worn raw and then numb and then, worse, not numb at all; the taste that filled his mouth, thick and coppery, that he did not yet understand was his own blood from a bitten tongue; the cold that came in waves, deeper each time, his body beginning — though he had no framework yet to call it this — to spend itself faster than it could afford, burning through whatever reserves nine curled-up years had banked in him, the first debt of many he would spend the rest of his life repaying.

He climbed until climbing became crawling, and crawling became a kind of blind, animal drag, elbow over elbow, toward a grey smear of not-black that his eyes, ruined for months afterward by real daylight, mistook at first for another kind of fire.

---

He came out of the mountain the way something is born: badly, too fast, into air that did not want him yet.

The service shaft ended in a drainage cut choked with bramble, and Caelen went through the last of it on momentum alone, tearing skin on thorns he could not see coming and would not feel until later, and then there was no more stone above him, no more ceiling of any kind, and the shock of that — the sheer unbounded *openness* of it — dropped him to his knees in mud as surely as a blow.

Rain was falling. He had no word yet for rain, only for the pulse and the dark and the small stone of his own name, but his skin knew it before his mind did, cold and relentless and utterly unlike the flood behind him, because this water fell from nowhere, from everywhere, out of a grey that went up and up and did not stop at any ceiling at all. He tipped his head back and it struck his face and he flinched from it like a blow before some deeper animal registered that it did not hurt, that this was only how the outside world touched things, all the time, always, and he had simply never been outside anything before.

The forest around him was a wall of green-black shapes, dripping, huge, sound coming off it in layers his ears could not yet sort — water on leaves, water on stone, some small thing moving fast and low through underbrush he would later learn to name, a wind moving through canopy far overhead with a hush that was almost, unbearably, like the pulse he had lost. He knelt in the wet bramble with blood cooling on his palms and mud soaking up through the knees of the thin shift the mountain had somehow left him in, and behind him, muffled now, distant now, the mine went on groaning as it settled into whatever new shape the blast had forced it to keep.

Smoke rose from the drainage cut he'd crawled out of, thin and grey, dissolving into the heavier grey of the rain before it rose very far.

Nobody came out after him.

He did not know, kneeling there, shaking with a cold that had nothing left to do with the water falling on him and everything to do with a debt his body had no way yet to name, whether that was because the mountain had closed the door behind him, or because everyone who might have followed was already part of what the mountain had kept.

He only knew that the dark had a pulse, and he had always lived inside it, and now the pulse was gone, and the rain did not stop, and somewhere behind the smoke and the settling stone was the only shape the world had ever had for him — and none of it, not one single part of it, was going to let him back in.

Caelen closed his hand into a fist in the mud, feeling for the small warm stone of his own name, the one thing the mountain had not managed to take, and got, slowly, unsteadily, onto legs that did not yet belong to him.

Then he walked into the trees, because the trees were the only direction left that wasn't stone.
