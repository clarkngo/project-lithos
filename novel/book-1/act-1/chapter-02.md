# Chapter Two — Green Dark

Hunger, Caelen learned, had a voice.

It did not speak in words. It spoke low in his gut and then higher, days in, in his chest and throat, a hollow ringing note that would not resolve, and by the third morning in the trees it had grown loud enough that he heard it over everything else the forest offered him — the drip-drip-drip off broad leaves long after the rain itself had stopped, the small violent rustlings of things that lived and hunted at a scale beneath his notice, the wind moving through the high canopy in that long unbearable hush that still, every time, made some buried part of him reach for a pulse that was no longer there to answer.

He had eaten nothing since the mountain. He did not know yet that a body could go this long and live; nine curled years in the warm dark had never once asked him to. Now it was asking constantly, and the asking had teeth.

He moved through the forest low, the way he had moved through the flooding gallery — not because he had decided to, but because his legs, still unfamiliar in their own length, found four points of contact steadier than two, and because the world at that height gave up more of itself. He could smell the difference between wet bark and wet stone before he saw either. He could hear a beetle's legs on leaf-litter from three strides off and did not yet understand that this was not a thing other boys could do. He simply did it, the way he breathed, and the doing of it was the only comfort the forest had offered him so far, because it meant some part of him still knew how to survive something, even if that something was no longer the dark.

His hands had stopped bleeding from the climb by the second day. That, too, he did not question. He turned his palms up sometimes in the grey daylight and studied the pink new skin knitted there, smooth and strange and a little too warm to the touch, the way a scar is warm before it fades, and felt no wonder at it at all — only the dull, exhausted logic of a child who has already used up his capacity for astonishment and has nothing left over to spend on his own body's small, wrong miracles.

He ate what the ringing note in his chest told him to eat, which was not always wise. Bitter shoots that left his tongue numb for an hour. A handful of black berries that came back up almost as fast as they went down, and he learned, retching into bracken with his whole small body shaking, that hunger and hunger's voice were not always giving him good counsel — only urgent counsel, which was a different thing, and a lesson that would serve him the rest of his life. He found a slow, cold stream and drank from it on his belly like an animal, and that, at least, quieted something.

He slept in the roots of a fallen tree the second night, folded into the hollow beneath it the way he had once been folded into the amber, knees to chest, and told himself in the dark, in a voice that did not feel entirely like his own thoughts, that this was not so different. It was a lie he needed and he let himself have it.

By the fourth day the cold had stopped being a thing that happened to him and started being a thing that lived in him, banked low behind his ribs no matter how the grey daylight warmed the canopy above. He noticed, distantly, that his wrists had gone thin — thinner than they'd been even at the mouth of the mine, the small stored softness of nine sedentary years burning away fast, faster than the little he'd managed to eat could replace. He did not have the word *debt* yet. He only had the feeling of it: a hollowness that ate inward from his skin, that made his own bones feel closer to the surface than bones should be.

He was sitting in the fork of a low tree on the fifth morning, still and grey-lipped, watching a spider rebuild a web the night's rain had torn apart thread by patient thread, when the man found him.

Caelen heard him a long time before he saw him — the particular unhurried crunch of a boot that knew exactly where it meant to land, nothing like the panicked wading of the men in the flood — and some new, unfamiliar instinct in him went very still and very quiet, the way it seemed to want him to go whenever the forest gave him something large and unknown to reckon with. He watched from the fork of the tree, breath shallow, as the man came into the clearing below.

He was not young. Grey threaded a beard that had been trimmed with more care than the rest of him suggested he usually bothered with, and he moved with the economical caution of someone counting his own steps out of long habit, eyes going to the ground, to the trees, to the ground again, in a rhythm that never quite settled. A pack rode heavy on his shoulders, straps worn pale with use. A short iron pry-bar hung from his belt beside something wrapped in oilcloth that Caelen's new, strange senses told him, without being asked, was warm — actively, faintly warm, the way stone should not be.

The man stopped at the base of Caelen's tree and did not look up right away. He crouched instead, studying the ground, and Caelen understood with a lurch that he was reading the mud — the small dragging prints, the broken bracken, the places where a child's knees had knelt to drink.

"You're bleeding on my census," the man said, without looking up, in a voice pitched low and dry, as if he were continuing a conversation they'd already been having for some time. "Guild counts every head that goes down that shaft. Doesn't count the ones that come back out a different shape. Going to be an interesting bit of arithmetic for somebody."

Caelen said nothing. He had not spoken aloud since the mountain; he was not certain, in that moment, that he still remembered how.

The man looked up.

Whatever he saw in the fork of that tree, he did not flinch from it, and that alone made him unlike every other grown voice Caelen had heard since the blast. His eyes moved over Caelen slowly — the too-thin wrists, the pink new skin on the palms, the eyes that Caelen did not yet know reflected lantern-light back at night the way a cat's did, the whole small starved wrong shape of him perched in the branches like something that had climbed up out of a story rather than out of a mine — and something in the man's face settled, rearranged itself around a decision, though his voice gave away nothing of the size of it.

"Strata child," he said, almost to himself. Not a question. He said it the way a man names a tool he's found more valuable than the job he brought it for. "Guild's been sitting on that collapse for five days like a hen on a cracked egg. Wouldn't say what they lost down there. Wouldn't say what they were looking for either." He tipped his head, considering. "You're a long way from either answer, and you look like you haven't eaten since before either question got asked."

Still Caelen said nothing. The ringing note in his chest had gone very loud, because the man was rummaging now, one-handed, in the pack at his hip, and had produced a strip of dried meat, tough and dark and smelling — even from the height of the tree, even over the wet green weight of the whole forest — like the single most important thing Caelen had ever encountered.

The man did not hold it up. He set it on a flat stone at the base of the tree and stepped back, unhurried, the way a person sets down bait for something they respect enough not to corner.

"Name's Varrick," he said. "I'm not Guild, not Warden, and I've got no particular interest in what you are, only in what you're worth, which I'll grant you is a conversation we can have once you've stopped looking like you'll fall out of that tree and break in half." He crouched again, a little apart from the stone, patient in the specific way of a man who has learned patience costs him nothing he isn't willing to spend. "Come down. Eat. I'm not fast enough to chase you and we both know it, so there's no trick in it. Just an old man's opinion that a thing worth finding is a thing worth keeping fed."

Caelen did not move for a long time. The rain had started again, soft, working its way down through the canopy in fat unhurried drops, and it struck his shoulders and the top of his head and he let it, unflinching now, the way he had not been able to help but flinch on the first day.

Then the hollow ringing note behind his ribs won the argument his fear had been losing since the fourth day, and he climbed down.

He did not go to the man. He went to the stone, quick and low on all fours the last stretch of it, and snatched the meat up and retreated three full strides before he tore into it, watching Varrick over every bite the way an animal watches a door it isn't sure is truly open.

Varrick did not move closer. He only watched back, arms loose at his sides, something unreadable working behind the dry economy of his face — not quite pity, not quite calculation, some third thing that did not yet have a name Caelen would learn for a long while.

"There it is," Varrick said, mostly to himself, mild as weather. "Something still in there that knows how to want things. Good. Wanting's useful. I can work with wanting."

He did not reach for Caelen. He straightened instead, slow, joints cracking audibly in the wet quiet, and turned his face up toward the canopy, judging the light the way a man judges a debt coming due.

"Half a day's walk to somewhere dry," he said. "You can follow or not. I'll tell you plainly I'd rather you did — a strata child's worth considerably more to me walking than worth carrying — but I've hauled heavier things out of worse woods than this, so don't mistake that for me asking twice."

He shouldered his pack and started walking, unhurried, without looking back to see whether he'd be followed, and it was that — the not-looking, the flat unsentimental certainty of it — that moved Caelen more than any kindness could have. Kindness, some new and old part of him understood without words, could be taken away. A man who simply expected to be followed, and walked as if the expectation cost him nothing, was harder to doubt.

Caelen swallowed the last of the meat, tasted salt and char and, beneath both, the first full meal his body had been given since it stopped belonging entirely to the dark, and got up on legs that were, step by uncertain step, beginning at last to feel like his own.

Then he followed the sound of the unhurried boots into the green.
