# Chapter Ten — The Wastes Ahead

They walked three days before anyone spoke more than was needed to choose a direction, and even then it was Varrick who broke it, because Varrick had always been the one willing to spend the first word on a silence too heavy for anyone else to lift.

"Glass Wastes," he said, on the third evening, while they sat around a fire too small to be seen from any distance and too small, too, to do much against the cold that had settled into all three of them somewhere back at the cistern and never fully left. "That's where we're bound. I should have said it plainer before now. You've earned knowing where your own feet are walking you."

Rook did not look up from the fire. He had not looked up from very much at all, these three days, his fast cadence gone somewhere none of them had found yet, his hands — the good one and the crooked one both — never quite still, always turning some small found object over and over, a stone, a scrap of brass, anything that gave his grief a shape to hold instead of nothing.

"Why there," Caelen asked, because someone had to, and his own voice, he noticed distantly, had settled into something flatter than it used to be, something that had stopped reaching for comfort in the asking.

"Because it's the one stretch of ground the Guild wants too badly to share with the Wardens, and the Wardens distrust too thoroughly to leave the Guild alone in." Varrick fed the fire a stick, watched it catch, slow and careful the way he did everything with his hands now, an old man's care that had nothing to do with injury and everything to do with knowing exactly how few sticks like it they had left to spend. "Whole territory's been mined out and fought over and mined out again so many times neither side can agree who holds proper claim to what's left. Which means neither side commits real numbers to patrolling it — too expensive, too contested, too likely to start a fight with their own supposed allies instead of whatever they're meant to be hunting. It's ugly ground. Poisoned in places. But ugly, contested ground is the safest kind of ground there is, for people like us, and I'd rather raise you both somewhere ugly and alive than somewhere pretty and found."

*Raise you both.* Caelen turned the phrase over the way he turned most things Varrick gave him now, and found, beneath the flat delivery, something that had never quite been said plain between them before — not since the shelter, not since *the terms of it*, not since a man who did not do charity had first decided a starving boy in a tree was worth more than the trouble of carrying him.

He did not say anything about it. Some things, he was learning, were better left exactly as sideways as the people who offered them meant them to stay.

---

He noticed the changes in his own body slowly, the way you notice a season turning — not in any single day, but in the sum of many small days laid end to end. His wrists, which had gone thin with starvation in that first week in the forest and then slowly filled back in under Varrick's careful measured feeding, had thinned again, differently this time, not with hunger but with use — a leanness that sat close to the bone the way a tool's handle sits close to a hand that's worn it smooth. His nails had grown back, after the weigh-station, faintly ridged at the base in a way that never fully evened out, a small permanent record of what they'd once split into. His jaw, when he ran his tongue along the inside of it in the quiet hours, carried a geometry that was not quite the one he remembered from before the mine — nothing visible to a stranger's eye, but felt, always, by his own tongue, a boy's jaw wearing the faint architecture of something that had once, twice, three times now, needed to be considerably more than a boy's.

He was not who the mountain had sealed away nine years ago. He understood that plainly now, walking, in a way he had only half understood at the treeline the morning after the blast. That child had died somewhere between the amber and the flood, or perhaps had never fully existed at all outside the warm patient dark that grew him — and what walked in his place now, lean and marked and careful with every choice it made about its own hunger, was someone else. Someone Varrick had fed into being with dried meat and dry humor and hard true lessons about the price of losing control. Someone Rook had fed into being with shared bread and a fast rough voice that had, even in its current silence, taught Caelen what it sounded like to be wanted for no reason beyond simply being present. Someone Ansel had fed into being, too, in three short days that had ended in fire — *you're a boy who came into my house hungry and left it fought for* — a verdict Caelen carried now the way he carried the small warm stone of his own name, worn heavier, worn permanent.

He did not know yet what to call the person all of that had made him. He only knew the child from the treeline was gone, in every sense but the memory, and that the going had cost exactly as much as everything else his life had asked of him so far.

---

They reached the edge of the Wastes on the ninth day, coming up over a long broken ridge that had once, Varrick said, been forested the same deep green as the country they'd been walking through for weeks, before three generations of deep-strata extraction had hollowed the ground beneath it until the whole hillside simply gave up holding its own shape.

Caelen had never seen anything like it. The land beyond the ridge did not roll the way land was supposed to roll. It sat scarred and glassy in the flat grey light, great cracked plates of ground fused amber-dark by old extraction fires, pale mineral dust lying in drifts where soil should have been, and here and there, distant, the skeletal iron remains of extraction rigs long abandoned, rusted into shapes that from this height looked less like machinery and more like the ribs of something enormous that had died standing up.

It was silent in a way the forest never had been. No birdsong carried up from it. No wind moved through canopy, because there was no canopy left to move through. Only a low constant hiss of dust shifting over glass-fused stone, patient, endless, the sound of a wound that had stopped bleeding a long time ago and simply never closed.

"Ugly," Rook said, the first full sentence he'd offered in three days, quiet, standing at the ridge's edge with his crooked arm cradled against his chest and his eyes fixed on the scarred horizon. He did not say anything else for a while. When he did, his voice had found, underneath the quiet, the faintest thread of its old fast rhythm again, worn thin but present, reaching for its own shape the way a fire reaches back up after being nearly smothered. "Ansel would've hated it out here. Man liked things that grew." He was quiet another moment. "Guess that's sort of the point, isn't it. Nobody wants it badly enough to watch it too close."

"That's the whole of the point," Varrick said, gently, for him.

Caelen stood between the two of them at the ridge's edge, looking out over ground that had been taken apart the same patient, thorough way his own body had been taken apart and put back together wrong three times now, and understood, with a clarity that felt less like a thought than like a fact his bones had already known for some while, that this was where whatever came next for him would have to happen. Not the mine. Not the forest that had first caught him falling. Not Hollow Cut, burned now behind them into something that would rebuild itself around an absence it could never quite fill back in.

Here. In the scarred, contested, unlovely ground that nobody wanted badly enough to watch too closely.

He thought, briefly, of the small stone of his own name, and found that he no longer needed to reach for it quite so hard to know it was still there. It had stopped being a thing he clutched against the dark and become, somewhere in the walking, simply a thing he carried — heavier now, permanently marked by everything the last year had cost to earn it, but solid, and entirely, unmistakably his own.

He stepped over the ridge's broken edge, down into the long grey hush of the Wastes, and did not look back to see whether Varrick and Rook followed, because for the first time since the mountain had first taken the ground out from under him, he understood, all the way down, that they would.
