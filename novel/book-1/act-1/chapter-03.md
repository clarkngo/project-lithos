# Chapter Three — The Terms of Taking In

Varrick did not talk on the walk, and Caelen was grateful for it in a way he had no words to offer back.

The half-day the man had promised turned out to be an honest measure, which Caelen would learn later was itself a kind of rarity in him — Varrick did not round his numbers up to sound generous or down to sound modest. He simply said the true count and let it be dull. They went along the shoulder of a ridge where the trees thinned and the rain came at them sideways instead of straight down, and then along a game track that dropped into a fold of the hillside so tight and green that Caelen would not have found it in a hundred years of looking, and the whole way Varrick walked a half-stride ahead and never once looked back to see if the distance between them had closed or opened.

It closed, by inches, all afternoon. Caelen noted this about himself the way he was starting to note most things about his own body now — not as anything decided so much as anything discovered, arrived at without his permission and reported to him after the fact.

The dry place turned out to be an old survey shelter, half sunk into the hillside and roofed with sheet-tin gone soft brown with rust, the kind of structure the Guild built fast and cheap for crews that were never meant to stay anywhere long. Varrick had clearly made it his own regardless — a stove pipe patched with wire, a groundsheet laid tight enough to keep the damp from climbing, two crates stacked and lashed into something like a table. It smelled of woodsmoke long banked, and oiled metal, and, faint beneath both, that same warm-wrong mineral note Caelen had caught off the bundle at his belt in the clearing.

"Sit," Varrick said, the first word he'd offered since the meat on the stone. He did not point to anywhere in particular. Caelen sat on the groundsheet near the cold stove, and Varrick crouched to coax a fire out of banked coals with the unhurried competence of a man who had done it ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more.

The fire caught. Warmth crawled out from it slow, and Caelen felt his whole body lean toward it without deciding to, the way a plant leans toward whatever light it's given.

"Here's the shape of it," Varrick said, not looking up from the flames, "since you strike me as old enough to want the shape of things even if you're short on words to ask for it." He fed a stick in, watched it catch. "I don't do charity. Charity's a rich man's hobby and I've never once in my life been rich. What I do is find things worth more than the trouble of carrying them, and I carry them, and I get paid for the carrying. You understand that much?"

Caelen said nothing. But he watched Varrick's mouth, and something in his chest — not quite understanding, but close enough to it that the distinction barely mattered — tracked the shape of what was being said.

"You're worth more than the trouble," Varrick went on, as if Caelen had answered. "That's not flattery, so don't go warming to it the way you're warming to that fire. A boy who came up whole out of a strata pocket the Guild's too spooked to even name out loud — that's not nothing. Men pay for the odd and the rare. Wardens pay more, generally, to make the odd and the rare disappear before it becomes somebody's problem. I'd rather be the one who gets paid than the one who gets disappeared, and I'd rather you were neither, which is the part where I tell you plainly: keeping you fed and keeping you quiet costs me something, and I'll expect something back for it. Not now. Not tomorrow. But eventually, and you'll know it when it comes."

He looked up then, finally, and the firelight caught his eyes in a way that made them look, for a moment, almost kind, though his voice never once bent that direction.

"That's the whole of the terms," he said. "You don't have to like them. You only have to understand you're getting fed either way tonight, terms or no terms, because I'm not fool enough to strike a bargain with something too hungry to hold up its end of it."

He set a pot on the stove, and dried strips of something Caelen didn't recognize, and the smell of it filling the small dry space was so enormous, so entirely consuming, that for a while Caelen forgot to be afraid of anything at all.

---

The trouble came after dark, with no warning that Caelen's new, strange senses managed to catch in time to matter.

He had eaten — carefully, the way Varrick doled it out to him in small measured portions instead of letting him gorge, though even that small measure was more food than Caelen had known in five days and it sat in him like a coal, banked and glowing. He had begun, for the first time since the mountain, to feel something close to safe, curled near the stove with the tin roof ticking as it cooled and Varrick's low even breathing settling into the rhythm of a man who slept light but slept.

The sound that woke both of them was not loud. That was what made it worse. A soft, deliberate weight shifting outside the shelter wall, close, testing — not the clumsy crash of something merely passing through, but the patient, considering step of something that had smelled the fire, or smelled them, and had decided the shelter's thin tin skin was worth investigating.

Varrick was up before Caelen had fully understood he was awake, pry-bar already in hand, silent in a way that told Caelen this was not the first time the man had woken to exactly this sound. "Stay behind me," he breathed, no dry humor left in it now, none at all, and that absence frightened Caelen more than the weight outside the wall had.

The thing outside made a sound then — low, wet, a rumble pitched below where a voice should sit, the kind of sound Caelen's body understood as *danger* before his mind had assembled a single thought around it — and something in Caelen's chest, the same hollow ringing hunger-note from the forest, changed pitch entirely. It went from a low ache to something sharp and enormous, filling him all at once the way the blast's heat had filled the amber, and Caelen had time to think, with the last clear thought that was fully his own, *I don't want this,* before his body stopped asking his permission at all.

It did not feel like power. He would understand, much later, why people who had never felt it might imagine it that way — a boy becoming something fanged and formidable, a gift, a door opening. It did not feel like a door opening. It felt like being torn.

Heat first, worse than the blast, radiating out from somewhere behind his sternum like a coal dropped into water, and then his own skin, too tight all at once, straining at the shoulders and the backs of his hands as though his body had decided, without consulting him, to become a size it had not yet earned. Something in his jaw cracked with a sound he felt more than heard, white and enormous, and he cried out — not a scream this time, something lower, wrenched out of a throat that did not yet know what shape it was trying to hold — as his fingers curled and the nails at their tips split and lengthened with a grinding pressure that emptied his lungs of every other sound he might have made.

He did not remember standing. He did not remember crossing the small dry space to put himself, snarling, low, half-doubled with pain and something that was not quite pain, between Varrick and the shelter's thin wall. He only remembered, after — because the after came fast, faster than anything before it, the whole enormous cost of it collecting itself and slamming due all at once — the cold.

It arrived like a held breath let go. One moment he was a shape too large for his own skin, hot enough at the joints to feel his own pulse in his teeth; the next, everything in him guttered at once, the borrowed heat gone as though a door somewhere had been thrown open onto winter, and he folded to the groundsheet shaking so hard his teeth cracked together, his body already smaller again, wrong in the opposite direction now — hollowed, drained, every ounce of the food he'd eaten that evening simply *gone*, spent in a handful of seconds the way the coal spends itself all at once when you finally let the draft in.

He heard, from very far away, the wet considering weight outside the wall reconsider, and withdraw, and go.

He heard, closer, Varrick letting out a long breath that was not quite steady.

---

Caelen did not remember much of what came after, not clearly — only pieces, the way the escape from the mine had come back to him in pieces. Varrick's hands, careful this time, not seizing but steadying, easing him back down onto the groundsheet. A blanket, rough wool, smelling of woodsmoke, pulled tight around a body that could not stop shaking no matter how close to the stove it was dragged. Something pressed to his lips — broth, this time, thin and hot, because Varrick had judged, correctly, that whatever Caelen's body had just spent could not be paid back all at once either.

"Well," Varrick said, some while later, when the shaking had eased into something merely exhausted, his voice pitched low and dry again, though it took him two tries to land the dryness where he wanted it. "That's one question answered, at least. I did wonder what exactly the Guild lost down that shaft besides a boy." He was quiet a moment, watching the fire, and when he spoke again the joke had gone thin, close to breaking, close to the true thing underneath it. "You ate three days' food in about as many seconds, near as I can reckon. If that's the price every time, we're going to need a considerably better system than me hoping you don't get frightened."

Caelen, hollow-eyed, too spent to do anything but listen, watched him in the firelight.

"Here's the amended terms, then," Varrick said, and pulled the blanket a fraction tighter around Caelen's shoulders with a roughness that was, Caelen understood even in his exhaustion, its own kind of care, offered sideways because it could not be offered straight. "I feed you. You don't die on me out of sheer inconvenience. And whatever in the nine hells you are, you and I are going to spend some considerable time finding out together, because I did not survive twenty years dodging Wardens by making bargains I didn't understand the terms of, and I am not about to start with you."

He banked the fire lower, settled back against the crate-table with the pry-bar still close to hand, and did not sleep again that night, though he let Caelen believe, for the small mercy of it, that he had.
