# Chapter Seven — What Rook Knew

Rook's arm healed crooked at the elbow, a shallow permanent bend that Varrick said would ache him every wet season for the rest of his life, and Caelen understood, without anyone saying it plainly, that the crookedness was going to sit between the two of them the same way it sat in the joint — small, permanent, worse in certain weathers.

It was not the arm itself. Rook wore the splint with the same fast, cursing bravado he wore everything else, made a running joke of it within two days that had Varrick almost smiling despite himself. It was the four feet of distance Rook had started keeping, quiet and consistent, that Caelen felt more than saw — the way Rook's bedroll had migrated, over three nights in the dry hollow, from an arm's reach of Caelen's to the far side of the fire. The way his fast talk, which had once run without a single wasted breath toward Caelen the same as anyone, had begun leaving small gaps exactly where Caelen used to be included in it, sentences that turned to Varrick instead, or to no one, or trailed off rather than land anywhere near him at all.

Caelen noticed. He noticed the way he had learned to notice everything since the mine — quietly, thoroughly, and with no idea yet what to do about what he found.

It came to a point on the fourth morning, over nothing, the way these things always seemed to. Caelen reached for the waterskin at the same moment Rook did, their hands nearly meeting over it, and Rook flinched back so hard and so fast that he knocked the skin from both their grips entirely, water darkening the dirt between them, and for one raw unguarded second before he could cover it, Caelen saw exactly what was underneath the four feet of distance.

Rook was afraid of him.

Not of the Wardens. Not of the Guild's ledger and its patient, terrible arithmetic. Of him — of the hands that had reached for the waterskin, the same hands that had torn a grown man's grip loose at the weigh-station with a sound that wasn't fabric, the same hands Rook had watched close around a body and throw it the length of a yard like it weighed nothing at all.

"Rook," Caelen said, quiet, and Rook was already up, already moving away toward the treeline with the water spilled and forgotten, his fast talk arriving too late and too thin to cover what his body had already said plainer than any words could.

"Need to check the road," Rook said, not looking back. "Somebody should. Varrick's arm's not fit for scouting and yours is—" He stopped himself. He did not finish the sentence. He did not need to.

He was gone into the trees before Caelen could find any words at all to send after him, and the dry hollow, in his absence, felt considerably colder than the morning's actual weather accounted for.

---

Varrick did not say anything for a long while after Rook left. He sat with his splinted arm cradled against his chest, watching the space where the boy had gone, and when he finally spoke, the dry economy that usually carried his voice through hard things had thinned down to something almost unrecognizable — plain, tired, entirely without its usual armor.

"He's not wrong to be careful," Varrick said. "I want you to hear that first, before anything else I say, because I watched your face just now and I know exactly what shape of hurt is sitting behind it, and I won't have you turning it into something it isn't. Rook being afraid doesn't mean Rook's stopped caring. Those aren't opposites, boy, whatever your gut's telling you right now. Sometimes they're the very same thing wearing two different coats."

Caelen sat with that, turning it the way he turned most hard things Varrick gave him, slow, uncertain of the shape.

"I'm going to tell you a word now," Varrick said, "and I want you to understand I've been putting this conversation off since the shelter, hoping I'd have more time to build up to it gentle. We don't have more time. Not after the weigh-station. Not after what I watched happen to your face out there, right before the end, when you were still fighting but something underneath the fighting had started looking out through your eyes that wasn't entirely you anymore."

He shifted, wincing at the arm, and made himself go on.

"Atavistic Drift," he said. "That's the word the Synod uses for it, in their proper doctrine-halls, and it's as good a word as any I've found, so we'll use theirs. It's what happens to a body that Shifts too far, too often, too close to the edge of what it can afford — and in your case, boy, I don't rightly know where that edge sits, because you're not spending Aethel-Cores like every other Resonant in the world, you're spending *something in yourself,* and I have got no ledger anywhere that tells me how deep that well goes before it runs dry of anything recognizably you."

"What happens," Caelen said, low, "when it runs dry."

Varrick was quiet a moment. When he answered, his voice had gone flatter still, the particular flatness Caelen was learning to recognize as the sound of a man reporting something he wished, very much, he did not have firsthand knowledge of.

"I knew a Resonant once," he said. "Older man. Ran a Talon Vector out of a proper licensed housing, three cores locked and legal, worked salvage same as me for the better part of a decade without trouble. Then a bad season came, and he took work he shouldn't have, Shifted more than any body ought to in a single month chasing a payday that would've kept his daughter fed through winter. I watched him lose himself in pieces. Small at first — forgot names, forgot which of us was which, forgot to come back all the way after a Shift instead of staying half in the shape a day, two days, longer each time. Then it wasn't small anymore." He stopped there, and Caelen understood, from the stillness in him, that whatever came next was not a thing Varrick was going to say plainly, not even now, not even with all his usual armor stripped away. "Wardens found what was left before winter's end. Wrote it up in their reports as a wild-animal incident. Easier for everyone's conscience that way, I imagine, than writing down what he actually used to be."

The dry hollow had gone very quiet. Somewhere off in the trees, faint, Caelen could hear Rook — not scouting the road at all, he understood now, only putting distance between himself and a fear he hadn't yet found words to carry — moving in slow restless circles that never went far and never came back either.

"That's what Rook's afraid of," Varrick said. "Not you. Not exactly you. He's afraid of watching the boy who shared his bread turn, by degrees, into somebody's report about a wild-animal incident. And I'll tell you plain, boy, so will I, if we don't find you some way to hold the line you held at the weigh-station on purpose, every time, instead of by the grace of however much of your own name you've got left to grab onto in the moment. That's not a controllable thing yet. That's luck wearing a brave face. And luck runs out."

---

Rook did not come back to the fire until well past midday, and when he did, he did not sit close, and he did not resume the easy unbroken stream of talk that had once made the space around him feel, however briefly, like something safe. He sat where he'd sat that morning, four careful feet distant, and picked at the crooked splint on his arm, and for a long while none of the three of them said anything at all.

It was Caelen, in the end, who broke it — not with words, because he still did not entirely trust himself with those, but by picking up the waterskin, refilled now from the stream, and setting it down in the space between himself and Rook. Not offered directly. Not pressed. Left, the way Varrick had once left a strip of dried meat on a flat stone at the base of a tree, for a thing to come to on its own terms or not at all.

Rook looked at it for a long moment. Then, slowly, he reached out — not for the skin, but close to it, close enough that Caelen understood the gesture for what it was: not forgiveness, not yet, not anything so whole and easy as that. Only a willingness, small and hard-won, not to run this time.

"I'm not — " Rook started, and stopped, and started again, his fast cadence gone careful in a way Caelen had only heard once before, at the fire in Hollow Cut, in the moment right before he'd decided to keep a secret. "I'm not going anywhere. I want that said plain, because I know how it looked this morning and I'm not proud of it. But I watched you throw a grown man like he was kindling, Caelen, and some animal part of my own head hasn't caught up yet to the part that still — " He stopped again, shorter this time, and did not finish it the easy way. He finished it the true way, the way Varrick's jokes never quite let him get to. "The part that still wants to sit close to the fire with you. Both parts are true right now. I don't know how long it'll take before there's only the one left."

It was not a promise. Caelen understood that clearly, in the four feet that still sat, unclosed, between them. It was something smaller and more honest than a promise, and in its own way harder-won for that.

He picked up the waterskin and drank, and passed it, this time, directly into Rook's hand, and Rook took it, and did not flinch.

It was not enough, not yet, to call the fracture healed. But it was, Caelen understood, watching Rook drink and pass the skin on to Varrick in turn, the first small stone laid toward whatever bridge might someday close the distance the weigh-station had opened between them — and for now, with the whole cold length of Atavistic Drift's warning still settling into him like a stone of its own, it would have to be enough.
