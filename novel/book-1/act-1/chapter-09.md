# Chapter Nine — Hollow Cut Burns

Ansel's forge sat at the quiet end of Hollow Cut's crooked lane, half-built into the hillside the same patient way the rest of the settlement was, and it was the first roof in three weeks Caelen had slept a full night under without one ear cocked for boots in the dark.

"He's not soft," Rook had warned them on the walk in, in the easier version of his fast talk that had been slowly, carefully rebuilding itself since the waterskin — not fully back to what it was, but close enough now that Caelen no longer had to strain to hear the warmth still living underneath it. "Don't let the beard fool you. Man's mended more housings than the Guild's whole licensed forge-hall and never once asked where the marrow in them came from, on account of he stopped believing questions like that were his to ask about twenty years back. He took me in when I had nowhere else worth the walking to. Feeds strays. You'll fit right in."

Ansel, when they reached him, turned out to be a broad, silver-bearded man with hands scarred pale from forty years of hot brass, who looked Caelen over exactly once — long, unhurried, the same thorough cataloguing look Rook had given him at the stall weeks ago — and said only, "Strata child, is it. Heard the whispers. Didn't credit half of them." He did not ask Caelen to confirm or deny it. He only turned back to his forge and said, over his shoulder, in a voice gone gruff with something that took Caelen a moment to recognize as simple, unremarkable kindness, "Well, you're thin as a whisper yourself. Sit. There's stew, and I'll not have a guest of Rook's going hungry under my roof on my account."

They stayed three days. It was, Caelen would understand much later, looking back, the longest stretch of ordinary peace he was given in the whole of that year — meals that did not have to be measured out in careful portions against a coming Shift, a fire that warmed instead of costing, Rook's crooked arm healing enough that his knife-work steadied, Ansel's low patient voice explaining the honest mechanics of a housing's fit while Varrick dozed, for once, without the pry-bar close to hand.

It ended on the third night, in the hour before dawn, with the sound of dogs.

---

Caelen woke to it before anyone else did — the particular sharp, coordinated baying of hounds worked in pairs, not the loose scattered barking of Hollow Cut's ordinary strays, and beneath it, low and growing, the tread of boots moving in the unhurried unison he had learned, at the weigh-station and long before it, to fear more than any noise a frightened man could make.

"Up," he said, and his own voice startled him with how steady it came out, how fast the old coiled readiness answered the sound before his mind had finished naming it. "Up, now—"

Varrick was awake before the second word left him, pry-bar already in hand, and Ansel a beat behind, moving with the grim economy of a man who had clearly heard hounds like this once before in his life and never forgotten the shape of what followed them.

The first torchlight found the window before any of them reached the door.

"BY ORDER OF THE WARDEN GARRISON AT KESH HOLLOW," a voice called out into the lane, amplified by nothing but its own trained carrying pitch, clause-stacked and flat and utterly unhurried, "THIS SETTLEMENT IS UNDER RECOVERY AUTHORITY PURSUANT TO ACTIVE THREAT CONTAINMENT. ALL RESIDENTS WILL REMAIN WITHIN STRUCTURES UNTIL CLEARED. ANY ATTEMPT AT CONCEALMENT OR OBSTRUCTION WILL BE TREATED AS ACCESSORY TO THE SUBJECT'S OFFENSES."

Caelen understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the pre-dawn air, that the subject was him.

Ansel was already moving, dragging the forge's heavy work-bench across the door with a strength that belied his age, shoving Caelen and Rook both toward the low rear passage that led out through the hillside itself, a smuggler's cut he'd clearly built or inherited long before any of them arrived to need it.

"Go," he said, and there was no fear in it at all, only the same gruff unremarkable steadiness he'd greeted Caelen with three days before. "Rook knows the way out past the old cistern. Varrick, you go with them—"

"Ansel—"

"I said go." For the first time since Caelen had known him, something hard entered the old man's voice, an authority that brooked no more argument than a closing door. "I've got forty years of Guild men and Warden patrols both thinking I'm nothing worth the bother of searching twice. That's worth spending, tonight, on making sure they keep thinking it a few minutes longer. Rook." His voice gentled, just slightly, just for the boy's name. "Go on, now. You've done enough hard leaving in your life. This isn't going to be one you have to carry the blame for."

Rook did not move. Caelen watched him not move, watched the fast cadence that had only just begun rebuilding itself go entirely, terribly silent, and understood that this was the moment the crooked bridge from the dry hollow was being tested against something far heavier than a spilled waterskin.

It was Varrick, in the end, who moved first — hauling Rook bodily toward the passage with a grip that brooked no further argument, and it was that, more than any word, that finally broke Rook loose from the doorway and sent him stumbling after.

The last Caelen saw of Ansel, glancing back once before the passage swallowed the light, was the old man setting his shoulder to the barricaded door, unhurried, steady, a man who had decided a long time ago exactly what he was willing to spend and on whom.

---

The passage came out behind the settlement's old cistern into a lane already thick with smoke, torches gone wrong somewhere in the chaos and caught a rooftop, the fire spreading fast through tin and dry timber with a hunger that made the settlement's earlier warmth feel, in retrospect, like a debt the whole place had been quietly accruing for years. Screaming carried from every direction now, and the clause-stacked amplified voice had given way entirely to the ordinary chaos of a raid gone larger than its own procedure, shapes running, shapes falling, the dogs' baying gone frantic and directionless in the smoke.

A Warden found them at the cistern's edge before they'd gone twenty strides — young, Caelen registered distantly, younger than the broad man at the weigh-station, his blade already out and his voice already climbing toward the particular pitch of a man reciting doctrine to steady his own nerve rather than to inform anyone listening.

"SUBJECT IDENTIFIED, JUVENILE MALE, PURSUANT TO RECOVERY ORDER SEVEN-DASH—"

Caelen felt the heat begin behind his sternum, the coal dropped in water, faster now than it had ever come, and for one long, suspended half-second he understood he had a choice in a way he hadn't fully had one at the weigh-station — the small warm stone of his own name held firm this time, not thin and desperate but deliberate, chosen, the way Varrick had spent three weeks teaching him to choose it.

He did not let the whole of it take him. He let only enough through to move faster than the young Warden's blade could track, enough to close the distance and drive a shoulder into the man's chest hard enough to put him down without tearing anything that wouldn't mend, and the cost of that restraint — of taking only what he needed and no more, of steering the enormous wrong-shaped hunger rather than drowning in it — showed itself instantly in how much smaller the aftermath was: a shudder, a wave of cold, his knees briefly unsteady, nothing like the collapse at the weigh-station. He had, for the first time, spent only what the moment required.

"Move," Varrick barked, already past him, and they ran.

---

They found Malakar's line at the settlement's eastern edge, where the lane opened toward the treeline and the whole crawling chaos of the raid funneled, inevitably, toward the one clean route out — which meant, Caelen understood with a sinking certainty, that it was also exactly where the Wardens had planted their most disciplined cordon to wait for precisely this.

Malakar himself stood a little apart from the line, unarmed, watching the burning settlement with an expression Caelen had no context yet to read — not satisfaction, not quite horror, something caught motionless between the two, a man doing the arithmetic of a doctrine that had promised him *contained* and delivered, instead, an entire settlement on fire.

"Hold the subject for recovery," Malakar said, to the line, in a voice that carried the same clause-stacked precision as the man at the window, but slower now, and Caelen — sharp-eared, sharp-eyed, closer to him now than he'd ever been to any Warden — caught the half-beat hesitation buried inside it, a word chosen and then, almost imperceptibly, replaced. "Non-lethal containment only. I want him alive and I want that order followed to the letter, is that clear to every man on this line—"

It was not clear to every man on the line. One of them, closer to the treeline, panicking at the sight of Rook's knife and Varrick's pry-bar closing fast, did not wait for containment orders at all — and it was Ansel, arriving at a dead run from the burning lane behind them with a strength that should have belonged to a man decades younger, who put himself into the blade's path meant for Rook's back.

Rook's scream tore through the smoke louder than any of the fire.

Caelen felt the coal behind his sternum ignite entirely then, past choosing, past the careful restraint he'd only just learned to hold — and this time he let it, because there was nothing left in that moment worth holding back for. The transformation came fast and total and terrible, and the last clean thought he carried into it, the small warm stone of his own name gripped tight enough this time that even the undertow couldn't fully drown it, was Ansel's voice from three nights ago: *I'll not have a guest of Rook's going hungry under my roof on my account.*

He did not remember, afterward, exactly how the Warden line broke. He remembered heat, and cold, and Varrick's voice somewhere distant shouting his name like an anchor line thrown into water. He remembered Malakar, of all the shapes in that burning chaos, not moving to stop him — standing very still instead, doctrine and whatever lived underneath it colliding openly across his face for one unguarded instant before training reasserted itself and he stepped back, out of the treeline's reach, and let the line scatter around him rather than order it to hold.

By the time Caelen came back to himself, smaller, hollowed, shaking with a cold that went all the way to the marrow, they were past the cordon, into the trees, the settlement burning orange-black behind them against a sky just beginning, indifferently, to grey with actual dawn.

Ansel was not with them.

---

They found him a hundred strides back, where Rook had refused to stop looking despite Varrick's hand hard on his good arm, propped against a fallen log with the wound already past anyone's ability to argue with. He was still breathing. Not for long, and all three of them understood it, but for long enough.

"Told you," Ansel said, to Rook, in a voice gone thin but still carrying, underneath the thinness, that same gruff unremarkable steadiness. "Not one you have to carry the blame for. Look at me when I say that, boy. I mean it plain."

Rook was not steady enough to answer. He held the old man's scarred hand in both of his own, his fast cadence entirely gone, nothing left in him but a silence Caelen understood, watching, as the loudest thing Rook had ever said.

Ansel's eyes found Caelen last, and something in them, even now, even here, held no fear at all — only the same thorough, unhurried cataloguing look from the forge three days before, settled now into something closer to a verdict.

"Strata child," he said, softer. "Whatever the Synod ends up calling what you are, whatever the Guild writes in its ledger — you're a boy who came into my house hungry and left it fought for. Remember that part longer than you remember the fire." His hand tightened, briefly, around Rook's. "Go on, now. All of you. I've done my spending for tonight, and I'll not have it wasted watching you linger."

He did not say anything else. Caelen understood, some while later, walking in the grey light with Rook's silence beside him and Varrick's steadying hand at his back, that there had been no clean moment of passing to mark, no single instant he could point to and say *that was when.* There had only been the fire behind them, and the walking, and Ansel simply, quietly, no longer among the three shapes moving through it.

Hollow Cut burned for most of that day, they would learn later, and rebuilt itself, in the patchwork stubborn way such places always did, within the season. But the small dry forge at the quiet end of its crooked lane did not rebuild, because there was no one left who knew, the way Ansel had known, exactly which questions were his to ask and which were better left in the ground where they'd come from.

Caelen carried the weight of that all the way to the Glass Wastes, alongside the small warm stone of his own name, and understood, walking, that the stone had grown considerably heavier for the carrying.
