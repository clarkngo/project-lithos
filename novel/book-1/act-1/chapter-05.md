# Chapter Five — Rook

Hollow Cut announced itself by smell a long while before it announced itself by sight — brass oil and woodsmoke and, threaded under both, that same warm mineral sweetness Caelen had learned to name in the weeks since the shelter, a whole settlement's worth of Aethel-glass cooking slow in a hundred small forges. He walked it now on two legs, steady, no longer dropping to all fours when the ground turned uncertain, though the habit still lived in him, coiled and ready, closer to the surface than he liked Varrick to notice.

Three weeks had taught him a great deal. He knew now what a conduit was for, though he had none of his own and Varrick had made it plain, more than once, in the dry flat voice he used for things that mattered most, that Caelen wearing one anywhere a stranger could see it would be a considerably faster way to die than simply staying wild. He knew the word *Warden* the way you know a sound that means duck low and go still. He had eaten enough, in small measured portions, that his wrists no longer looked like something a strong wind could snap. He still did not fully have the word for what had happened to him in the strata, or for what he was now, only the two words Varrick had settled on between them — *grown, not touched* — worn smooth with repetition into something almost like comfort.

He had not Shifted again. Varrick did not say so aloud, but Caelen understood, in the careful way the man steered them wide of anything that startled loud or moved fast in the dark, that the avoiding was deliberate, and constant, and cost the both of them something in the miles it added to every day's walking.

Hollow Cut, when the trees finally gave it up, was not what Caelen had built in his head from Varrick's descriptions. He had imagined something like the mine — hard edges, iron, a place built for extraction. What he found instead was a settlement grown the way a scab grows, patched together out of whatever the Guild had discarded on its way past: salvaged tin roofing, cart frames turned into stall counters, a whole crooked lane of lean-tos built from crates stenciled with Guild markings half-scratched out. Smoke stood over it in a low permanent haze. Voices carried in every direction, haggling, laughing, arguing over weights and measures, and under all of it a sound Caelen had not heard since before the mountain — the ordinary, unafraid noise of people simply living somewhere, going on with the business of a day.

"Don't stare," Varrick said, not unkindly, steering him with a hand briefly at the shoulder. "And don't talk unless somebody talks to you first, and if somebody does, you let me answer for you. Strata child's a story people tell each other over a fire. Strata child standing in Hollow Cut's marketplace is a story that gets somebody a very good price from a Warden captain, and I'd rather it wasn't us who taught them that trade was worth making."

They cut through the lane toward a stall at its far end, half-swallowed in the haze, where a boy not much older than Caelen — though built leaner, harder, with the particular watchful stillness of someone who had learned early that stillness kept a person fed — sat perched on an overturned crate, whittling something out of a scrap of conduit brass with a knife too big for his hand and exactly the right size for the point he was making with it.

"Varrick," the boy said, without looking up, in a voice that ran fast and a little rough, words crowding each other the way water crowds a narrow channel. "Heard you were dead. Was hoping, honestly. Would've simplified a debt I've been carrying since spring."

"Wouldn't dream of inconveniencing you that permanently, Rook." Varrick set his pack down with a grunt, unhurried, clearly enjoying himself in some understated way Caelen was starting to recognize as his baseline state around people he trusted. "You still owe me for the housing I moved in Long Reach, or has your memory conveniently rusted along with the rest of you?"

"My memory's fine, it's my *conscience* that's rusted, there's a difference — " Rook's knife stopped mid-stroke. His eyes had found Caelen, and stayed there, the fast tumble of his talk running down like a wheel losing its spin. "Who's this, then."

"Caelen," Varrick said, in the same flat, giving-away-nothing tone he used for the price of grain. "Travels with me now."

"Travels with you," Rook repeated, slow, testing the shape of the lie in his mouth the way a man tests a coin for the ring of good metal. He looked at Caelen a moment longer — not unkindly, but thoroughly, the look of someone cataloguing every wrong detail at once: the too-careful stillness, the eyes that tracked movement a half-beat too sharp, the way Caelen's weight had settled, unconsciously, onto the balls of his feet the instant a stranger's attention landed on him. "Right. Sure. And I'm the second son of a Spire lord, slumming it for the fresh air."

"Believe whatever keeps you sleeping easy," Varrick said. "I need three housings reforged before week's end, and I need them quiet, and I need you not to ask the Guild factor down the lane where the marrow in them came from."

"Everything you need is quiet lately." Rook set the knife down, unfolded himself off the crate in one economical motion, and came around the stall's edge to stand closer — not threatening, but close enough that Caelen's whole body went very still, the old coiled readiness flexing once under his skin before he made himself let it go slack. Rook noticed. Of course he noticed; Caelen was coming to understand that noticing things was the whole of what had kept a boy like Rook alive this long in a place like this.

"You're jumpy," Rook said to him directly, the fast rough cadence gentling half a step, though not all the way — bravado still riding close over whatever was underneath it. "Somebody been hunting you?"

Caelen, remembering Varrick's instruction, said nothing.

"He doesn't talk much," Varrick said, easy, closing the subject the way he closed most subjects that mattered — by simply declining to open it further. "You'll live with the disappointment."

Rook studied Caelen another long moment, and something behind the appraisal shifted, settled into something Caelen didn't have a name for yet but would, in time, learn to call *decided.* "Fine. Doesn't talk. I talk enough for three people anyway, my aunt used to say it like it was a diagnosis." He grinned, sudden and crooked, gone as fast as it came. "You eaten today, strata boy? — don't answer that, you've got the look of somebody who eats like it's a favor being done to him instead of a thing he's owed. Come on. I've got bread that's only slightly stale and a fire that only slightly smokes, which around here is practically a feast."

---

The fire, when they reached it — a low pit behind Rook's stall, ringed with salvaged brick, tucked out of the lane's worst traffic — did smoke, considerably more than slightly, but Caelen found he didn't mind it. It smelled, under the char, faintly of the same warm mineral sweetness that clung to everything in Hollow Cut, and he had stopped, somewhere in the last three weeks, flinching from that smell the way he once had.

Rook talked while he tore the bread into three uneven pieces, and did not stop talking for a long while — about a Guild factor who'd shorted him on a weigh-in, about a girl two lanes over who wouldn't speak to him anymore on account of a misunderstanding he insisted, twice, wasn't his fault, about the particular indignity of a knife that wouldn't hold an edge past a week no matter how he stropped it — and Caelen found, sitting close to the smoking fire with bread in his hands that he had been *given*, freely, by someone who hadn't measured the portion first, that the talking was its own kind of warmth. He did not have to answer any of it. He only had to be there, and let it wash past him, the ordinary noise of a boy his own age simply existing out loud, and something in his chest that had been clenched tight since the mountain loosened, by degrees, without his quite noticing it happen.

He caught Rook watching him once, mid-sentence, in the pause between one story and the next — not the sharp cataloguing look from the stall, something softer, quickly covered over with another burst of talk before it could be examined too closely. Caelen understood, dimly, that he had just watched Rook do the same thing Varrick did with his dry jokes: cover the true thing fast, before anyone, including himself, could get too good a look at it.

It was Varrick who broke the ease of it, straightening from where he'd been half-dozing against a crate, head coming up at a sound that hadn't yet reached Caelen's own ears — and then, a breath later, it did: boots, several pairs, moving in the particular unhurried unison of men who did not need to hurry because no one in Hollow Cut had ever successfully told them no.

Rook's talk cut off mid-word.

The patrol came down the lane in grey-and-brass, four of them, unhurried the way water is unhurried right up until it finds the crack it's been looking for, and the ordinary noise of the market did not stop so much as it lowered, folded in on itself, every stall-keeper suddenly very interested in their own hands. One of the four carried a paper, held out at intervals to be glanced at and dismissed, and as they passed close enough that Caelen's sharp new hearing could catch the words over the crackle of the fire, he heard the phrase before he understood he was meant to fear it.

"— survivor of the Long Seam collapse, presumed juvenile, unaccounted-for remains never recovered — Guild's offering considerable weight for confirmed sighting, considerably more for the article itself — "

The paper moved past. The patrol moved past, unhurried, checking faces the way a man checks coins for a counterfeit's telltale weight, and did not stop at the smoking little fire behind a salvage stall, because a boy sitting quiet between two others, bread in his hands, gave them nothing yet worth stopping for.

Rook did not resume talking once they'd gone. He sat very still, for the first time since Caelen had met him, watching the lane where the grey-and-brass had disappeared, and when he finally spoke again his fast rough cadence had gone low and careful in a way that made him sound, for just a moment, considerably older than the crate he'd been perched on an hour before.

"The article," he repeated, quiet, and looked at Caelen — really looked, the whole shape of the last hour rearranging itself behind his eyes into something he'd clearly, in that instant, finished deciding. "That's you, isn't it. Not *travels with him.* You're the thing they're paying weight for."

Varrick said nothing, which was its own kind of answer, and Caelen, bread going cold and forgotten in his hands, understood with a lurch that the ordinary warmth of the last hour had just become something that could be spent, or kept, entirely at the discretion of a boy he had known for the length of one smoking little fire.

"Huh," Rook said, at last, and something settled in his face that was not quite a smile and not quite anything Caelen had a name for yet — decision, again, the same decided look from the stall, worn now into something with more weight behind it. "Well. Guess that explains the jumpiness." He tore off another piece of the bread, unhurried now in a way that mirrored Varrick's own particular stillness, and held it out. "Eat. You look like you're about to fall over, and I didn't share my only decent loaf with a ghost just to watch him faint in my fire pit."

He did not say he'd keep the secret. He did not need to. He simply went on sitting there, close, unbothered, the fast rough talk starting up again a moment later — quieter now, gentler at the edges in a way Caelen was only beginning to understand was Rook's version of the truth Varrick's dry jokes were built to hide — and Caelen, watching the smoke rise thin over Hollow Cut's crooked lane, understood that he had, without quite meaning to, been given a second person worth being careful for.
