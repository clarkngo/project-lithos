# Chapter Four — Names for What He Is

Morning came grey and windless, the rain finally spent somewhere in the night, and Varrick woke Caelen not with a hand on his shoulder but with the flat clink of metal set down close beside his head — deliberate, unhurried, a sound meant to be heard rather than felt.

Caelen opened his eyes to find a shard of something amber-dark resting on the groundsheet a hand's width from his face.

It was small, no bigger than a knucklebone, and even in the flat grey light coming through the shelter's one warped window it seemed to hold a little of that light inside itself, the way water holds the sky. He did not need to touch it to know it was warm. He had known the warmth of things like it his whole life, from the inside, and some part of him — the part that still, on bad mornings, reached for a pulse that was no longer there — recognized it the way a tongue recognizes salt before the mind has caught up to naming it.

"Go on," Varrick said, from across the shelter, where he was working a whetstone along the edge of his pry-bar in long unhurried strokes. "Won't bite you. Not that piece, anyway. Small enough it hasn't got the strength left in it to bite anybody."

Caelen picked it up. It sat in his palm heavier than its size accounted for, warm the way a stone left in sun is warm, though there was no sun in the shelter and had not been for two days.

"That," Varrick said, watching him over the whetstone, "is an Aethel-Core. Cut small, near spent, worth about what a decent meal's worth to the right buyer and nothing at all to the wrong one. It's marrow. Old marrow. Comes out of the deep strata the same place you did, which I don't imagine is a coincidence either of us is going to enjoy thinking too hard about before breakfast." He set the whetstone down. "You've got a hundred questions behind those eyes and no words handy to ask them with. I've decided that's inconvenient for both of us. So we're going to fix that today, one piece at a time, starting with the piece in your hand."

He held out his own palm, and after a moment Caelen passed the shard back, reluctant in a way he didn't examine too closely.

"Marrow," Varrick said again, turning it in the light. "Lithoi marrow. There used to be people — not people like you and me, something older, something the mountains grew instead of birthed, near as anybody's ever proven — and what ran in their bones instead of blood is what's sitting in your hand right now, vitrified, cut, sold by the ounce to men with more ambition than sense. Mining Guild pulls it out of the deep seams. Has done for longer than either of our grandfathers were alive to complain about it." His voice did not change pitch as he said it, flat and dry as everything else he said, but something moved under the flatness, brief, gone before Caelen could be sure he'd seen it at all. "There's not many of them left to complain on their own account. Hasn't been for a long while."

Caelen did not understand all of it. But he understood *gone*, and something in the word settled into him beside the pulse he still missed, cold and heavy, a grief with no shape yet, borrowed before he'd earned the right to feel it and kept anyway.

"This," Varrick went on, businesslike again, reaching to his belt for the oilcloth bundle Caelen had marked as warm back in the clearing, "is a conduit." He unwrapped it with more care than he'd shown the shard — brass, dull with wear, shaped to lock around a forearm, three small fitted housings sunk into its face like sockets waiting for eyes. "Man locks a core like that one into a housing like this, and if he's done the fitting right and hasn't lied to himself about what he can afford to spend, his body does something a body's got no business doing. Changes shape. Changes what it's made of, for a while. There's a whole cursed grammar to it — what shape you take, what temper it comes with, how far you push past sense into something worse — but the short of it, the part that'll keep you breathing, is this." He tapped the brass once, a small flat sound. "Costs. Always costs. Costs food, costs warmth, costs — " He stopped himself there, the dry economy of his voice catching on something he'd decided, visibly, not to say plainly. "Costs more than food and warmth, sometimes, if a man's fool enough to keep spending past what he can pay back. You felt a piece of that already. Last night wasn't even the whole bill. Wasn't even close."

Caelen's hand found its way, without his permission, to his own sternum, to the place the heat had come from.

"That's the word for the thing you did," Varrick said, watching him do it. "Resonance. Lithosomatic Resonance, if you want the whole mouthful, though nobody outside a Synod hall says all of that twice." He wrapped the conduit back in its oilcloth, unhurried. "Here's the part you need to hear plainest of all, boy, plainer than any of the rest of it, because it's the part that keeps you alive and me out of a Warden cell. What you did last night — no core, no conduit, no housing, no fitting, nothing locked to your arm at all — that is not a thing a Resonant is supposed to be able to do. Every soul who's ever done what you did last night paid an Aethel-Core and a brass housing for the privilege. You paid nothing. You just — " he gestured, a short economical motion that stood in for a whole missing vocabulary, " — did it. Off your own body. Off whatever's sitting where a boy's marrow ought to be instead."

He let that sit in the small dry space between them for a long moment, the fire ticking low in the stove, rain starting again somewhere outside, soft against the tin.

"Wardens hear about a wild Resonant," Varrick said, quieter now, and this time nothing in his voice tried to be dry at all, "unlicensed, unbound, no core to confiscate and no housing to seize — they don't take that boy in for questioning. They don't have a procedure for a boy like that. They've only got the one procedure for a thing that scares them and can't be licensed, and it doesn't end with paperwork." He looked at Caelen very directly then, the closest thing to gentleness Caelen had yet seen cross his face, and no less hard for it. "So you're going to learn the words for what you are, because a thing with a name is a thing a man can plan around, and a thing with no name is just a thing that gets you both killed by accident."

Caelen sat with the shard's warmth still ghosted in his palm, and something in him — the same hollow place that had learned to want food, to want warmth, to want the sound of unhurried boots not to walk away — reached for the last piece of what Varrick had said and turned it over, slow, the way he'd once turned the small stone of his own name.

"Not," he said.

His voice came out of him wrecked, unused, cracked in the middle like a thing too long shut in a box. It was the first word he had spoken since the mountain, and it cost him something to make it, a rawness at the back of his throat that had nothing to do with any Shift at all.

Varrick went very still.

"Not," Caelen said again, steadier the second time, though it still scraped. He touched his own chest, where the coal-heat had come from, where the pulse used to be. "Not — touched. Not like the piece." He struggled, the words coming slow and badly fitted, a boy relearning a tool he'd once used without thinking and now had to build again from nothing. "Grown," he said, finally, the word arriving from somewhere he didn't examine too closely, some certainty laid into him at the marrow the way the shape of the mine's dark turns had been laid into him. "I was grown. In it. Not — touched."

Varrick did not say anything for a long time. He looked at Caelen the way he'd looked at him in the clearing that first morning, weighing something, rearranging his face around a decision whose size he still wasn't showing.

"Grown," he repeated, finally, very quietly, and for once there was no dry humor anywhere in it at all, nothing left to hide the true thing underneath. "Well." He set the wrapped conduit down beside the whetstone, careful, as if it had gotten heavier in his hands. "That's a considerably worse sentence than the one I was building toward, and I want you to know I do not say that lightly, having spent the better part of the night worrying that the *good* explanation was going to be plenty bad enough."

He was quiet a moment more, watching the rain find its way in thin threads down the warped window glass.

"Well," he said again, and pushed himself up onto his feet, brisk now, the moment folded away the way Caelen would learn he folded away most moments too large to carry openly. "Grown or touched, you're still a boy who needs feeding and a name the world can use without getting us both hanged, so we'll start where we can and work up to the rest of it." He held out a hand — not to be taken, Caelen understood after a beat, but to be seen, an offer laid flat rather than pressed. "Caelen," he said. "That's what you gave me back in the clearing, near as I could make it out of all that not-talking. That still the truth of it?"

Caelen nodded. His throat still ached from the two words it had cost him, but he found, testing it, that there was a third one left in him after all.

"Caelen," he said, and heard his own name in his own wrecked, unfamiliar voice for the first time since the mountain had taken everything else away from him, and it felt, in the cold grey morning with the rain starting again on the tin, like the first true thing he had been given back.

"Caelen," Varrick agreed, as if closing a bargain, and did not offer his hand again, because he had already seen what he needed to see. "Right, then. Eat something. Small portions — we're not making last night's mistake twice on an empty stomach's account. And then, boy who was grown and not touched, you and I are going to have a longer conversation about exactly how far Hollow Cut is from here, and what kind of quiet you're going to need to learn to keep between now and when we reach it."
