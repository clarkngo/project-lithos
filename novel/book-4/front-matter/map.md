# A Note on the Territory — Book Four

*(Supplements the earlier territory notes. This account covers only the ground this book adds: the Salt Coast, three weeks by sea from the Spire.)*

The sea has no ground to read, and this account will not pretend otherwise. It records only what its keepers consider worth the ink.

**The crossing** takes twenty-two days from the Spire's port in fair weather, and is dangerous after the first autumn gales, which in the year of this book arrived a month early.

**The Salt Coast** is a long black seaboard whose cliffs are banded with honey-colored stone. It has no great cities. Its people mine by lantern and rope at low water, never at scale, and keep a record of everything they pull from the cliffs.

**The third harbor** is built on a stair: ledge over ledge of whitewashed houses climbing a cliff from the quay, joined by flights of black steps, with a boom chain laid on the bottom since before the oldest Ledger and raised only for storms.

**The tide-house** stands on stilts at the head of the stair, with the sea running beneath it twice a day. It holds the **Deep Ledger**: hundreds of oiled-sailcloth volumes, one for every year, recording every body ever drawn from the cliffs.

**The cliff road** runs north along the headland, swept shell and gravel edged with white stones, lit by forty-one small tin lamps in niches at the cliff's lip. Nobody walks it. Somebody sweeps it.

**The Honey Chapel** lies at the head of the road: a whitewashed passage forty paces long, and, at its end, a natural chamber whose walls are veined with luminous stone and carved with a spiral nobody read for three hundred years.

**The pockets** have names, given by the harbor and not by any survey: the Weeping Stair, the Long Shelf, the Heron's Knee, Nessa's Dip, the Drowned Bell, the Cradle Hole, the Old Mouth. There are forty-one. The Ledger's margin counts a hundred and nine sleepers among them.

**The Teeth** are a line of rocks beyond the harbor mouth where the sea breaks white in a gale. Boats have been lost there since the harbor was founded.

*Neither this account nor any before it has mapped the country whole. It notes only that Halse's survey names four hundred and eleven seams with a periodic pulse along the western seaboard, and that no one knows how many are occupied.*
