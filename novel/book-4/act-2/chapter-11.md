# Chapter Eleven — What the Coast Remembers

He woke on the fourth day, in a clean white bed, and the first thing he noticed was that he was not cold.

It was so strange that he lay quite still for a long time, looking at the ceiling, trying to find the shape of it. It was the infirmary on the upper floor of the tide-house, the room with the open window. The light was grey and soft. Outside, gulls were crying over a calm sea, and the sound of hammering came up from the quay, and somewhere in the next room a child was humming, thin and tuneless, over and over, the same three tones and a fall. He had been cold every waking hour of his life since a mountain split open. It had never left him, not once. He had banked it and rationed it and eaten against it and wrapped himself against it, a cold that lived in the bone, and now it was gone, and in its place lay a quiet so wide that he did not know where to put his hands.

"You're awake," said Tamsin.

She was in the chair by the bed with a blanket over her knees and her black book open on it, and a smear of ink on her cheek that had been there so long it was nearly a feature. Her braid had come undone. She looked as though she had not slept in a week. She looked, he thought, quite wonderful.

"How long?"

"Four days. The storm blew out the second night. We thought you'd — " She stopped, and pressed the heel of her hand to her mouth, and took it away. "We've been taking turns. The girl wouldn't leave. She sat on the end of the bed for two days with that horse. Seraphina made her eat."

"The boy?"

"Alive. He's down the hall. He's — " Her face did something helpless. "He sings. All day. He's learned the Reeve's name and he says it at him. Hask comes every morning and sits there. I don't think he's said more than ten words."

"And the others?"

"Two in the Drowned Bell. Still in the glass. The cracks held. Seraphina says the shell has to be sealed before the next tide and she thinks it can be, with — with what you showed us. With the song." A pause. "She says nobody's to be woken until they're ready. She says it's what the wall means by *don't hurry.* She's been sitting with them every night."

He closed his eyes.

It was all right, then. It was all right. He lay in the white bed in the grey light and felt the thing go through him, slowly, like warmth into numb fingers, and did not know whether it was grief or joy or simple exhaustion. He had held. They had held. The cliff was still there. Rook was alive and Corwin was alive and the girl was alive and a boy of nine, in the room next door, was learning a Reeve's name.

He put his hand flat on his chest, over the place where the bank had been. It was quiet. It was warm. It was only a chest.

---

They had decided it without him.

That was the first thing Malakar told him, in the afternoon, sitting on the end of the bed with a cup of something black. The council had met on the day after the storm, in the tide-house, with the wind still blowing the shutters. It had not taken long. Hask had not spoken. He had walked in, still in his salt-stiff coat, with the boy's blood on the sleeve, and had stood at the head of the black oak table, and laid his father's key on it.

It was an iron key, as long as a man's hand, black with age. It opened a door in the cliff nobody had ever found. The Reeve had put it down beside the lead box and said, "I will not vote. I am not fit." A pause. "But I will tell you what I know. I know that the vow was *we'll not wake what we can't keep.* I know that I have kept for twenty-six years and I did not keep him. A stranger did that. A stranger, and a child, and the whole harbor in the rain with lamps." His great shoulders had shaken. "I should like to learn how."

The vote was ten to one. The shipwright had abstained, with dignity, and then voted aye.

"What did they decide?" said Caelen.

"To open the Ledger." Malakar turned his cup. "To enter the sleepers on the roll, publicly, under the hall's finding, by name, where there is a name, and by note where there is none. To wake them, a pocket at a time, in the open, with the rite, and a physician, and the lamp families, as many as wish to sit. To accept the Guild's money in escrow under the Ledger's seal, and refuse every other thing it offers. The Engineer-Captain's party will be sent south on the first fair tide, with a letter." He glanced up. "Reth wrote it himself."

"What does it say?"

"That Captain Halse is dismissed from the service of the Mining Guild for firing a charge in defiance of the Chairman's written order, and that the Board is requested to note that the order was lawful and the defiance was not." A dry breath. "It is the kindest document I have ever seen end a career."

"Halse?"

"Is in the cells under the quay. Not by anyone's wish. He asked." Malakar's voice was quite level. "He sat in the infirmary for two nights, outside the boy's door, and did not speak. On the third he asked to be locked up. I think he wanted to be somewhere where he could not do anything."

Caelen was quiet for a time.

"I don't hate him," he said.

"No. Nor does the Chairman." Malakar finished his cup. "That is the thing about this affair that I should have liked to explain to myself at twenty-five. There is no villain. There is only a very large, very reasonable machine, and a small number of people who have decided, one by one, to stop being part of it."

---

They gave him the afternoon to get up.

He walked the length of the quay, slowly, with Rook on one side and the girl on the other, and every person he passed took off their cap.

He bore it better than he would have a month ago. He did not know why. He walked in the thin gold light with his hand on a child's shoulder, and the harbor opened round him, the white houses, the green water, the nets spread on the stones to dry. The lamp-keepers were on the cliff, trimming their wicks in broad daylight, and when Nessa saw him she raised her net-needle, once, like a salute, and went back to her work.

"Rook." He said it softly. "Can you hear me?"

"Right side only." Rook tapped his left ear. "Seraphina says it won't come back. Not all of it. A bit, maybe." He grinned crookedly. "It's got an advantage. If you mumble at me from the wrong side I can pretend I didn't hear."

"I'm sorry."

"Shut up." But his voice cracked. "Shut up, Caelen. I'd pay the price again. I'd pay it for the kid."

Corwin sat on a barrel at the end of the quay with his arm in a sling of tarred canvas and a pencil in his other hand, writing a letter to Lisbet in a vile scrawl. He looked up, saw them coming, and held up the page. *Dear Lis,* it began. *I have broken my wrist. It is the single bravest thing I have ever done and it was entirely an accident. I am coming home soon. The kettle had better be on.*

"I've been thinking," Corwin said, "of settling here."

"You're a Spire noble."

"I was a second son with a mended cuff." He looked out over the harbor. "I never had a house of my own. I think I should like to have one with a view of the sea, and a green door, and a sign over it that says *come in.*" A faint wry smile. "I'm told the harbor is short of clerks."

---

Vesh found him on the way back.

The Harbor-Master was standing at the door of the tide-house with the great Ledger open in his arms, as if he had just carried it down the steps. His spectacles were pushed up in his hair. He wore no coat. In the plain blue shirt, with his sleeves rolled and a smear of ink to the elbow, he looked ten years younger.

"I've been counting," he said. "You will forgive me. I could not stop."

He turned the Ledger round, and Caelen looked down.

It was the page at the back. It had been ruled in the old columns, in the neat angular hand of the clerks, and the margin down the outer edge was black with little tally-strokes: a hundred and nine of them, in rows, grouped in fives. But beside the first of them, in new ink, someone had drawn a line through the stroke and written beside it, in Vesh's own exact shaking hand, a long low curve that climbed in the middle and bent over to a final soft peak, and the word *awake.* And beside the second, a smaller curve, and *awake, the Long Shelf.* And beside the third, with a date: *The Drowned Bell. The Reeve's boy.*

There were three of them. The rest were still strokes.

"I shall do it a line at a time," Vesh said. "It will take me the rest of my life. I find I do not mind." His voice shook. "Nineteen years I believed I was keeping the dead. I am told it is the best news the Keeper of the Deep Ledger has had in three hundred years."

"What will you do with the strokes?"

"Cross them out." He looked up. "One by one. As they are woken. And write what I am told." A trembling smile. "I should like it recorded, Mister Caelen, that I did not need the Chairman's money, or the hall's finding, or the Guild's seal. I needed someone to sit down on a floor and be quiet. You did that. And they came out."

Caelen could not answer. He stood on the steps in the thin light with the girl's hand in his, and for a long moment the only sound in the world was the sea, and the child at his side, humming.

It was a note he knew. It was the three tones and a fall. And under it, so quiet that he almost missed it, was another sound, one he had never heard: the plain, ordinary, empty ring of his own breath, in a chest where something used to burn.
