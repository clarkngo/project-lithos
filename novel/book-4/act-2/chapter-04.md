# Chapter Four — Grown, Not Touched

He walked out of the chapel, up the long whitewashed passage, past the tallow candles and the cold brackets, and stood on the gravel under the sky for a very long time without knowing what to do with his hands.

It was late afternoon. The cliff road ran away north along the headland in the low gold light, white edge-stones and swept gravel and small tin hoods, and the sea beyond it was the color of a dove's breast. Gulls turned over the water. A boy was driving goats along the clifftop with a stick. He saw Caelen standing there, and stopped, and took off his cap, and put it on again, embarrassed, and drove the goats on.

Behind him he heard Seraphina come out of the arch. She did not speak. She came and stood a few feet off, with her arms wrapped round her middle, and for a while they looked at the sea together.

"There's more," she said, at last. "I wasn't going to tell you here."

"Tell me."

"The Synod keeps a commentary on the old texts. Four hundred years of glosses. There's a section on a doctrine called *the grown and the touched.* I read it in the reliquary the first winter, in the cold, copying fragments by the light of a lamp that smoked." Her voice was quite level. "It describes two kinds of Resonant. The *touched,* those changed by contact with the marrow, as a man is changed by fever. And the *grown.* Children said to have been formed inside the stone, not changed by it. The commentary calls them a myth. A cautionary tale for Guild crews, to keep them from digging too deep."

"Grown, not touched," Caelen said.

He had said it himself, in a dry shelter, with a scavenger across the fire. *I was grown. In it. Not touched.* He had had the words and not the meaning. They had come up out of the bottom of him like a bubble from deep water.

"I do not think it was a myth," said Seraphina.

"No."

"I think the Lithoi made them. I think that is what the cradle *is.* Not a hiding place. A womb." She turned to face him, and her eyes were brimming, and she did not look away. "Caelen. You were never a child the stone changed. You are one of them. You are the thing they were making in the dark, while the world above was being taken apart."

---

He did not go to the guest house. He went up.

There was a place above the town where the cliff road bent inland and the grass came down to the edge, a small grassy knoll with a single wind-bent thorn tree, and he lay down under it on his back with his hands open on the turf and watched the sky go from gold to green to the deep, clear blue of a summer night. The first stars came out. The lamps came on along the headland, one by one, and the pulse came up through the ground into his spine, slow and warm, a hundred and nine heartbeats and the sea beneath them.

*They sang to me,* he thought.

It was the first thing. It came very quietly. He lay there with it, in the dark, and let it unfold.

Somebody had laid him down. Somebody, a long time ago, had stood in a gallery in the dark with a child in their arms and sung a single long note, and the stone had grown soft, and they had put him in it. They had known they would not be there when he woke. They had known the cold would come. And they had done the only thing left to them. They had made a cradle out of the world itself, and trusted that someone, someday, would come and sit by it.

For ten years he had been the child who woke in a mine, torn out of the dark by a charge of black powder, thrown into a forest in the rain. He had told himself it was a thing that had been done to him. He had told himself that he did not know why.

*A broken cradle breaks the child.*

He understood, now, what he had been running from. It was not the Guild. It had never been the Guild, or the Wardens, or the Synod, or any of it. It was the dark in his own chest. The coal he banked every hour, the vast patient hunger that waited at the edge of every Shift, the undertow that had taken his name at the weigh-station and nearly taken it for good at the Vault. He had thought it was a predator. He had thought it was a sickness he had caught in the mine.

It was an unfinished waking.

It was the held breath. It had been in him for ten years, the whole of him that had never been let out of the stone, pressing up against the part that had been dragged into the light. It was not his enemy. It was his *self.* It was the half of him that still thought it was in the cradle, listening for a song that had never come.

He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

---

The rage came later, and it was worse than the grief.

It came up in him slowly, in the small hours, with the stars wheeling overhead, and it was cold and clean and entirely without direction. He lay with his teeth set and thought of a blast. A survey crew in the deep strata, a charge set in a wall, a foreman's hand on a lever, a man who had written *recovered asset* in a ledger and gone home to supper. He thought of what had been in that wall. He thought of what had been *broken.*

He thought of all of them. A hundred and nine in the cliff. Seven in the old book, dead in painted rooms. And who knew how many others, in how many seams across the world, taken out of the dark by men with picks and lamps and no idea, and carried up and fed and loved wrongly and watched while they went quiet. The Guild had been blasting cradles for three hundred years. They had been breaking children, and entering them in the loss column under *crew losses, unlicensed.*

His hand closed in the grass. He felt the heat come up behind his sternum and he let it come, just for a moment, because there was no one there to see. The turf under his palm went warm. A thin pale line of scorch ran out from his fingers and died.

*Don't,* he thought. *Not like this.*

It was Varrick's voice, dry and flat, from a long way off. *Boy, the thing you're angry at doesn't care that you are.*

He lay still. He breathed. It took a long time. When he had put the coal back in its bank, the sky over the sea had begun to lighten, and the stars were going out, and in all that vast pale morning there was no sound but the tide.

---

He found that he had been thinking about his name.

It was a small thing. It had no business arriving then, with so much else. But it came, out of the grey light, and sat down beside him.

He had had a name in the dark. He had always had it. It was the one thing that had come up out of the stone with him, the first thing he had known, a small warm shape in the mind that he had turned over and over like a pebble. *Caelen.* He had never asked where it came from. He had assumed, in the way of a child, that one simply had a name, as one had hands.

But nobody has a name by themselves.

Somebody had said it to him. Somebody had said it a great many times, over a long time, in the warm, in the dark, until it stuck. Somebody had held him and said, *this is who you are.*

He turned over on the grass, and put his face against the cold dew, and cried for a long time without making a sound.

---

The girl was awake when he came down.

It was after dawn. The town was stirring, a smoke of breakfast fires rising from the lower lanes, a bell ringing the hour. The white house at the head of the fourth flight had its shutters open. Tamsin lay curled on the floor inside the door under a blanket, asleep with her pencil still in her hand. The wooden horse stood on the sill. And in the middle of the boards, in the pale yellow light, with her legs folded under her and her hands loose in her lap, the child sat looking at the door as though she had been looking at it all night.

He stopped on the threshold. She looked at him. Her eyes went to his face, and then, as they always did, to his chest. But she did not hum. She did not lean. She only looked, and he understood that she had known. That she had been waiting up for him, as he had waited for her. That she knew exactly where he had been all night, and what he had found there, and how long it had taken him to come back.

He crossed the floor and knelt in front of her.

"I'm sorry," he said. His voice cracked. "I don't know how to say it. I didn't know."

She held out her hand.

She laid it flat on his sternum, over the bank, over the place where the coal lay sleeping, and he did not flinch. It was warm. It was very small and very sure. She closed her eyes, and her lips parted, and the hum came up out of her chest and into his.

It was different.

It had been a question, in the beginning. A test, a reading, a thing she had done to find out what he was. Then a word, on the cliff, a low strange note that had gone through him like a hand on the back of the neck. Now it was neither. It was not asking. It was not telling. It went down through his ribs and into the dark behind his breastbone, slow, soft, even, and the coal answered not with heat but with something he had no name for, a loosening, an unclenching, as though a fist that had been closed for ten years had been very gently, very patiently, told that it could open.

*Do not break. Do not hurry.*

He felt it. He felt, for the first time since the mountain, something in him that was not afraid.

She did not finish. She sang for the space of a dozen breaths, and then she stopped, and opened her eyes, and sagged against him, small and limp and exhausted, her forehead against his collarbone. He held her. The sun came across the floor. He sat there with his arms round a child who had sung him the first line of the song his own people had left for him, and he did not move, and he did not speak, and it was the first morning of his life that he was glad to be what he was.

"Later," she said, into his coat, in her cracked hoarse voice. "Not now. Later."

"Yes," he said. "Later."
