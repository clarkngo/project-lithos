# Chapter Six — Forty-One

They chose the boy because he was the easiest to reach, and because nobody could bear to choose any other way.

Malakar had read his clause to the council at dawn, in a flat clear voice, with Reth standing white-faced at the end of the table, and the room had gone through three kinds of silence. Then Hask had said, quite quietly, "Strike it, Chairman, or I will strike you," and Reth had taken a pen from the shipwright's hand and drawn a line through *F-9* in front of eleven witnesses, and signed his name beside it. It had not ended the argument. It had made the argument possible. For the first time in two days, nobody was talking about the Guild.

They were talking about the boy.

"The Long Shelf," Vesh said. He stood at the black oak table with the chart unrolled before him, pointing with a pen. "Pocket nineteen. Tidal access at the lowest ebb, a ledge four feet wide, a gallery cut by the lamp-men a hundred and fifty years ago and never opened. One sleeper. A boy, by the margin mark. He was seen in the stone in the year of the Black Tide and noted as *small, curled, dreaming.*" His thin hand shook, and he steadied it. "He is the nearest. I do not say he is the best. I say that if a mistake is made, the sea is close."

"That is a cold thing to say," said Ibb.

"It is the only warm thing I have."

The vote was six to five. Hask spoke last. He stood with his big hands flat on the table and did not look at anyone, and said, "One. Not two. If the boy dies, the doors are closed for another three hundred years, and no man on this council will raise a hand against it. I will have your word."

They gave it. Caelen gave it with them, because there was no other way to give a promise of that kind. He looked at the girl, who was sitting in the corner on a stool with her wooden horse, listening to every word with her head tilted. She looked back at him. Then she slid off the stool, and walked across the plank floor, and took his hand.

"Go," she said.

---

The waking took nine days.

He would remember them as a single long unbroken day, a stretch of grey light and green water and the endless soft sound of two voices. They went down on the first morning at dead low tide, down a rope ladder to the ledge, six of them in oilskins with lamps: Caelen, the girl, Seraphina, Tamsin, Vesh with a ledger on a board, and the old lamp-keeper Nessa, who had said nothing, but had arrived at the foot of the cliff at dawn with her oil-can and a net-needle and a basket of bread.

The Long Shelf was a slot in the cliff. It was dry on the ledge itself, barely, with a black overhang above and a skirt of weed below and the sea breathing a yard from their boots. Behind them the lamp-men's gallery went back into the rock, narrow and low, hung with old iron brackets. Halfway along, at the height of his knee, the wall was banded with honey stone. And at the end of it, where the gallery closed, there was a niche in the cliff the size of a child's bed.

He was lying in it.

Caelen knelt and held his lamp up, and the light went into the stone like honey into water. He could see the shape of him, a small curled body in a smoothness of amber, not a statue and not a corpse, but a thing that had been put down very gently and told to wait. A thin boy of nine or ten with his knees drawn up and one hand under his cheek. His hair was long. His mouth was a little open. And through the stone, faint and slow and regular, there came a pulse.

"Hello," Caelen said softly. He did not know why. "I'm here. We're here."

He sat down, cross-legged, on the wet rock, at the head of the niche. The girl sat down opposite him with her palms flat on the stone. Seraphina took the foot of it. Nessa set a lamp on a bracket, and trimmed it, and for the first time in his hearing she began, very quietly, to tell the tide.

*Low water. Nine hours until the turn. The wind is south and cold. The sea is calm.*

He closed his eyes. He found the long note.

---

It was the hardest thing he had ever done, and the least like anything he had done before.

It was not a Shift. There was no tearing, no fire, no lengthening of hands. There was a coal in his chest, small and banked, and a long low sound in his throat, and a child asleep in a rock. He sang. He held the note. It was neither high nor low; it was exactly the pitch of the sea beneath them, and he found that he knew it. His body had known it all his life. He let it go out of him slowly, a thread, into the stone, and felt the stone answer.

On the first day, nothing changed. On the second, the honey stone grew a little warmer under his palm. On the third, the pulse in the niche changed its rhythm, from the slow sleeping beat to something quicker and lighter, the heartbeat of a child at the edge of waking, and Seraphina whispered, *there.* On the fourth day, the girl sang the next phrase.

He had not asked her to. He had not known she could. She sat opposite him in her cut-down smock with her eyes closed and her thin hoarse voice rose, a half-step above his, tracing the second line of the song, the one the wall had said was hers. They sang together for six hours. When the tide came in and the ledge went under, they were carried up the ladder with their hands still pressed to the stone, and sang on the clifftop, in the rain, under oilcloth, and in the night when the others slept, because the boy would not wait.

He did not eat. He drank when Tamsin held a flask to his mouth. He slept in snatches, sitting up, against the rock. He felt the cold coming for him the way it always did, creeping up from the marrow, and he held it off with the long note and the small warm bank of the coal, and he thought of nothing but the child.

On the sixth day the stone began to soften.

It was Nessa who saw it first. She had gone quiet the previous evening, and stopped telling the tide, and sat with her back against the rock and her eyes on the amber, and now she said, very low, "Look."

The honey stone around the boy's hand had turned the color of warm wax. It had lost its edge. A faint translucence crept outward from the fingers, spreading, a flush like blood under skin. The surface rippled. Caelen watched it, and felt the pulse under his palm rise, and rise, and hold.

*Do not break. Do not hurry.*

"Steady," Seraphina whispered. "Steady. Don't push it. Let him come."

---

It happened on the ninth morning, at the turn of the tide.

The sea had been rising for an hour. The ledge was awash to their ankles. Nobody had spoken for a long time, and the two voices, the man's and the child's, had fallen together into a single sound, a low rolling chord that filled the gallery and the niche and the slot of grey sky above. Caelen had stopped feeling his hands. He had stopped feeling his knees. He was a note, held in the dark, and he was not afraid.

The stone parted.

It did not crack. It did not break. It opened the way a flower opens, in a long slow unfolding, the amber softening and drawing back from the small body like water from a stone, until the boy lay in a hollow of warm honey-colored glass with nothing between him and the air. He was thin. He was naked. His skin was the color of tea. His eyelids fluttered.

He drew breath.

It was a ragged, shuddering, astonished gulp, like a swimmer coming up from the bottom of the sea. His chest heaved. His eyes opened, enormous and dark and gold-flecked, and went from the lamp to the rock to the sea, and came to rest on the face above him.

"It's all right," Caelen said. His voice was a ruin. "It's all right. I'm here. Don't — "

The boy screamed.

It was the scream of a child who does not know where he is. It rang off the cliff. His whole body arched and twisted, and the honey bands in the gallery wall flared white, and Caelen felt the heat come, the dreadful old heat, the coal about to break its bank, and he thought, *no, not now, not like this* — and a small hand closed on his sleeve.

The girl had not let go of the song.

She was kneeling at the boy's head with her palms on either side of his face, humming, in the high thin voice that had learned to hold his note. It was not the long note. It was a new one, a little soft rising phrase, three tones and a fall. The boy stopped screaming. His eyes went to her face, huge and wet and bewildered. She hummed it again. And he, with a shaking, uncertain, astonished mouth, hummed it back.

Nessa had put her hand over her face. Vesh was weeping openly, without noise, the way a man weeps who has not done it in thirty years. Tamsin was on her knees in the water, saying something over and over that was not a word.

Caelen did not move. He sat on the wet rock, with the sea coming in round him, and let the small warm bank of the coal settle quietly back into itself. It had not broken. It had not so much as flickered. He was cold, and spent, and utterly empty, and across the narrow gallery two children were humming at each other in a language older than any harbor, and one of them was alive who had been asleep since before the Culling.

*He's fine,* he thought. *He's just fine.*

It was the first time in all the years he could remember that the song had simply worked.
