# Chapter Two — What Hask Fears

They locked Reth in the tide-house guest room, in the end, and it was the worst thing they could have done.

It was meant as a courtesy. The Reeve had said as much to the council with his hand on the black oak table: *The man put himself in our hands. We will not put him in the cells.* So Reth was given a room at the end of the long upper corridor, with a bed and a basin and a window overlooking the quay, and a clerk's stool outside the door, and a boy of fourteen with a boathook to sit on it. He thanked them. He asked for a pen. He sat at the window with his satchel and his small leather book and wrote, in a clear unhurried hand, for the rest of the afternoon, and every person in the harbor who passed beneath the window looked up at him, and looked away.

"It's like keeping a lit match in the hay," Malakar said.

"It's like keeping a preacher in a tavern," said Rook, "if the preacher were right."

They were standing in the corridor, the two of them and Caelen, at the foot of the stair that led up to the council chamber, where the voices had been going for three hours behind the door. It had begun quietly. It had not stayed quiet. Every few minutes a sentence broke out of the room, loud and ragged, and was swallowed again. He heard Vesh's thin exact voice, and Ibb's old dry one, and once, very loud, the Reeve's, saying *no.*

"You'd think they'd have decided by now," said Rook.

"They decided three hundred years ago," Malakar said. "What they're doing is finding out what they decided."

---

They were called in at the fourth hour. Caelen went alone.

The chamber had changed. The shutters were open, and the evening light came in slantwise and gold across the plank floor and the black table, and the eleven chairs in their horseshoe had been pushed back, as though the men and women in them had stood up and sat down again too many times. The lamp-keepers had gone. There were only the council left, and the clerks along the wall, and the Brine-Reeve in the middle, in the big chair, with his heavy hands flat on the wood. Vesh sat at the left end with his spectacles in his fist. Ibb sat beside him, bolt upright, with his hands in his sleeves. Tamsin stood at the back, by the door, in her blue sash, with ink on her cheek and a face as white as paper.

"Mister Caelen," Hask said. "You will forgive the state of us."

"I'd like to help if I can."

"You may. I will tell you what has happened, and you will tell me if I misstate it." He took a long breath. "The council is divided. Four are for the Reeve's view, that the Guild must be refused and the pockets sealed again for another three hundred years, with the lamps kept, and the world told nothing. Four are for the Harbor-Master's, that the sleepers must be woken, carefully, and soon, by whatever means can be found, before the Guild finds its own. Two are for the Superior's, that the sleepers are sacred, and must be brought to the Honey Chapel, and tended there, in ceremony, until the tide-priests can decide. And one," his eyes went, without malice, to the youngest of the council, a stout red-faced shipwright in a leather apron, "wants to hear what the Guild will pay."

"I did not say pay," said the shipwright, with dignity. "I said *offer.*"

"That is eleven." Hask closed his hands. "It is not an argument. It is a drowning."

---

They put the questions to him one at a time, and he answered them as honestly as he could.

Ibb asked whether the sleepers felt pain. Caelen said he did not know, that the ones he had touched seemed to dream. Ibb asked whether he believed they were holy. Caelen said that he did not believe in holy, but that he had no word for what they were except *people.* The shipwright asked what the Guild's offer might be worth, and Caelen said he had no idea, and that he had been offered money by the Guild once, in a spring, through a woman called Kell, and had kept the brass token for two years and never spent it. Someone laughed. The sound did not last.

It was Vesh who asked the question. He did it quietly, at the end, with his eyes on the table.

"You were woken," he said. "As they would be. Will you tell the council how it was?"

The chamber was very still. Caelen looked at the Harbor-Master's thin shaking hands, and at the Reeve's stone face, and at the little knot of clerks by the door; and he understood that this was the question the council had been circling for four hours without saying it aloud. They had been arguing about what to do with a hundred and nine. They had not asked what it was to be one.

"A blast woke me," he said.

His voice came out steady. He had not been sure that it would.

"I was in a seam in a Guild mine, in the deep strata, sealed in marrow-glass. The Guild set a charge. It broke the stone. I remember heat, and sound, and being thrown out of the dark into a place with no walls. I couldn't stand. I couldn't breathe. My body had been one shape for as long as I could remember, and I'd been torn out of it. I ran. I don't know how. There was water coming in, and men screaming, and I got to the top, and I was alone in a forest in the rain." He stopped. "It was the worst thing I've ever felt. I didn't know the word for it. I've been afraid every day since that it wasn't over."

"And yet you lived," Ibb said.

"A man found me. He fed me. He didn't ask me for anything." Caelen met the old clerk's eyes. "I think that's all it was. I think anyone would have lived, if someone had sat with them. The seven who didn't — "

He stopped. He had not meant to say it. He saw Vesh's head come up.

"Seraphina's found something," he said. "She's still working it out. I'd rather she told you herself. But I'll say this much. I don't think waking kills. I think waking *alone* does."

---

The council broke on that.

It did not break cleanly. It broke the way a wave breaks on a shelf of rock, in noise and spray and a sudden fierce confusion, and for a little while Caelen stood in the middle of the floor and let it go over him. Ibb was on his feet, saying that the sleepers were not Caelen's to speak for. The shipwright was saying, *if there is a way, if there is truly a way* — Vesh was saying, in a thin cracked unfamiliar voice, *a hundred and nine, a hundred and nine, I have a hundred and nine in the margin* — and over all of it came the Reeve, who had not moved, who had not raised his voice, who struck his open hand once on the black oak table with a sound like a gunshot.

"Enough."

It stopped.

"I will say what I fear." The deep slow voice came flat and heavy into the silence. "I would like it written down, Harbor-Master. I would like the whole council to hear it. Then you may vote how you like."

He stood up. He was a very big man. He put his hands behind his back and looked, not at the council, but at the open window, at the long gold evening and the cliff beyond it with its small lit lamps.

"My father told me, on the last night he lived, that we were the keepers of a door. I have thought about that every day for twenty-six years, and I have never been sure what he meant. I think I know now." A pause. "I do not fear the Guild. I fear what it will bring. I fear that a grey ship and a good man and a list of names will be the end of a promise that three hundred years of my people have kept in the dark, with their own hands, for nothing. *We'll not wake what we can't keep.* That was the vow. Not *we will never wake.* We *can't keep them.* Do you understand? Not one of us has the faintest idea how."

"The strata boy — "

"The strata boy lived because he was lucky." The Reeve's eyes did not move. "I do not say it to wound him. He knows. He said it in this room. A man found him in a forest and fed him, and it was one chance in a thousand. And I have a hundred and nine." He swallowed. "If we open the doors, and the children come out, and they die, as seven have died, I will have done it. I will have killed them with my own hand, by lamplight, in the name of mercy. If we leave them where they are, they sleep. They are warm. They are dreaming. Nobody has ever been able to tell me what is wrong with that."

Nobody answered. Outside, the gulls wheeled over the quay.

"It is not the Guild I fear," Hask said, very quietly. "It is that I might be wrong."

---

They did not vote. There was no way to do it. When the light began to go the Reeve dismissed the council with a gesture and sat down heavily in the big chair, a tired old man with his hands hanging between his knees, and the clerks went out one by one.

Tamsin did not move. She stood by the door with her back against the wall, and when the room had emptied she came forward, slowly, across the plank floor, and stopped in front of the table.

"Reeve."

"Clerk."

"May I say something?"

"You have been wishing to for four hours." He did not look up. "You may."

"Nobody asked her."

He lifted his head.

"All day," Tamsin said. Her voice shook, and she let it. "All day, you've been deciding what to do with a hundred and nine. The Superior wants to put them in the chapel. The Harbor-Master wants to wake them. You want to keep them asleep. The Guild wants to count them. And one of them is awake. She's been awake four months. She's sitting in a room above this one with a wooden horse and a name that she gave herself, in front of the whole harbor, and not one person on this council has asked her what she wants." She drew a breath. "She's got words. She's got maybe forty. She can say *no.* She said it today, when the chain went up. I heard her."

Hask looked at her. The stone had gone out of his face. What was underneath it looked, for the first time, old and bewildered and ashamed.

"She is nine years old," he said.

"So was he," said Caelen, behind her.

The Reeve closed his eyes.

"I do not know how to ask her," he said.

"I do," said Caelen.
