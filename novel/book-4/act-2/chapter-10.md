# Chapter Ten — The Third Pocket

For a long time there was no sound at all.

He lay on his back in the mud with the rain falling into his open eyes and knew only that it was falling. He could see it. It came out of a sky the color of slate in long silver threads, each one separate, each one slow. He lifted a hand and it moved through the air as if through water. There was a high clean ringing in his skull like the note of a struck glass, and beneath it, very far off, an enormous silence, as though the world had been taken away and a picture of the world left in its place.

*Count,* he thought. It was Varrick's voice. *Count what you've got.*

He had hands. He had both. He had a body, cold and aching and wet, that obeyed him. He rolled over and got up on his elbows and the turf slid under them, and the ringing flooded back in, and with it sound: the scream of the wind, the thunder of the sea, a woman's voice shouting a name over and over. The cliff-edge was gone.

It had not gone far. A slab of turf the size of a wagon had slid forty feet down the slope and hung there, grey and raw and trailing roots. Where the boulder had stood, there was a crater. The air above it smelled of burnt wax and wet stone. Small stones were still falling, ticking down the rock, and the lamp that Tamsin had carried lay on its side in the grass with its flame streaming sideways like a banner.

"Girl," he said. He could not hear himself. "*Girl.*"

She was beside him, on her face in the mud. He turned her over with shaking hands. Her eyes were closed. Her mouth was open. A thread of blood ran from her nose, and she was breathing, fast and shallow, and when he laid his palm on her chest he felt, under the thin wet cloth, a heart going like a bird's.

Her lips moved. She was still humming.

He put his forehead against hers and wept.

---

Rook was on his hands and knees by the crater, with his head hanging and blood running from his left ear in a bright steady stream down his neck. He did not look up when Caelen shouted. He did not look up when Malakar, bent double a few yards off with one arm round the Engineer-Captain's neck, lifted a hand and waved. He knelt there swaying, staring at the earth between his palms, and when Caelen reached him and gripped his shoulder he turned his head, slowly, with a puzzled expression, like a man who has heard his name called from a long way off.

"I can't," he said, loudly. "Caelen. I can't hear the — it's all on one side. It's all gone on this side."

"I know. It's all right."

"It's not *right.* There's no — " He broke off. He put his hand to the ear and brought it away red and looked at it. "Oh," he said, quite calmly. "That's blood."

"Sit down."

"I'm fine. I'm fine, I'm — go on. Go and look." He shoved at Caelen with the flat of his hand. "I'm *fine,* damn you, go!"

Corwin was on his knees at the lip of the crater, his good grey coat in rags, with his left arm clasped to his chest and his face the color of paper. Beside him was a block of stone as big as a hearth. It had come off the boulder in the blast and fallen across the mouth of the gallery that led down to the niche, and Corwin, who had not been a brave man all his life, who had said so, laughingly, in a tall thin house in the Spire, had put his arm under it. He had held it, for one slow terrible second, until the others got clear. Something had broken. The wrist was bent where no wrist should bend. He was smiling, in a ghastly sort of way, and shaking.

"It's nothing," he said, thinly. "It's a — I think it's just a — don't *look* at it."

"Corwin."

"There are two in there." His voice rose. "Caelen. There are two that are fine, I could see them, the glass is cracked but they're — and there was a third. There were *three,* and one's — " He stopped, and swallowed, and pointed with his chin toward the edge. "It was thrown."

---

He found the third pocket by the sound.

It was below the cliff. The slope fell away in steps of black rock to a narrow shelf awash with spray, and beyond the shelf, at the foot of the headland, a cave opened in the stone: a sea-cave, a half-drowned hollow with the surf boiling white at its mouth. The blast had thrown the third sleeper out of the niche and down the slope, and he lay in the wash of the waves with his back across a rock, a small naked thing in the froth, and he was making a noise.

It was not a scream. It was thinner than that. It was a thin high unbroken wail, on one note, without breath or pause, like wind in a keyhole. The honey stone that had held him lay round him in shards. His eyes were open. They were enormous, and gold, and utterly without sense. His small hands clawed at the rock. His whole body was jerking, in great shuddering convulsions, in rhythm with the sea.

*That's me,* Caelen thought. *That's me, in the mine.*

He went down the slope on his heels and his hands, sliding, tearing his palms on the rock, and reached the shelf, and the sea took him to the knees. It was freezing. It beat at him like a fist. He dropped down in the water beside the child and did what he had done all his life: he put his back to the storm, and took the thing in his arms.

The boy bucked and clawed. A nail raked his cheek. He held on, and said in a voice that he could not hear, *I have you. Hold. I have you.*

It did no good.

The child was beyond words. He was beyond anything but pain. He was drowning in the first moment of his life, in the dark, in the roar, alone, and the thing in Caelen's chest, the small banked coal, the held breath, began to answer, because it knew exactly what this was. It was rising. He felt it come up through his ribs. He felt the old undertow reach for him, vast and patient, and for the first time in his life he did not fight it, because he knew now what it was. It was the other half of him. It was the part that had never been let out.

*A broken cradle breaks the child,* he thought. *Then be the cradle.*

He opened his mouth and sang.

---

It took everything.

He had thought he knew what that meant. He had spent nine days on a ledge, with a note in his throat and a bank of coals in his chest, and he had believed it was the limit. It was nothing. It was a rehearsal. In the freezing froth of the sea-cave, with the boy screaming in his arms and the storm tearing at his hair, he let go of every hold he had ever kept on himself, the banking and the rationing and the long cold discipline of ten years, and poured it out.

He gave the child his warmth. He gave him his weight, his hands, his arms, his name. He gave him the coal, all of it, the whole vast patient hunger that had stood at the edge of every Shift in his life, and it went from him in a long bright rush like water out of a broken jar. He felt it go. He felt the cold come in behind it, a cold such as he had never known, the cold of an empty house.

*Hold,* he sang. *Hold. I'm here. Hold.*

And across the water, from the ledge above, thin and clear and unbearably steady, a second voice came down to meet his.

She had followed. She stood on the lip of the slope in the screaming wind, with the blood still on her upper lip and her small drenched shape black against the lightning, and her voice came down out of the dark and joined his, a half-step above, and together they made the chord. The child in his arms went rigid. His wail faltered. It wavered on its single note.

And then, one by one, along the cliff, the lights came.

---

They came down the headland in the dark with the storm at their backs: Nessa, with her oil-can and her net-needle; and her people behind her, the lamp families, nine of them, and then more, the net-menders and fishwives and boys with goats, a long straggling line of small lights on the black cliff, each one a tin-hooded lantern held up against the wind. They came down the slope without speaking. They stood in a ring on the shelf, round the child and the man and the sea, with the surf breaking at their boots. And in her cracked old voice, Nessa began to tell the tide.

*Low water in two hours. The wind is south and strong. The sea is high. It will not come above this stone.*

It was the oldest thing in the harbor. It was what a lamp-keeper said to a sleeper in the dark so that the dark would not be empty. One by one the others took it up. A man's voice. A woman's. The chant went round the ring, soft and low and ragged, under the howl of the gale: *the wind is south, the sea is calm, the lamp is lit, you are not alone.*

The boy stopped screaming.

It did not happen all at once. It came by degrees, in a slow subsiding, like a pot taken off a fire. His eyes found the lanterns. They found the faces. They found, last, the small girl standing on the rock above him, humming, with her hands out. His mouth worked. A sound came out of it, the very first sound of its kind, three tones and a fall.

And at the edge of the ring, in his salt-stiff coat, with his hat in his hand and the rain running down his face, the Brine-Reeve of the third harbor went down on his knees in the sea.

"Give him to me," said Hask. "Give him here."

Caelen did not hear him. He had stopped hearing. The cold had reached his heart, and the dark behind it was soft, and in the very last instant before it took him he felt something go in his chest, quietly, like a latch lifting, like a held breath let out at last. It did not hurt. It was only a great stillness, and a great release. And as he went down into it with a child's hand in his, he thought, with perfect clarity, *oh. That's what it was for.*
