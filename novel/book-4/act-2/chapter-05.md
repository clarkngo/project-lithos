# Chapter Five — The Case File of the Sleeping

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Malakar. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**MALAKAR**

Reth had given him the satchel himself, and that was what made Malakar read it twice.

"All of it," the Chairman had said, in the corridor of the tide-house, with the boy and the boathook watching from the clerk's stool. "The survey, the appendices, the Board's charter, the correspondence. I would like someone to read it who reads as you do. I have never been able to see what is in a document that I wrote myself." A faint dry smile. "You were an Inquisitor. I am told you could find a flaw in a prayer."

"I could find a flaw in an apology," said Malakar. "It is a different skill."

He took the satchel. He carried it back to the guest house through the dusk, past the fish-wharf and the shuttered net-lofts and the knots of men in salt-stiff coats who stopped talking as he went by, and set it on the table in the room he shared with Rook, and lit the lamp. Rook was asleep on the floor with his coat over his face. Malakar sat down in the one chair, drew out the first sheaf, and began, as he had begun some ten thousand times in the cold north room at Kesh Hollow, with the page numbers.

They were out of order. That was the first thing. A survey of this kind should have been paginated straight through, front to back, in a single clerk's hand. This one had been bound in five different sections, each numbered separately, with a title sheet, two cover letters, and a table of contents that listed an *Appendix F* and gave no page. He turned back. He turned forward. He found, at the back of the fourth section, tucked inside a flap of grey card, a single bundle of eleven sheets with no number on any of them.

*Appendix F,* said the top one. *Recovery Estimate, Charter 9. For Board circulation only.*

He read it slowly. He read it with his pen in his hand, as he read every document, and by the third page he had put a small cross in the margin, which was his old mark for *this sentence is lying.*

---

It was a very polite document.

That was the horror of it, and he saw it clearly, sitting in the lamplight with the tide breathing under the floor. There was not one cruel word in it. It was written in the plain, mild, grey language of the Guild's best clerks, the language of tariffs and weights, and it said, in effect, that the Board wished to be of service. It had read the Hall of Canon's finding. It approved of the finding. It proposed, in a spirit of cooperation, to *recover* the persons identified in Survey Section Three, *wherever such recovery may be effected safely, in conformity with the rite recognized by the hall,* at the Guild's expense.

It listed them. It numbered them, with the Ledger's own names, from the Weeping Stair to the Old Mouth. It assigned each pocket a *recovery class,* A through D, according to depth, tidal access, and estimated *occupancy.* It budgeted the cost of the waking in hours of labor and fathoms of rope. It budgeted the cost of *stabilization,* and of *custodial placement,* and of a *transitional household* for each recovered person, with a nurse and a tutor and a physician, in a house on the quay. It was, he thought, almost tender. It might have been written by a good man who had spent his life watching children fall through a floor.

On page six, in a sub-clause of a paragraph headed *Legal Basis,* it said:

*F-9. Persons recovered under this Charter shall be entered as wards of the Office of Custody (Guild Division) for the duration of their minority, with all rights and obligations of guardianship, including provision for their maintenance, instruction, and the disposition of remains.*

Malakar put down his pen.

He read the sentence again. He read it aloud, under his breath, to hear the grammar, because he had learned long ago that a lie does not live in the nouns. It lives in the verbs, and in who has been left out of them. *Shall be entered.* By whom? *Wards,* of the Guild. *For the duration of their minority.* Which was defined, in a footnote, as *until the ward attains a licensed Resonant status, as determined by the Office.* And at the end, in four words, as quiet and as smooth as a stone in a pocket: *the disposition of remains.*

He sat back.

*Remains.* A ward of the Guild, raised in a house on the quay with a nurse and a tutor, fed and taught and tenderly loved, who died: what became of the body? A child of the stone. A child of marrow. He thought of Aethel-Cores, cut and graded and sold by the ounce to men with more ambition than sense. He thought of the Culling. He thought of how very well-made it was, this clause, how perfectly it fitted the world the finding had made: a world in which the Guild was a *partner,* a funder of mercy, an honest friend to the drifting. It had taken the language of the hall and turned it, quietly, like a key in a lock.

*It is not a hunt,* he thought. *It is an adoption.*

And on the last sheet of the bundle, in a clerk's tidy pencil, the figure he had been dreading, as a man dreads the second shoe.

*Estimated residual marrow yield, per ward, lifetime: 3.8 stone.*

---

He did not wake Rook. He did not wake anyone.

He put the bundle in his coat, and took his hat, and went out into the night, and walked, without hurry, along the quay and up the stair to the tide-house. The boy with the boathook was asleep on his stool. Malakar stepped over his feet and knocked, twice, on the door at the end of the corridor.

"Come."

Reth was sitting by the window in his shirtsleeves with the shutters open and a candle on the sill. He had not been writing. His hands lay open on his knees. When he saw who it was, he did not rise, and he did not ask, and Malakar understood that he had been sitting there waiting for him since the satchel left his hands.

"You have read Appendix F."

"I have read Appendix F."

"What do you think of it?"

Malakar crossed the room and laid the bundle on the sill beside the candle. He opened it to page six. He put one finger on the sub-clause and did not take it away.

"I think it was written by a man who admired the finding," he said. "I think it was written by a man who wished the recovered children well. I think he was extremely careful. There is not one unkind word in this document, Chairman, and I have been reading unkind documents for twenty years." He lifted his eyes. "I should like to know if you have read this."

Reth said nothing.

"The wording," Malakar said. "The *disposition of remains.*"

"No."

It was so quiet that he nearly missed it.

"I have not read it." The small grey man looked at the page, and then, with a visible effort, at Malakar. "I read the survey. I read the recommendations. I signed the Charter in February, and the appendices were bound separately, as the Board's practice is, and circulated to the clerks of works. I did not read them. I never read the appendices." His voice cracked, a very small crack, like frost in a cup. "I do not know who wrote this. I could find out. I think I know already."

"You signed it."

"I signed it." He took the page. He read the sub-clause once, slowly, with his lips moving, and Malakar watched the color go out of his face from the forehead down. "*Disposition of remains.*" A long pause. "Forty years, Malakar. I have spent forty years learning the exact weight of that phrase. I have entered it in ledgers. I have *written* it, in that column, in my own hand. And I put my name on a charter that used it as a clause."

"Not knowing."

"It is the same."

"Not quite."

"It is the same to the dead." He laid the page on the sill. His hand was not steady. "I did not write this. I did not intend it. I would not have permitted it for the world. And it has my seal at the bottom of the packet, and a hundred and nine children on the other side of that window who are, tonight, in law, a line in a Board appendix." He looked up. "What do you propose to do with it?"

---

It was a question Malakar had asked himself in the corridor, and on the stair, and at the quay's edge. He had asked it with the cold clear habit of an Inquisitor, and he had not liked the answer.

He could use it. He could carry the bundle up to the council chamber in the morning, lay it on the black oak table, and read F-9 aloud to eleven frightened people who already half believed it. The Guild would be finished on this coast. Reth would be finished, a man who had given forty years to a column of the unattended and would be remembered as the architect of a trap. It would be the strongest thing he had ever held. He had held such things in the Warden order. He knew exactly what they were for.

He thought of a ruin on a cart road, and a captain who had come alone, and had asked for three names.

"I propose to read it to the council myself," he said. "In the morning. With your leave, or without it."

"With it."

"You will be asked to answer for it."

"I know." The small man straightened. "I should like to. I have not been able to answer for anything since I was twenty-six." He took up the candle, and the light moved across his face, which was old and tired and, for the first time since Malakar had known him, entirely without defense. "Will you do something for me? Read it as it is. Do not make it worse. I should hate to be judged for a wickedness I had not the wit to commit."

Malakar looked at him for a long moment.

"I have never read a document otherwise," he said.

He took the bundle off the sill and went out into the corridor, past the sleeping boy, and down the dark stair. In the street the tide was coming in, a long grey hush under the quay, and above him on the cliff the little lamps burned in their niches like a string of patient eyes. He stood a moment, with the pages against his chest.

He had spent twenty years of his life teaching himself to see the thing that language was built to hide. He had thought of it as a vice, and then as a weapon, and then, lately, as neither. It was only a kind of attention. It was what you gave a document you did not intend to let lie.

*It is an adoption,* he thought again. *Not a hunt.*

And he went back to the lamp, and sat down, and began to write out what he would say.
