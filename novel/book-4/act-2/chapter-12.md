# Chapter Twelve — The Salt Coast, Wider

Seraphina asked him to try on the sixth day, and he already knew what would happen.

They did it in the infirmary, behind a closed door, with the afternoon light coming in across the boards and the sea breathing under the windows. She had not wanted to ask. He could see that. She sat in the chair opposite him with her hands folded very tightly in her lap and said, in the careful voice she used for things that were going to hurt, "I think you should find out. Whatever it is. I would rather you knew, with me here, than discovered it in the middle of a fight."

"I know already."

"Then show me."

He held out his right hand, palm up, and closed his eyes, and reached for the place behind his sternum where the bank had lain for ten years. He knew the way. He had known it in his sleep. It was a door in the dark, and beyond it the coal, a small hot weight, banked and watchful, the vast patient hunger that had stood at the edge of every Shift he had ever made. He walked toward it as he had walked toward it a thousand times, and put his hand on the latch.

There was no latch.

There was nothing. There was a quiet, warm, empty room with sunlight in it, and the faint smell of salt. He stood in it a long while. He felt for the old weight, the pressure, the cold. He felt for the Drift, and the hunger, and the long slow pull of the dark. He found only himself, a man with ordinary hands, breathing in a chair.

He opened his eyes. The hand lay palm up on his knee, brown and scarred and entirely human. The nails were short. They did not thicken. The fingers did not lengthen. The air above them did not warm.

"I see," said Seraphina, very softly.

"It's gone."

"Not gone." She leaned forward. Her eyes were wet. "Finished. I think that is the word. The held breath was the coal. They were one thing. You gave all of it to that child in the surf, and the waking completed, and there was nothing left to hold." She took a breath. "You will not Shift again, Caelen. I do not think you can. You are not drifting, and you will never drift. You are not hungry in the old way. You are not in danger of yourself. You are only — "

"Woken."

"Yes."

He sat with that. It was a great relief. It was the greatest relief of his life, and it frightened him more than anything that had ever happened to him, because he had spent ten years believing that he was a weapon with a safety catch, and he had been, and it was gone. He thought of a forest. He thought of a weigh-station, a flooded cellar, a Vault. He thought of a man in a fallen tree saying *whatever in the nine hells you are.* He thought: *I do not know what I am for.*

"It is not a loss," Seraphina said, as if she had heard. "It is only a change of shape."

"I know."

"Do you?"

He looked at his hand, for a long time. Then he laughed, short and cracked, and put it over his eyes.

"No," he said. "But I'm going to find out."

---

The cold did not come back.

It was the oddest thing about the next weeks, and the one he never quite got used to. He ate when he was hungry, and stopped when he was not. He slept through the night and woke at dawn. He could stand in a wind without bracing. He caught himself, once, walking across the quay in the early light with nothing in his pockets, no strip of dried meat, no ration, and stopped dead in the middle of the stones, and had to stand there a minute until the feeling went.

He could not feel the cliff.

That was harder. He went out to the headland on the ninth evening, alone, and lay on his stomach at the edge as he had on the first day, and put his palm flat on the gravel. He felt cold stone and wet grass. He felt the wind. He felt the sea, a great distant weight, and the long slow patience of the headland in the dusk, and nothing in it that spoke. The pulse was gone. The hundred and six that still slept, in their niches, under their little lamps, had not stopped. They were there. They were warm, he knew it, as one knows a house is lived in. But he could no longer hear them.

He lay there until it was dark, with his hand on the stone, and cried a little, not for himself, quite.

And then, from somewhere behind him, at the end of the road, a small voice began to hum.

---

She was on the harbor wall.

He found her there at dusk, the next evening, when the lamps were coming on along the quay and the water was the color of tarnished silver. She sat at the very end, where the breakwater curved out into the roads, with her legs hanging over the drop and the wooden horse in her lap, looking at the sea. She had grown. He saw it in the pale light. She was taller than she had been in June. Her hair had come in thick and dark, with a gleam of gold in it, and the cut-down smock had been replaced by a real dress, a blue one, that Tamsin had sewn.

She did not turn when he came. She went on humming.

It was her name. He knew it before he was near enough to hear the shape. It was that long low curve that climbed in the middle and bent over to a soft peak, the one she had sung from the threshold of the white house with the whole cliff answering. She sang it quietly, to nobody, to the sea. The tide ran under the wall. The lights of the *Tally* hung at anchor beyond the boom, and behind them, a long way off, a few late gulls turned over the green dark.

He sat down beside her.

She looked at him. Her eyes were gold in the lamplight. She regarded his chest, from habit, with her head a little tilted, and then his face, and a small frown came between her brows.

"Quiet," she said. It was one of her new words. She had perhaps three hundred now.

"Yes."

"Gone?"

"Yes." He swallowed. "But I'm all right. I'm — " He found he did not have the word, and tried another. "I'm *here.*"

She considered it, gravely. Then she put out her small brown hand and laid it flat on his sternum, where the coal had been, and held it there, and he waited for the hum. It did not come. She sat very still, with her palm on his chest, listening. And then her face cleared, as if she had heard something he could not, and she took her hand away and folded it in her lap and nodded once, with great satisfaction.

"Good," she said.

---

He had thought about what to say for twenty-two days at sea, and for a hundred more on the stair, and he found, when it came to it, that he did not need any of it.

The light was going. The sea lay quiet. Behind them, up the long black stair of the town, the windows of the white houses were coming on one by one like a held breath let out. Somewhere up there a boy was singing the Reeve's name. Somewhere a woman was telling the tide. Somewhere Seraphina was sitting with two sleepers in a cracked glass, and Rook was laughing at something from the wrong side of his head, and a man with a mended cuff was writing a letter to his sister.

He thought of a forest. He thought of a spider rebuilding its web in the rain. He thought of a man's unhurried boots going away through the green, and the fact that they had not once slowed, or turned, to see.

"I want to tell you something," he said.

She looked at him.

"A man found me, once. A long time ago. I was in a tree, and I was hungry, and I was very afraid. He gave me meat. He said, *come down.* He never looked back to see whether I was following." The words came slowly. "For nine years he told me it was arithmetic. It wasn't. It was the other thing. He just didn't have the word. I didn't either, until I met you."

She said nothing. She had folded her hands in her lap.

"You were never a debt," he said. "I want you to know. You were never anything I was owed, or that I owed, or that anybody did anything for because of what you are. You were just *you.* I'd have crossed the sea for nothing. I'd have sat on that floor a year." His voice cracked. "I don't know what you are. I don't know what I am. I don't know what happens now. But I'm glad you came down. I'm glad you ate."

She was quiet for a long time. Then she leaned her head against his arm, with a weight like a bird's, and said, in her hoarse, cracked, careful voice:

"Stay."

"Yes," said Caelen. "I'm staying."

---

The letter came at dusk the next day, carried down the quay by Tam, who stopped, and bowed, and said that the Chairman had asked him to give it into Mister Caelen's own hand.

It was a single sheet, folded small. It was in Reth's clear dry script.

*Halse has given me his survey notes. I had not asked. He sat in the cells for ten days and wrote them out, because he said he could not bear to keep them. I am sending them to Kell in the Spire, and a copy to the Hall of Canon. I thought you should know first.*

*The party that surveyed the third harbor in the spring was one of six. The Board's Charter 9 ran along the whole of the western seaboard. There are other coasts. Halse's notes name four hundred and eleven seams with a periodic pulse, from the Salt Coast to the southern capes. He does not know how many are occupied.*

*I do not think it is a number any of us will be able to enter in a ledger.*

*— R.*

He read it twice. Then he folded it along its crease and put it in his coat, beside a brass token he had never spent, and looked up at the cliff.

It was dark. The lamps were burning. All along the headland, in their tin hoods, forty-one small gold flames burned steadily in the dusk like a string of patient eyes, and under them, in the stone, a hundred and six hearts beat on. Some of them would wake in the spring. Some in the summer after. Some, perhaps, would have to wait for a hand that had not yet been born.

The girl was at his side. She had followed him down the quay, quietly, as she followed him everywhere now, and stood with her wooden horse under her arm, looking up at the cliff. He looked down at her. Her face in the lamplight was serious, and open, and entirely unafraid.

She hummed a note.

It was not her name. It was the first line of the long song, the one on the wall. And from the dark headland, very faintly, from a hundred and six places at once, the cliff hummed back.

He could not hear it. He knew that. But he saw her smile.

"All right," said Caelen, and took her hand. "Then let's go and tell them."
