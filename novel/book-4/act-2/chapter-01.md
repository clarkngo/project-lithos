# Chapter One — The Grey Sail

The harbor had never needed walls, and by noon it had found out how to build them.

It began with the chain. There was a boom across the mouth of the third harbor, an iron chain as thick as a man's thigh, laid on the bottom since before the oldest volume of the Ledger and raised only for storms. Hask gave the order from the quay without looking round, and a dozen men leaned on a capstan older than anyone's grandfather. The chain came up out of the green water in long dripping links and stretched taut between the headland and the breakwater, black and wet, with weed trailing from it. The *Tally* hove to a cable's length outside and let go her anchor with a sound like a door closing.

After that, nobody needed to give any more orders.

The women went home. They went quickly and without speaking, gathering their children against their skirts, and the doors of the lower town closed one by one down the length of the quay. The men stayed. They came out of the net-lofts and the boat-sheds and the fish-houses with whatever they had in their hands, boathooks, gaff poles, a whale-lance with a rusted blade, and stood along the stone edge of the harbor in a loose silent line, facing the sea. Nobody raised a weapon. They held them the way they held their nets, low and ready, and watched a grey ship ride at anchor in the sun.

"I've never seen a town hold its breath with a harpoon," Rook said.

"They've had practice," said Caelen.

He stood at the head of the third flight with the others, looking down. The girl was behind him in the white house, with Tamsin, with the door barred and the shutters closed and a bowl of cherries between them. She had heard the chain. She had made a sound when she heard it, a low rough hum with a rasp on the end, and then she had gone to the window and put both palms flat on the sill and said *no,* very clearly, to nobody.

He had not been able to tell her it would be all right. He had stopped telling her things he did not know.

---

A skiff put out from the *Tally* at the turn of the tide.

It carried two men. One rowed, a sailor in a grey jersey with his eyes on the oars. The other sat in the stern, very upright, in a plain grey coat, with a flat leather satchel across his knees. He did not wave or hold up a flag. He sat with his hands on the satchel and looked at the cliff, and the little boat came across the green water, slowly, toward the boom, and stopped.

"He wants to come through," Malakar said.

"Let him wait," said Hask.

The Reeve had come down to the quay-head and stood at the edge of the stone with his arms folded and the wind in his coat. He did not shout. He did not need to. The sound of his deep slow voice carried across the water as though he had spoken into a room. "Who are you, and what do you carry?"

"Reth, of the Guild's Board." The reply came back thin and clear. "Chairman, for what it is worth. I carry nothing but a satchel and a clerk, who is not in this boat. I have no instruments. I ask the harbor's leave to land, and the council's leave to speak, and I will go back to the ship afterward whatever is decided. I will not ask for the girl. I will not ask for the lamps. I have come to read you a list."

There was a long pause.

"A list," said Hask.

"Forty-one names. I think the council should hear them."

Nobody moved. Along the quay the line of men stood still as posts, with the lances in their hands. Caelen saw Hask's shoulders rise, and fall.

"Lower the chain," the Reeve said, at last, to nobody. "A fathom. No more."

---

They gave him the council hall, because there was nowhere else big enough, and then locked the doors behind him.

It was a long bare room at the top of the second stair, with a plank floor, a table of black oak, and eleven chairs in a horseshoe, much smaller than the Hall of Canon in the Spire and, to Caelen's eye, a good deal more frightening. The windows were shuttered. There were lamps. The council sat in the horseshoe in their salt-stiff coats, and Hask in the middle, and behind them, standing, in a double row against the wall, were the keepers of the lamps. Caelen counted nine. Nessa stood at the end with her net-needle in her hand. She had not put it down.

Reth stood at the end of the table with the satchel closed in front of him.

He looked smaller than Caelen remembered, and older, and more tired. He had lost weight. There were grey hollows under his eyes. But he held himself exactly as he had held himself at the lectern in the Hall of Canon, upright and plain, hands lightly folded, and when he spoke, it was in the same quiet dry voice that had read a thousand deaths into a public record without a flicker.

"I should say first," he said, "that I have no right to be here. I have no sanction from the Board. I was sent to review a survey and have exceeded my authority by the width of an ocean. If you would like to arrest me, I am told you have a very good lock."

A faint ripple of sound went through the room. It might have been a laugh. It died almost at once.

"I came to read you a list," Reth said. "I will read it, and then I will answer any question the council wishes to put. I ask you only to hear it to the end."

He opened the satchel and took out a single folded sheet. He did not look at it. He put it on the table, smoothed it flat, and said, in a perfectly level voice:

"The Weeping Stair."

---

It took him eleven minutes.

He read them one at a time, with the same weight on each, neither slowly nor quickly: the *Weeping Stair, the Heron's Knee, Nessa's Dip, the Drowned Bell, the Long Shelf, the Cradle Hole, the Pilot's Ear.* They were not the Guild's designations. They were not seam numbers. They were the names the harbor had given them over three hundred years, the names the lamp families used among themselves, the names that were written in no book but the oldest margin and on no map but the one the fishermen carried in their heads. He had gathered them somehow, in the spring, from the boatmen and the net-wives, from a polite man with a brass instrument asking where each place was. And he read them in the order that they lay along the cliff, from the headland to the signal-post, with the faint, careful reverence of a man calling a roll.

"The Mother's Step. The Salt Door. The Little Chapel. The Lantern Shelf."

At the fourteenth name, Nessa made a small noise in her throat. At the twenty-second, she put her net-needle down.

"Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. The Last Light. Forty."

He stopped. The room had gone absolutely silent. The lamp on the table burned straight up, without a flicker.

"Forty-one," Reth said. "The Old Mouth."

He folded his hands.

"They are not the names of the dead," he said. "I want to be exact about that. I have read a great many lists of the dead in my life. This is not one of them. These are the names of places where something is alive. A hundred and nine, if the old Ledger is correct. I think it is. Each one of these is a decision."

"A decision," said Hask. His voice was rough.

"Yes." Reth did not look away. "Somebody will have to decide what is done with each. Leave it, or open it. Light it, or wake it. Defend it, or yield it. I have been in this work forty years, Reeve, and I do not know of a thing in the whole territory that has been so long put off. Your people have decided, three hundred times, in the dark, by lamplight, that the answer was *not yet.* I am told it was a good answer. I would like to say that I honor it." A very faint breath. "But I did not come to honor it. I came because the Guild put a ship in your roads in the spring, and I was not there to stop it, and the survey it left behind has written *test charges* beside every name on this list."

---

Nobody spoke for a long time.

It was Hask who moved first. He stood up slowly from the middle of the horseshoe, a big heavy man in a salt-stiff coat, and walked round the end of the table until he stood face to face with Reth. He was a head taller. He looked down at the small plain man for a very long moment.

"You said you carried nothing," he said. "You carry forty-one names."

"I do."

"Where did you get them?"

"From your boatmen." Reth held his gaze. "A man named Dacey, at the fish-wharf, told the surveyor where the Pilot's Ear was and thought nothing of it. A woman called Brann gave him the Salt Door in return for a cup of tea. They were not betraying you. They were being polite to a stranger. It is the thing I fear most in the world, Reeve. That the harm will come to this harbor entirely by way of people being polite."

Hask stood very still.

"Do you want something?" he said, finally. "I ask plainly. I am too old to dance."

"I want the harbor to hold the Guild's engineer to its own law," Reth said. "He carries sealed orders from the full Board. I cannot countermand them. You can. No instrument, no charge, no tool of the Guild is to be landed on this coast without the Reeve's leave." He paused. "I ask you to refuse him. I ask you to refuse *me.* I have no power on this headland but the one you choose to give. I would sooner you gave it to the Ledger than the Board."

It was, Caelen thought, watching from the back of the hall, the most dangerous sentence he had ever heard a man say. It was an honest offer of surrender. And he did not for one moment believe that it was all of what Reth meant.

"Why?" Hask said.

Reth looked at him. And for an instant, Caelen saw it, the raw tired thing behind the dry composure, the same look he had worn in the Hall of Canon when a stranger took a Warden's hands.

"Because it is the only way I know," Reth said, "to keep a hundred and nine children from being counted by a man who means them no harm."
