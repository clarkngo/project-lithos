# Chapter Eight — What Reth Offers

They brought the instruments to the council in a fish-crate, and laid them on the black oak table like evidence at a trial.

There were four. A brass theodolite no bigger than a man's head, packed in felt. A folding level. A coil of graduated chain. And a small, sealed, lead-lined box with a screw-down lid, which Hask had not opened, and which he set down in the middle of the table with great care, as a man puts down an egg.

"I found them at the Drowned Bell," he said. "At the third hour of the morning. Four men, with a shuttered lantern. They said they were taking bearings." His deep slow voice was perfectly steady. "They had a tripod set, with a plumb line over the edge, and a hole bored in the turf a hand deep, and that." He nodded at the box. "I did not ask what was in it. I took it."

Nobody spoke. Reth stood at the end of the table in his plain grey coat. He had not slept. He looked at the little lead box for a long moment with an expression that Caelen, standing against the back wall with Rook and Malakar, could not read, and then he said, very quietly:

"May I?"

"No," said Hask.

"Then I will ask you to open it. In front of the council. I would rather not be the one." A pause. "I know what it is. I would like you all to see."

Hask unscrewed the lid. He did it slowly, with big careful fingers. Inside, on a bed of felt, was a stick of grey waxed paper the length of a man's hand, with a short fuse coiled beside it, and a brass cap.

"A test charge," said Reth.

The silence was complete.

"Engineer-Captain Halse is aboard my ship," he said. "I gave him my written order, in my own hand, on the twenty-first night. *Not a single charge.* He told me he would convey it to the party. I took that for a refusal." He lifted his eyes. "I did not think he would go ashore the same night. I should have. It is how a very reasonable man disobeys."

"He has broken the harbor's law," said Hask.

"He has."

"I could arrest him."

"You could hang him, if you wished, under the Salt Office statute. I would hold the rope." The small grey man's voice did not rise. "It would not help. The Board would send another. It would send three, in the spring, with a warship. This is what I came to say, Reeve. It is the thing I could not say on the quay. May I say it now?"

Hask looked at him for a long moment. Then he sat down.

---

"I cannot stop the survey," Reth said.

He said it to the council, standing, with his hands clasped lightly in front of him, in the voice of a man reading a sentence. "I want that on the record before anything else. I have tried, and I have failed. The Board holds orders that predate my suspension, and in the Board's practice an old order can be countermanded only by the whole Board in session. The whole Board does not sit until November. The gales close the roads before that. If the harbor refuses me, it will be surveyed this autumn by blast, by men who are not wicked, who have never been told what is in the cliff, and I will not be able to prevent a single charge."

"Then why do you stand there?" Ibb said, in his dry old voice. "You have just told us you are powerless."

"I am. Over the Board." Reth turned to him. "I am not powerless over the thing the Board is *for.*"

He drew a folded paper from his coat. He did not give it to anyone. He held it for a moment between his fingers, small, grey, creased with many folds, and then laid it on the table beside the lead box.

"The Guild exists to extract," he said. "It does not know how to do anything else. I have spent forty years trying to teach it to do one thing differently, and I have failed, and I am too old to try again. But the Guild also follows its own law, with great fidelity. The Hall of Canon's finding says that persons of the rite may be recovered under the hall's registry, at public expense, by trained hands, in the open, with a roll. That is law. The Board is bound by it. If the Guild were to recover your sleepers *under the finding,* at its own cost, on the hall's roll, under your Reeve's seal, then the survey becomes redundant. There would be nothing left to survey. The cliff would be a house of treatment." He stopped. "I propose that the Guild fund and staff the waking of every sleeper on this coast. Openly. Slowly. Nine at a time, over three years, with your Harbor-Master's Ledger as the register, and your Reeve as the authority on the ground. The Guild would pay for the physicians, the houses, the food. It would hold no guardianship. It would claim no remains. It would take nothing."

"F-9," said Malakar, from the wall.

"Struck. Initialed. Witnessed." Reth did not turn his head. "I have a second copy, with the Master of the Salt Office's seal on it, in the satchel."

Caelen felt something shift in the room, a loosening, like ice breaking up in a river. He looked at Hask. The Reeve was staring at the folded paper with his heavy hands flat on the oak.

---

It was, he thought, the most intelligent thing he had ever heard a man say.

He saw it whole, in the grey light of the chamber, with the sea moving under the floor. Reth had not come to take. He had come to give. He had walked into the harbor with his hands open, and read the names of the pockets, and said *refuse me,* and now he stood before them, small and plain, and said *use me.* He had found a place where the Guild's oldest appetite and its newest conscience met, and he had stood on it. It was the same thing he had done in the Spire. He had made mercy and extraction the same line in a ledger, and he had done it honestly, and he would very likely save a hundred and nine lives.

*And it will not be enough,* Caelen thought, with a cold sudden certainty. *Because of the one thing he can't give.*

He stepped forward.

"May I ask something?"

"Please," said Reth.

"If we take your money. If the Guild pays for the houses, and the physicians, and the food. Who decides when to wake a pocket? And who decides when not to?"

"The Reeve."

"And if the Reeve says no?"

"Then the Reeve says no."

"And the Board?"

Reth was silent.

"You can't promise me that," Caelen said, gently. "You said it yourself. The whole Board doesn't sit until November. You can fund the houses. You can pay the physicians. You can strike a clause and sign it in front of a Reeve. But you can't promise me that when the Board sits, and sees the Guild's name on a hundred and nine wards, and a hundred and nine marrow-sleepers who might, in thirty years, be worth four stone apiece to a man who isn't you, it won't read your charter differently." He took a breath. "I'm not saying you'd lie. I'm saying you're one man."

"Yes," Reth said. It came out quite plainly. "I am."

"Then I'll say what I said in the Hall of Canon." Caelen's voice shook, and he let it. "Fund the door. Don't hold it. Pay for the physicians. Pay for the houses. Put the money in the Ledger's keeping, in escrow, under the Reeve's seal, and let nobody wearing the Guild's grey set foot on this headland with an instrument or a charge. Not Halse. Not his party. Not you. And if you want to know why — " He stopped.

The girl had come in.

He had not heard her. She stood at his elbow, small and quiet, in her cut-down smock, with the wooden horse under her arm. Tamsin hovered in the doorway behind her, looking stricken. The council had turned. Every head in the tight little room.

She looked at Reth.

She had never seen him up close. She studied him, gravely, from his plain grey coat to his tired grey eyes, with the same deliberate care she had given the faces on the quay, and Reth, to his everlasting credit, did not look away.

"*No,*" she said.

It was perfectly clear. It was one of her forty words. She said it without anger, without fear, with a kind of absolute calm. And then, because she was nine, and had only forty, she put out one finger and pointed at the lead box on the table, and said the other word she had that fitted.

"*Hot.*"

---

They voted at dusk.

It was seven to four. The Guild's money would come, in escrow, under the Ledger's seal. The Guild's physicians would come, Tam the clerk would come, and a nurse, a tutor, and a doctor from the Spire whom Seraphina would name. No instrument. No charge. No Guild survey party. Halse's men were to be off the headland by the morning tide, and the *Tally* was to weigh anchor and stand out beyond the Teeth, and Reth himself, if he wished to stay, would stay as the guest of the Reeve, under the harbor's law, with a boy and a boathook.

Reth bowed. His face was grey with fatigue. He said, "I accept," in a voice that cracked on the second word, and sat down heavily in the nearest chair, an old man who had been standing for two days.

It should have been over. It was the best that could have been made of it. Caelen stood at the window as the lamps came on along the cliff, and Rook came up and leaned beside him, and for a minute neither spoke.

"It's good," Rook said, finally. "Isn't it? It's the right thing."

"It's the right thing."

"Then why's the hair standing up on my neck?"

Caelen did not answer. He was looking at the sea.

The sky to the south had gone a strange color. It was a dull bruised yellow-green, low on the horizon, like the underside of a leaf, and above it the wind-streaked cloud was moving fast, much too fast, in long parallel bars toward the land. The air had that heavy, listening quality he had known before. The gulls had gone from the quay. The water in the harbor mouth lay flat and oily, without a ripple, and beyond the Teeth, very far out, a thin white line was forming where there should have been none.

Hask had come to the window. He stood with his big hands behind him, looking out, and his weathered face was quite still.

"That is early," the Reeve said. "That is a month early."

"What is?"

"The gales," said Hask. "They are coming in off the sea."

Behind them, on the table, the little lead box sat in the lamplight with its lid open and its grey stick of paper lying in the felt like something asleep.
