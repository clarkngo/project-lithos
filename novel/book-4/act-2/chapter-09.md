# Chapter Nine — The Storm Season

The gale struck at the fourth hour of the night, and by the fifth the harbor was a throat full of white water.

Caelen woke to it in the dark of the guest house with the whole building shuddering round him like a ship. The shutters were banging. Rain came through the roof-tiles in thin hard streams. He lay for a moment listening to the wind, which did not howl but screamed, on one long rising note, and underneath it a deeper sound, a vast slow grinding, that was the sea beating on the Teeth. Then he was on his feet and dressed, and Rook was beside him, and Malakar in the doorway with a lantern, all of them wordless.

"The *Tally,*" said Malakar.

"Dragged her anchor," said Rook, from the window. "I can see her lights. She's going down on the breakwater."

She was not. They watched, from the top of the stair, as the long grey ship swung at the end of her cable in the screaming dark, bow to the wind, and held. Her lamps swung like a pendulum. A boat had put out from the quay, and then another, and then the first came scudding back, and a voice shouted that no skiff could cross the roads tonight, not with the sea like this. The Guild's engineers were off the headland by order of the council. They had not been taken aboard. They had never reached the ship.

"Where are they?" said Caelen.

Nobody answered. Then Corwin appeared at the foot of the stair, soaked to the skin, his good grey coat black with rain and a lantern in his fist, and said in a high strange voice, "Nessa's boy came down the cliff road. He says there's a light on the headland. At the Drowned Bell. He says it's *burning.*"

---

He ran.

He had not known he could still run like that. He had slept four hours in nine days, and eaten almost nothing, and the cold was in his bones like a second skeleton, but his legs went out from under him on the stair and carried him down it three steps at a time, and then he was in the lane with the rain driving into his face like thrown gravel and the others strung out behind him in the dark.

The cliff road was a river. The gravel had washed out in long gullies, and the white edge-stones stood up out of the flood like teeth. To the left, the headland fell away into a black roaring void where the sea beat on the rock and flung spray fifty feet into the air. To the right, along the cliff face, the little lamps were all lit. Every one. They burned in their tin hoods through the storm, steady, gold, undisturbed, forty-one tiny flames in a night that had put out the stars. Nessa's people had been out since midnight with oil-cans, and had not let one fail.

He ran past them. He felt each one go by. He felt, under his feet, in the soaking road, the entire cliff beginning to sing: not the slow sweet murmur of the nights before, but a thin high rising tone, like a string drawn tight, a hundred and nine hearts all beating faster. They knew. They could feel it, the way he could feel it: something wrong in the stone.

*Hold,* he thought. *Hold. I'm coming.*

"Caelen!" Rook, behind him, hoarse. "Wait — the girl — "

He turned. She was at his heels.

He had not seen her follow. She was in her oilcloth, a small drenched figure with the wooden horse buttoned inside her coat, running flat out through the flood with her mouth set and her eyes huge and gold in the lantern-light. Tamsin was two strides behind her, white-faced, a rope round her shoulders and a lamp in her hand.

"She heard," Tamsin gasped. "She was at the window. She just — *went.*"

He did not argue. There was no time. He reached out without breaking stride and took the girl's hand, and her fingers closed on his, hot and hard, and together they ran on into the dark.

---

The Drowned Bell lay beyond the signal-post, where the cliff dipped and the road ran down to a ledge above the sea.

He saw the light first. It was a small red spark, low on the turf at the cliff's edge, in the lee of a boulder, hissing in the rain. A fuse. Someone had been very careful with it. They had laid a length of tarred cord down a groove in the rock and under an oilcloth to keep it dry, and it was burning, a bright steady bead of fire, toward a hole in the ground.

There were three men by the boulder. They had a shuttered lantern and a canvas tarpaulin and the kind of look that people have when they have been caught doing something sensible. One of them was a square sandy man in a grey coat, with whiskers, who was holding a pocket-watch.

"Halse!" Caelen shouted.

The Engineer-Captain turned. He did not look afraid. He looked tired, and wet, and extremely reasonable.

"Stand clear!" he called. "It's a relief charge! The seam is unstable, the storm is working it, there's a pocket of gas under the shelf — we've got to vent it before the cliff goes!"

"It's not gas!"

"It's a periodic anomaly, we've measured it — "

"It's *people!*"

The watch in Halse's hand swung. The man beside him, young, with a rain-streaked face, looked from the fuse to the stranger running at him through the dark, and something in his expression broke. He started to say something. Caelen did not hear it. He saw only the red bead of fire, burning inch by inch along the cord, and judged the distance with the part of him that had been judging distances in dark mines for ten years.

*Forty feet. Thirty seconds. Less.*

"Rook!" he roared.

Rook was already past him. He went like a thrown knife, low and flat and silent, across the sodden turf, with his coat flying, and Malakar behind him; and Halse, startled, swung round with his arms flung out to bar the way, and Malakar hit him at the knees. They went down together in a tangle in the mud. Rook leaped the boulder. He had his knife out. The fuse lay at his feet, burning, a handsbreadth from the hole, and he threw himself on it, and slashed.

The blade went through the cord.

The flame went on.

It was an old trick. The engineers had known their business. The tar-cord was a double core, with a slow-match inside the outer skin, and the cut did not stop it; the red bead leaped the gap and sped, hissing, along the inner core, down into the bored hole, and was gone.

Rook rolled away on his hands and knees, screaming a word Caelen could not hear.

---

He understood, in the next instant, exactly what was about to happen. He had understood it in the forest, nine years old, in the dark, in the wet. He saw it whole.

The hole went down into the pocket. At the bottom of the hole was a stick of grey waxed paper. When it went, the stone would shatter. The shell of honey glass that held the sleepers would break into a thousand pieces, and what was in it would be flung out into the storm, into the sea, broken, torn, alone in the dark. It would be the Long Shelf, but wrong. It would be every one of the seven. It would be him.

*A broken cradle breaks the child.*

He dropped on his knees at the cliff's edge, in the mud and the rain, and slapped both palms flat on the turf.

The cliff screamed. He felt it come up through his hands: three of them, close together, curled in the dark, in the warm. Three small hearts, going fast. Three sleepers asleep in the Drowned Bell, in the glass, in the stone, who did not know.

He had no time. He had no song. He had a coal in his chest and a breath and a note.

He sang.

It was not the long note. It was the first sound he had ever made, in the dark, under a mountain, before there were words. It tore out of him raw and wide and wordless, into the screaming wind, and he felt it go down through the earth like a spike, and he felt the stone take it. He reached down, down through the root of the cliff, toward the three small hearts. *Hold. Hold. I have you. Hold.*

A small hand closed on his.

The girl had knelt beside him. She had thrown her arms round his arm, her face pressed to his sleeve, her whole thin body shaking with the effort. She opened her mouth, and a note came out of her, high and clear and impossibly steady, a half-step above his, and the two sounds met and locked in the dark like a hand in a hand. Her palm was on the stone. His was on hers.

Beneath them, in the hole, a spark reached the end of a cord.

He did not see it. He did not hear it. There was a sound that was not a sound, a vast soft pressure, like a hand clapped over the world; and a flash that turned the rain to silver; and then the cliff rose up under his knees like the back of a living animal, and threw them into the dark.
