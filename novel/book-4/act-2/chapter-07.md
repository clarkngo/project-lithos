# Chapter Seven — Three Hundred Years

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Brine-Reeve Hask — a controlled exception, given to the man whose fear the book owes a hearing. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**HASK**

He walked the cliff road at night, alone, as his father had, and counted the dead.

There were forty-three of them, and he knew the stones by feel. They stood on the seaward shelf below the signal-post, where the headland ran out and the rock fell sheer to the Teeth, a small bare plateau of salt-bitten turf with a low drystone wall round it and no tree for a mile. The keepers lay there. Not the Reeves; the Reeves lay in the town graveyard, in rows, with their ledger numbers and their long brave epitaphs. These were the others. The ones who had held the door.

They had no names on their stones. That was the custom. Each was a flat grey slab, set upright in the turf, with a single mark scratched in the face: a small upright stroke, and a slash across. He had never been told what the mark meant. He had known since he was nine years old. He ran his thumb down the nearest one, in the dark, as another man might touch a rosary, and the cold went up his arm.

*One,* he thought. *And one. And one.*

His father was the forty-first. The stone was new, as stones went. It was twenty-six years old, and the lichen had barely begun. Hask stood before it with his hat in his hand and the wind coming off the sea, and did not speak for a long time.

---

He had been sixteen when the old man first took him down to the Long Shelf.

It was the autumn before the herring failed. They had gone at dead low water with a lantern, down the lamp-men's ladder to the ledge, and his father had put him on his knees before the niche at the end of the gallery and said, in the harsh low voice he used for the things he could not say, *look.* Hask had looked. He had seen, in the lantern light, in the glow of the honey stone, a boy of his own age, curled in the amber like a seed in a nut. He had seen the small slow pulse in the wall. He had thought it a corpse.

*He's not,* his father had said. *None of them are.* A long breath. *We are not the owners of this, Hask. We are the keepers of the door.*

*What's behind it?*

*Nothing,* said his father. *That's the burden. We don't know. We know that it must not be opened by the wrong hand. And we know that the right hand has never come.* He had put his big scarred palm flat on the stone. *When I am gone it will be you. You will light the lamp. You will tell the tide. And one day somebody will come and ask you to open it, and you will say no. Because you will not know how. And because a keeper who opens a door he cannot close has not kept it.*

He had said no, for twenty-six years. He had said it to the Salt Office and the Synod's clerk and the Guild's polite men, and to a priest from the cathedral at Marrow Bay who had wept and quoted scripture. He had said it to his own wife, who had wanted him to give the sleepers a chapel. He had never once felt that he was lying.

*Three hundred years,* he thought. *And not one of us has known how.*

---

They had woken the boy on the ninth day, in the grey of the morning, and Hask had not gone down.

He had stood on the clifftop above the Long Shelf in his salt-stiff coat with his hands behind him and a hundred of his people silent beside him in the dawn, and watched the tide come in. He had not been able to see the ledge. He had seen only the six oilskinned figures going down the ladder, nine days ago, and the lamp at the foot, and the small steady yellow light that had burned there night and day since, like a heart. He had heard the song. It had come up out of the slot in the rock for nine days, the man's voice and the child's, thin and clear and endless, and the women of the lower town had stopped at their doors to listen, and the fishermen had come in early from the boats.

He had thought, *they will kill him.* He had thought it every hour. He had stood on the cliff with that thought in his belly like a stone, and he had counted the stones in the graves in his head.

At the turn of the tide, he had heard the scream.

It had gone through him like a hook. It was the cry of a child waking in a place he did not know, and his whole body had moved before his mind, and he had been at the head of the ladder with one boot on the rung and his hand clenched on the rope, ready to go down and take the boy out of the hands that had broken him — when the song had changed.

It was a small new sound. It was three tones and a fall. And out of the slot in the rock, thin and shaky and astonished, a second voice, a boy's, had answered it.

Hask had stopped.

He had stood on the ladder with his hand on the rope and the sea in his ears. He had heard the old keeper's voice, Nessa's, rise from the bottom of the shaft, cracked and wild, saying something over and over that was not a prayer. He had heard a man weeping. And he had understood, slowly, in the cold light, that the stone in his belly had gone.

It had not been replaced by anything. It was only gone.

---

He had gone down the next evening, when the tide-house had sent word that the boy was sleeping.

They had put him in the infirmary on the upper floor, in a clean white bed by an open window, with a clerk to sit by him and the girl curled at the foot with her wooden horse. The boy was thin and brown and small. He lay on his back with his mouth open, breathing slowly, and one hand flung out on the sheet. Hask stood in the door in his coat and did not know what to do with his hat.

The girl looked up. She regarded him gravely. She had seen him in the great hall, in the crowd on the quay, and once through a window with the chain going up behind him. He saw her take the measure of him and decide.

She put one finger to her lips.

"I will not wake him," he said, in a whisper. It was the first time he had spoken to her. "I only — "

He stopped. There was nothing to finish the sentence with.

She slid off the bed and came across the boards in her bare feet and stood in front of him, a head and a half shorter than his belt. She looked up. Then she took the hand that held his hat, quite deliberately, and turned it over, and pressed her small palm into his. It was warm. It was very warm. It was as warm as the stone at the Long Shelf. She held it there, and her lips moved, and she said, in her hoarse cracked careful voice, one of her forty words.

"*Stay.*"

He could not answer. He stood in the white doorway with a nine-year-old's hand in his, in the smell of salt and linen and warm child, and found that his eyes were running and his chin was shaking and he could not make it stop.

---

He went back to the shelf of stones before midnight.

He did not know why. He had told himself he would see to the lamps. He had trimmed the Old Mouth himself for twenty-six years, a thing he had never told the council, nor the lamp-keepers either, because a Reeve who trims a lamp is a Reeve who has been caught being afraid. He had done it every night, in the dark, with his own oil-can. His father had done it before him.

He stood before the forty-first stone.

*I was wrong,* he thought. He tried the words, in his own mind, to see how they sat. They sat badly. *I was wrong about the door.*

It had never been a door to be kept shut. That was the thing. He saw it plainly now, in the dark, in the wind. His father had said *keepers,* and he had heard *guards,* and for three hundred years every Reeve in this harbor had stood with his back to the rock and his face to the world, holding the handle. And all the time it had been a door to be opened, one day, by the right hand, in the right way. A door held for someone.

*He did not say what to do when the right hand came,* he thought, and a thin, bitter, almost-laugh rose in his chest. *He said I would not know. He was right. I did not know.*

*But I do not have to know. I only have to let them in.*

It was a sentence he could not have said a week ago. It sat in his mouth like a stone he could finally spit. He stood with his palm on his father's cold grave and the sea roaring below, and was a very old man, and a very young one, and did not move.

The light was on the clifftop when he lifted his head.

---

It was a small light, a mile north, up on the headland above the signal-post. Not a lamp-keeper's yellow. A cold, pale, steady glow, like the light of a shuttered lantern with a brass hood, and it moved. It went along the clifftop in a slow line. It stopped. It moved again.

Hask stood very still.

He had given his word, in front of eleven witnesses, in his own council chamber. *No instrument, no charge, no tool of the Guild to be landed on this coast.* He had posted a man at the quay. He had had the *Tally's* skiff watched since the chain went down. And a mile up the headland, in the dark, a light was moving along the edge of the cliff exactly where the grey painted stakes stood in their little line.

He counted the places the light stopped. One. Two. Three.

*The Drowned Bell,* he thought. *They are at the Drowned Bell.*

He took his hat from his belt and put it on. Then he turned and walked up the cliff road, not fast, an old man in a salt-stiff coat, toward the light, with his father's key in his pocket and forty-three stones at his back. He did not call out. He did not run.

He had stood at that door for twenty-six years, and for the first time he knew exactly what he was guarding it from.
