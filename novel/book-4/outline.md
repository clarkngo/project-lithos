# The Last Lithoi — Book Four: The Sleeping Shore (working title)

**Structure:** Two acts, continuing directly from Book Three's close. Book One asked what Caelen would spend to keep his own autonomy. Book Two asked what it costs to let that choice belong to other people. Book Three asked what a whole territory's doctrine owes the people it governs. Book Four turns the series' oldest question around: Caelen is no longer the child in the amber. He is the one standing over it. See [style-guide.md](../style-guide.md) for voice and terminology; see [outline.md](../outline.md), [book-2/outline.md](../book-2/outline.md), and [book-3/outline.md](../book-3/outline.md) for everything this volume assumes as read.

**Time frame:** Opens a few weeks after Book Three's close, in late spring. The voyage to the Salt Coast takes twenty-two days. The action runs through the summer and ends with the first autumn storms, so the sea itself is a clock.

**Setting:** The Salt Coast, a harbor people far from the Spire, who have kept their own counsel about the deep places for as long as anyone has kept records. Their strata run under the sea cliffs and flood twice a day with the tide, so they have mined marrow-glass by lantern and rope for generations, and never at the scale the Guild did. They have a doctrine of their own, a priesthood of tide-clerks, and a ledger called the Deep Ledger that records every body ever pulled from the cliffs.

**Cast additions:**
- **Ondrei Vesh** — Harbor-Master and Keeper of the Deep Ledger, the author of the letter. Dry, exact, frightened, and honest about it. He wrote because the coast's own doctrine had nothing left to try.
- **The girl** — roughly nine years old, found sealed in honey-colored stone in a flooded gallery of the third harbor. She has no language and, as far as anyone can tell, never has. She speaks in pulse and hum, matching the marrow around her. She does not yet have a name.
- **Tamsin Orre** — a tide-clerk and the only person on the coast the girl will let near her. Young, stubborn, and out of her depth, she is the first to treat the girl as a person rather than a discovery.
- **Brine-Reeve Hask** — head of the coast's harbor council. Not a villain: a man who believes, with some evidence, that outsiders have only ever come to the coast to take something.
- **Chairman Reth** *(returning)* — arrives by sea in Act Two, with names, as promised.

**Who goes:** Caelen, Rook, Seraphina, Malakar, and Corwin. Orell stays at Kesh Hollow to run the Wardens' house of treatment. Lisbet runs the Spire's.

## ACT ONE — The Third Harbor

*POV: Caelen, with inserts from Seraphina, Tamsin (new), and Chairman Reth. See [style-guide.md §4](../style-guide.md): each insert is flagged, rare, and given to someone whose reasoning the book needs from the inside.*

1. **Chapter 01 — "Twenty-Two Days"**
   The sea crossing. Rook is unhappily seasick, Malakar is quietly delighted, and Corwin has never seen open water. Caelen cannot sleep, because he keeps imagining what he is sailing toward: a child who has been awake for weeks with nobody to tell her it is over. The chapter's quiet argument is about what, exactly, he intends to say to her.

2. **Chapter 02 — "The Deep Ledger"**
   Arrival. The Salt Coast is not hostile, and the courtesy is the first warning. Vesh receives them with perfect formality, reads their credentials with the care of a man checking a lock, and takes them to the harbor's tide-house, where the Deep Ledger is kept. They are shown the entry for the girl. It has a date, a depth, and a line for a name, which is blank.

3. **Chapter 03 — "The Honey-Colored Stone"**
   The first sight of her. She is held in a whitewashed room above the harbor, and the room has been made into a cell by people trying very hard to make it a nursery. She does not look at Caelen's face. She looks at his chest. Her hum finds the banked coal there and matches it, and he understands, with a cold drop, that she can feel what he is.

4. **Chapter 04 — Seraphina insert — "What the Fragments Never Said"**
   Seraphina reads the Salt Coast's own records of the amber children, and finds that the coast has pulled sleepers from the cliffs before, a handful in three hundred years. Every one of them died within weeks, of causes the tide-clerks recorded as *waking*. The Ledger has no theory for it. Seraphina has a half-formed one.

5. **Chapter 05 — "Terms"**
   Caelen's first real attempt at contact fails. He tries to do for her what Varrick did for him, and finds he does not know how, because Varrick's method was never a method: it was patience and meat on a stone. He sits in the room for three days without trying to do anything at all. On the fourth she eats.

6. **Chapter 06 — Tamsin insert — "The Girl Who Learned to Wait"**
   The flagged exception. Tamsin has spent the girl's whole waking life fighting a harbor council that wants her studied, a priesthood that wants her named a relic, and her own wish to simply fix what is wrong. She watches a stranger do something she could not: nothing, at length, and be trusted for it.

7. **Chapter 07 — "Names for What She Is"**
   The book's mirror of Book One's fourth chapter. Caelen teaches her words the way he was taught them, one at a time, grounded in the body. She learns *bread* and *cold* and *stay*. She learns that she is not the only one of her kind, because he shows her the small warm stone of his own name. Then she does something he did not expect: she hums a sound back, and it is a word, and it is not in any language he has heard.

8. **Chapter 08 — "The Cliff Road"**
   Rook and Malakar, left to their own devices, walk the sea-cliffs and find what the harbor has been careful not to show them. The Ledger lists forty-one pockets of honey-colored stone mapped along the coast, holding by its own reckoning a hundred and nine sleepers, charted by the tide-clerks over three centuries and never opened. The coast has been guarding a nursery.

9. **Chapter 09 — Chairman Reth insert — "The Names"**
   Reth, three weeks behind them on a Guild ship, with a leather book. He has spent the spring gathering failures: a fraudulent camp, a practitioner's error, a child dead in a barn. He is not here for the girl. He is here because the Guild's coastal survey, sent months before the letter, has just reported forty-one anomalous seams, and Reth does the arithmetic of what one blast could do to forty-one of them.

10. **Chapter 10 — "The Name She Chose"**
    Act One's climax and its tenderest beat. She gives herself a name, not in any language, but in the hum Caelen has been teaching her to hold. It is, to his ear, a note. Tamsin writes it into the Ledger's blank line as a sound. Caelen understands that he has just done, for someone else, the one thing Varrick never quite managed to say he was doing for him. The chapter ends with a sail on the horizon, flying the Guild's grey.

## ACT TWO — The Sleeping Shore

*POV: Caelen, with inserts from Seraphina, Malakar, and Brine-Reeve Hask (new). The last is the book's controlled exception for the argument that the coast has been right to be afraid.*

1. **Chapter 01 — "The Grey Sail"**
   Reth's ship makes harbor, and the town, which has never needed walls, discovers that it does. He asks for nothing. He presents his credentials, his survey, and his ledger, and he reads the council forty-one names in the same flat clear voice he used in the Hall of Canon. They are not the names of the dead. They are the names of pockets, and every one of them is, as he puts it, *a decision somebody will have to make.*

2. **Chapter 02 — "What Hask Fears"**
   The coast's council divides. The Brine-Reeve wants the Guild gone and the pockets sealed for another three hundred years. Vesh wants to wake them, carefully, because the Ledger has a hundred and nine unnamed sleepers in its margin and he cannot stop counting. Tamsin wants the girl left out of it entirely. Nobody asks the girl.

3. **Chapter 03 — Seraphina insert — "The Cradle Rite"**
   The breakthrough. Seraphina finds the missing fragment in the coast's oldest cliff-carvings, and it recasts the series: the amber was never a prison. It was a cradle. The Lithoi, facing the Culling, sealed their young in marrow-glass to carry their resonance past the extinction, and the tethering rite was never meant for the drifting. It was the waking rite, designed to bring a sleeper out slowly, with an anchor. The blast that woke Caelen is why he nearly drifted his whole life.

4. **Chapter 04 — "Grown, Not Touched"**
   Caelen takes this in at the cost of an entire night. He was not a human child changed by proximity. He was a Lithoi-made child, a seed, left in a mine for a people who were gone. Everything he has said about himself for a decade is true and incomplete. He goes to the girl. She is awake. She puts her palm flat on his chest, and the hum between them changes.

5. **Chapter 05 — Malakar insert — "The Case File of the Sleeping"**
   Malakar reads the Guild's coastal survey as he once read every document: for what it hands the keys to. He sees what Reth has not said. The survey does not just map the pockets. It prices them, and Reth's own engineers have submitted a proposal to *recover* them, under a licensed charter, in the name of mercy. Malakar's old habit of seeing the thing the language is built to hide is, for once, a weapon.

6. **Chapter 06 — "Forty-One"**
   The party decides to wake one. Not for a cause, but for a name: Vesh's Ledger has a sleeper in the third harbor's own gallery, a boy. The waking is done as the cradle rite requires, slowly, with Caelen as the anchor and the girl, to everyone's alarm, as the second. It takes nine days. It costs Caelen more than anything since the Vault. The boy wakes, and he is human-sized, and he is afraid, and he is *fine.* It is the first time in the series that the rite has simply worked.

7. **Chapter 07 — Brine-Reeve Hask insert — "Three Hundred Years"**
   The flagged exception. Hask walks the cliff road alone and counts his ancestors' graves. His family has guarded the pockets since before the Culling's records began, and the coast's whole doctrine of refusal was handed down as a grim bargain: *do not wake them until the world is ready to hold them.* He has watched the world arrive at the harbor in a grey ship, and he knows exactly how ready it is.

8. **Chapter 08 — "What Reth Offers"**
   Reth makes his case, and for the first time in the series it is not an offer for money, or a register, or a license. He proposes that the Guild fund and staff the waking of every sleeper on the coast, openly, on the roll, under the hall's finding. He is not lying. He has simply, patiently, found the way to make mercy and extraction the same line item. If they accept, forty-one children are saved. If they refuse, the Guild will survey the pockets by blast, and he will not be able to stop it.

9. **Chapter 09 — "The Storm Season"**
   The autumn storms arrive early, and the sea cuts the harbor off. The Guild's engineers, impatient, start a survey blast on the third pocket without orders. Tamsin sees it from the cliff. The party runs. The climax is not a battle but a race against a charge: reaching the sleeper before the stone does, with the girl beside Caelen, holding a hum steady against a detonation.

10. **Chapter 10 — "The Third Pocket"**
    They are half in time. One sleeper is woken badly, by blast, and it is the exact thing that happened to Caelen. He holds her anchor with everything he has, and the girl holds his, and between the three of them the sleeper does not drift. It costs Rook the hearing in one ear and Corwin a broken wrist. It costs Caelen something the book does not name until the last chapter. Hask, who has been standing on the cliff with a lantern, finally comes down.

11. **Chapter 11 — "What the Coast Remembers"**
    The aftermath. The council votes to open the Deep Ledger to the roll and to wake the sleepers, one at a time, in the open, under the rite and the hall's finding. Reth pledges the Guild's money and Kell's audit. Hask abstains and, afterward, asks to be taught the rite. The Ledger's blank line is no longer blank.

12. **Chapter 12 — "The Salt Coast, Wider"**
    Denouement. The girl stands on the harbor wall at dusk, humming a name that nobody but Caelen can pronounce. He thinks of a forest, a spider, a man's unhurried boots, and of what he was never able to say to the man who walked them. He says it aloud, to the girl, who does not yet have the words, and who understands him anyway. Sets up Book Five: a hundred and nine sleepers on the Ledger's margin, a continent's worth of strata the Guild has not yet surveyed, and a question no hall can vote on, which is what the Lithoi were trying to say.

## Cross-Book Throughlines to Track

- **Caelen becomes Varrick.** Book One's whole structure was a child being taken in on terms. Book Four reverses it. Every beat of Varrick's method (meat on a stone, "terms, not charity," not looking back to see if you're followed) should recur, and recur differently, because Caelen is not Varrick and must find his own version of the same care.
- **"Grown, not touched" finally means something.** A decade and more of a phrase the series has used as a mystery gets its answer here, and the answer should reframe the earlier books without invalidating them. The blast that woke Caelen was a violation, and the waking rite is the repair.
- **The rite's true purpose.** Seraphina has spent three books treating the tethering rite as a medicine for Drift. Book Four reveals it as a midwifery, and treats her three books of work as having been exactly right and exactly partial. Her arc is not a correction. It is a completion.
- **Scale again, and then a limit.** Reth is the series' best antagonist because he is almost always right about the problem. This book should give him his best possible argument, and then show the one thing that argument cannot account for: a nine-year-old who will not be a line item.
- **Hearing, voice, language.** The girl has no language. The book's formal discipline is to keep her out of other characters' mouths until she has one of her own.
- **The found family's roster.** Ansel, Varrick, and Fenn are gone. This book should spend someone's *body* (Rook's hearing) rather than their life, for the third time in a different register, and should end with the roster larger, not smaller: Tamsin and the girl are family by the last page.
