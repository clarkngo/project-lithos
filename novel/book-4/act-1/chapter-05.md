# Chapter Five — Terms

He made it worse on the second day, and he knew exactly how.

She had come three feet toward him on the first. It had seemed like the whole road. He had lain awake that night in the guest house with the tide moving under the foundations and gone over it, step by step, and every step had looked like a victory. She had looked at his face. She had answered his note. He had spent twenty-two days wondering what to say to her, and the answer had turned out to be nothing at all, and for the space of an evening he had let himself believe that was the end of the difficulty.

So on the second morning he went up the stair with a bowl of warm porridge and a plan.

It was a good plan. It was the plan of a man who had been shown, by a hundred patient people, what care looked like and had decided to do it faster. He would sit where he had sat. He would offer the bowl. When she came near, he would hum her note, and light the coal, a little, just a thread, so she would feel the warmth in him and know it for safe. He would give her what she had given him. A conversation.

Tamsin stood at the door with her arms crossed and said nothing, which he took, at the time, for permission.

The girl came out of the blankets as soon as he sat. She came faster than the day before. She crossed the boards on all fours, quick and low, and stopped a yard away with her eyes on his chest, and he held out the bowl and hummed. It was easy. It was almost like speaking. He felt the coal answer in his sternum, small, bright, obedient, and he let it rise.

He meant it as a hand held out. He did not think about what it would feel like from the other side.

She screamed.

It was not a child's scream. It was a note, one long ragged shearing tone that went through the room and into the stone, and the handprints on the walls flared white. The lattice over the window rang like a struck bell. He felt the heat come out of the floor under his knees, and the bowl of porridge in his hand cracked in two. And she went backward across the boards in one scrambling convulsion and was under the bed before he had finished flinching, a small hot shape in the dark with her back against the wall and her eyes burning gold.

The coal was lit. He had not noticed. It had gone from a thread to a flame in the space of her cry, and when he looked down, his right hand was grey to the wrist and the porridge was boiling on the floor.

"Put it out," Tamsin said, very quietly. "Caelen. *Put it out.*"

He put it out. It took him a long time. When he had finished, there was a burn across his palm in the shape of the bowl's rim, white at the edges, and he was so cold that his teeth had started to chatter.

He had been so sure. That was the thing he kept turning over, afterward, on the stair, in the wind. He had been sure that what she wanted was to be reached. He had never once asked what it meant to a thing that could read a heartbeat to have a stranger light a fire inside it and call it kindness.

---

"I could have told you," Tamsin said.

They sat on the steps outside, on the third landing, with the town going about its morning below them and the tide drawn back from the foot of the cliff. Caelen held his hand out of the wind. She had wrapped it in a strip of clean linen, with a competence that told him she had done this before, for someone else.

"Why didn't you?"

"Because you'd have had to want to hear it." She shrugged. She was picking at the knot of her sash. "People come up that stair with a plan. The Reeve came with a plan. The priests came with a bell, a tide-bell, to ring her up out of the sleep. A man from the council came with a list of questions in a box. You're the fourth. You're the only one who's been burned."

"What happened to the others?"

"She hid. She hid for a day each." A short unhappy breath. "She doesn't hide from you for the same reason, I don't think. I think she's frightened of you in a different way. I think you scare her because you're like her." She looked at him sidelong. "That's a thing I noticed, I'm a clerk, I notice things. When you hummed, she didn't scream at the sound. She screamed at the *heat.*"

Caelen was silent.

"She's not the one who needs to be taught what's safe," Tamsin said. "That's all I mean. She's been awake thirteen weeks in a house full of people who all want something. She knows what safe is. She just hasn't seen it."

He turned the linen over. It was warm against his hand.

*Come down,* he thought. *Eat.*

It was not what he had done. He had come up the stair with a bowl and a plan and a flame. He had not once done the thing Varrick did. He had not once walked away.

---

On the third morning he went up the stair with nothing.

He wanted to be sure of that. He took nothing in his hands. He took a thin blanket for the cold, and a flask of water, and a strip of dried meat in his coat that he did not intend to offer, and he told Tamsin at the door what he meant to do, and she heard him out without a word.

"All day?"

"All day."

"And the next?"

"If I have to."

"The council will not like it." She was already lifting down the bar with its stupid blue ribbon. "The Reeve will ask what you're doing. I'll tell him you're listening." She stopped, and for a moment the stubborn guarded look went out of her face. "That's the truth, isn't it? That's what it is. You're listening."

"I don't know," Caelen said. "I'm going to find out."

He sat down in the same place as the first day, cross-legged, a long arm's length from the nest, and did not look at it. He looked at the wall. He did not hum. He did not reach for the coal, and when it stirred, once, in the afternoon, at some distant sound of a child's laughter in the street, he held it down with a patience he had not known he possessed. He ate his ration at noon. He drank. He sat.

The room was quiet. The tide went out and came in. The stars on the ceiling, which some clerk had painted so carefully, shone gold in the long light. He found he could count them. He found he had counted them. There were a hundred and twelve, and one of them had been painted upside down.

She did not come out. He had not expected her to. He sat in the warm yellow room, and the afternoon went over him slowly, and the light moved across the floor and up the wall, and when it was dark he lit the lamp Tamsin had left and went on sitting. At midnight he lay down on the boards with the blanket and slept as he had slept in the root-hollow of a fallen tree, folded and wary and half-awake. When the light came, he was still there.

---

The second day was longer. The hum started in the evening, from the nest, thin and wandering, and he did not answer it. He thought, *Don't. She's not asking.* It went on a while and stopped.

The third day was the worst, because the council came.

They came up the stair at midmorning, four of them in dark blue, with the Brine-Reeve at their head, and stood in the open doorway looking in. Caelen did not turn. He heard Hask's deep slow voice say, "He is sitting there."

"He is," said Tamsin.

"He has been sitting there two days."

"Three. He sat on the first."

"For what end?"

"I'd say he's waiting for her to be ready."

"And if she is never ready?" That was one of the others, a thin dry voice. "We have a harbor to run, Clerk. We have a doctrine. We cannot afford to keep a stranger in a locked room for the length of his patience."

Caelen did not move. He felt the whole room waiting, the clerks in the doorway and the child in the dark and the sea breathing under the house, and understood that this was the true test. Not whether the girl would come out. Whether he would turn round, and make a speech, and say, *give me time,* and become one more person who wanted something.

He kept his eyes on the wall.

"Let him sit," Hask said, finally. A long pause. "I have sat in a dark room a good many years, waiting for the sea to give something back. I have not noticed that shouting improved it." The boots shifted. "Come. We are in the way."

When the door had closed, the quiet that came after it was different. It was thicker. It had somebody in it.

---

The hum began again an hour later, from nearer.

He did not look. He kept his breath long and his hands open on his knees. He felt her cross the floor, that small hot presence, as clearly as if she had spoken, and stop, a yard away, and stay. He heard her breathing. It had slowed. He heard the faint scrape of a nail on the board, and the rustle of a smock.

And then he smelled it. He had not noticed it was there. It was the strip of meat, dried and salt and dark, which had fallen from his coat pocket sometime in the night and lay on the floor between them, where he had put his hand down when he stretched. He had not placed it. It had simply been left.

*Let her find it,* he thought. *Don't look.*

He looked at the wall. He counted stars. He got to forty, and lost his place, and started again, and somewhere in the seventies he heard her take it.

It was a very small sound. A rasp of teeth on leather. A quick wet intake of breath, and then the slow, steady, unmistakable rhythm of a child eating something she did not trust and could not stop. It went on for a long time. He did not turn his head. He had learned to be still at a fire, and in a tree, and in a stone circle, and in none of them had it been as hard as this.

At the door, Tamsin made a sound. It was barely a breath. He did not look at her either, but he knew without turning that she had put the back of her hand to her mouth, and that she was crying, with great decorum, in the manner of a clerk who has seen a figure at last add up.

---

He told the room that night, because there was nobody else.

The others were asleep or pretending to be, and the girl had gone back into the blankets with her fist wrapped round the last scrap of leather. The lamp was low. He sat with his back against the wall and his burned hand in his lap, and said into the dark, in a voice just above a whisper, the words that a man had said to him once on the first night, in a rusting survey-shelter, with a fire between them.

"I don't do charity," he said. "I want to be honest about that. I don't know how. I'm going to keep you fed. You don't owe me for it. I'm not going to ask you for anything." He stopped. "That's the terms."

It sounded odd. It sounded like a thing a man says to cover another thing. He sat with it a long while.

Varrick had said *terms, not charity,* and had spent nine years not meaning it, and had told the truth at last, with a rag at his mouth, by a fire. *Might have started as arithmetic. Didn't stay that way.* He had needed the word *terms.* It had been the only door his pride would go through.

Caelen found that he did not need it. He had only needed to say it once, out loud, to know what it was.

"That's not the terms," he said softly, to the dark. "I lied. There's no terms. I'm just going to stay."

From somewhere in the blankets, thin and high and wavering, a note answered him. He did not know what it meant. He knew it was not a scream.
