# Chapter Six — The Girl Who Learned to Wait

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Tamsin Orre — new to this series, and given an insert for the same reason Seraphina and Malakar were: the book needs her reasoning from the inside. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**TAMSIN**

She kept count of everything, and the count, on the morning the stranger first came up her stair, had been ninety-four.

It was not an important number. It was not a number the Ledger would ever want. It was simply how many days she had carried a tray up the fourth flight of the harbor stair and set it down on the boards by the door and withdrawn, as she had been taught, without speaking, with her eyes down, because that was how one approached a thing one did not understand. Ninety-four days. Two hundred and eighty-two trays. She had written every one of them in a little black book she kept inside her sash, in pencil, in a hand she had trained smaller and smaller until the page held a whole season.

*Day 61. Porridge. Refused. Egg. Refused. Tried humming the third-harbor lullaby. Hid.*
*Day 77. Brought a shell from the strand. Refused. Brought two. Refused.*
*Day 90. Read to her for an hour from the Tide-Book. She listened to the sound. Did not listen to the sense. Hid.*

She was twenty-two. She had been a tide-clerk for three years, a lamp-girl for five before that, and an orphan of the drowned for as long as she could remember. Her father's boat had gone down off the Teeth the winter she was seven, with all hands, and the harbor had taken her in the way it took in every such child: it gave her to the tide-house, where the dead were kept and counted, and told her that it was an honor. It had turned out to be one. She had learned to read the Ledger by the age of ten. She had learned to write it by twelve. She had learned, somewhere in the intervening years, that a person who is good at keeping a record of loss will be trusted with almost anything except doing something about it.

She had been the clerk on the rope.

That was the thing no one else in the harbor ever said aloud and everyone knew. The day the lamp-men broke into the seam at the Weeping Stair, it had been Tamsin Orre they lowered, on a hemp line, thirty-one fathoms into the dark, because the Ledger required a clerk to be present at the opening of any gallery and she was the one on duty. She had gone down swinging in the cold with her little black book between her teeth. She had seen the honey-colored stone come out of the wall in the lantern light, whole, smooth, and warm to the touch, with the shape of a small curled body inside it. And she had put her palm against it, because she was a clerk and it was unrecorded, and felt it crack.

She had been the first thing the girl saw.

That was not in the Ledger either.

---

"They'll want the shrine," Ibb said.

He had caught her on the stair, at the turn of the second landing, where she stopped most mornings to get her breath. Clerk-Superior Ibb was seventy, bone-thin, with a face like a sail left out in rain and a voice that had read the dead into the Ledger for forty years without once cracking. He was not a bad man. She had loved him, as a child. He had taught her her letters.

"The shrine, Superior."

"The Honey Chapel, at the head of the cliff road. It was built for exactly this, Tamsin. The first founders cut it for the Sleepers. Three hundred years it has stood empty, and now we have one, awake, and she is being kept in a *house.*" He said it as though the word were an obscenity. "The spring tide is eleven days off. The council is minded to agree. We hold the Naming, we give her a proper station, we put her in the chapel where the harbor may attend her."

"Attend her."

"With respect. With ceremony. As we do with everything that is holy."

"She's not holy, Superior. She's nine." She heard her own voice go thin and hard, and let it. "She's a child who hasn't eaten properly in three months and can't tell you why she's frightened. If you put her in a chapel with four hundred people singing, she will go to ground in the wall."

"You do not know that."

"I know that I carried the tray ninety-four days before he came." It came out louder than she meant. "I know that she screamed at a man who tried to be kind to her, and that no one has been in her room who wasn't afraid of her, or wanting something from her, or both. I know exactly what she needs. She needs nobody to do anything for a while."

Ibb looked at her, and for a moment she thought that he might simply say it, the thing he always said. *You are a junior clerk.* It stood ready on his face like a stamp.

He said, instead, very quietly: "That is what the stranger is doing."

"Yes."

"And it is working."

"It is the only thing that has." She swallowed. "I don't know why. I'd give a year of my life to know why."

"I do not like it," Ibb said, after a pause. "It has nothing in it I recognize. A man comes to a locked door and does not knock." He shook his head slowly. "But I have kept the Ledger forty years, and I have never seen it describe a thing that worked."

He went on up the stair. She stood with her back against the wall, shaking, and waited until she had stopped.

---

It was the strangest week of her life, and she wrote almost none of it down.

She had been prepared for the girl to remain a closed door. She had been prepared for the stranger to fail, and go home, and for the council to take it as proof. She had even, God forgive her, been prepared to be glad. She had made herself ready for that, in the dark, in the bed in the tide-house where she slept, with a resentment she had examined as honestly as she could: that a man from the great cities, with a famous name, could stroll into a room she had spent three months in, and be loved.

He had not strolled. He had burned his hand. He had sat on a bare floor for three days with a face like something carved, and she had stood in the doorway and watched, and had found it impossible to hate him.

What she could not forgive was that he made it look so simple.

The girl was out of the blankets now. That was the fact, and it stood in the middle of the room like a piece of furniture. On the sixth day she came out and sat, not near him, but in the same patch of window light, and put her palms on the sill and looked, for the first time, at the harbor. On the seventh she took a blanket from the nest and laid it over his knees while he slept. She did it with an expression of such stern, unexamined tenderness that Tamsin had to leave the room.

She had never done anything like that for Tamsin. She had never once taken the tray.

*Of course not,* Tamsin thought, on the stair, with her forehead against the cool whitewash. *You never stopped trying.*

It was a humbling thing to learn at twenty-two. She had spent three months being good. She had been patient, which she'd thought was the same as waiting. She had been gentle, and quiet, and reliable, and on time. She had brought the tray the same hour every day, set it in the same spot, withdrawn the same distance. She had been, she saw now, a perfect clerk. She had been the best record-keeper the harbor had ever trained. She had treated the girl as a thing to be tended and entered, in good order, and the girl had understood that perfectly, and had known the whole time what she was to the harbor. She was an *entry.*

The stranger had not entered her. He had just sat down.

---

On the ninth night, Caelen fell asleep sitting up.

It was the first time. He had gone three days on bread and water and cat-naps and the hard cold patience he carried like a coal, and on the evening of the ninth his head went back against the wall, and stayed. Tamsin came up the stair at the end of the day to relieve him, as she had every night, and stopped in the doorway.

The girl was sitting in the middle of the floor, cross-legged, in the moonlight from the window, with her back to the nest and her eyes on the sleeping man. She was humming. It was very low and very faint. It was not the wandering thread of the first days. It was a slow, even, careful tone, held and held, as if she were keeping a lamp alight.

Tamsin did not move.

She stood in the door with the tray in her hands, as she had stood ninety-four times, and she thought of Ibb, and the shrine, and the spring tide eleven days off. She thought of the black book in her sash, and of every pencil line. She thought, *I could enter this.* She could write it down. *Day 103. Subject observed to hum while the visitor slept.* It would be accurate. It would be a record. It would be precisely the kind of thing that a harbor with no idea what to do would read, and file, and put in a box.

She put the book away.

It was an act of such small, enormous, appalling disloyalty that her hands shook. She stood very still. She did not write. She did not speak. She sat down, slowly, on the boards just inside the door, a good ten feet from the girl, with the tray at her knee, and folded her hands in her lap, and for the first time in ninety-four days she did nothing at all.

The hum went on. After a while, she found that she was humming too. Not well. Badly, flat, in the key of a lullaby she had not thought of since she was six, a low tuneless thing that came out of her throat before she knew it was there. She stopped, appalled.

The girl's head turned.

She looked at Tamsin across the moonlit floor, with the strange green-gold eyes, and for a long moment neither of them moved. Then the girl lifted one finger, very slightly, and moved her own note up a half-step. Tamsin stared. She moved hers. Up. The girl nodded, a tiny, severe, unmistakable nod, the nod of a teacher with a slow pupil. She moved hers again.

It took Tamsin a full minute to understand that she was being corrected.

She laughed. It escaped her in a startled little gasp, and the girl's face did something unfamiliar, a slight tightening at the corners of the mouth, and Tamsin thought, with a wild disbelief, *she's smiling.*

Then the girl stood up, and crossed the floor with her light quick steps, and stopped in front of her. She looked down at the tray, at the bread and the cooling egg and the bowl with its skin of cold milk. She looked at Tamsin.

She picked up the bread.

She did not take it away to the nest. She stood where she was, in the moonlight, and ate it, crumb by crumb, with her eyes on Tamsin's face, as if saying, in the only language she had: *there. Now you know.*

Tamsin sat very still and let her.

A hundred and three days, she thought. It wasn't a number the Ledger wanted. She didn't write it down.
