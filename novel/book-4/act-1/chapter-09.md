# Chapter Nine — The Names

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Chairman Reth. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**RETH**

He did his sums at sea, as he did them everywhere, and by the twentieth day the number had stopped changing.

The Guild ship was called the *Tally.* It was a long grey three-master with no figurehead and no brass, built for survey work and cargo and the quiet movement of men with instruments, and it sailed with a steadiness that Reth found, as he found most of the Guild's works, both admirable and faintly oppressive. It had a cabin for the Chairman. He had asked for a smaller one, and been refused, and had made the best of it. He sat at the narrow table with the lamp swinging and his leather ledgers laid out before him, the public one and the private one, and let the pen go down the columns.

*Spring tally. Failures, confirmed.*

*A camp at Dunmere, east of the Wastes, fraudulent: a man who charged four families a season's wages each for a working he had copied from a broadside and could not perform. Two drifted past reach. One died.*

*A practitioner at Long Reach, honest, untrained, who attempted the full rite on a Shifter in the last hour of drift and held the anchor wrong. The Shifter bit through his own tongue. The practitioner has not spoken since.*

*A child, nine, in a barn at Greyfen, South Quarter. Mother licensed under the finding as a house-of-treatment keeper. Mother absent two nights. Child's drifting brother, fourteen, Shifted in the straw. The mother returned to find them both. The straw was still warm.*

He had sat with that last one for some time. He had gone to Greyfen himself, in April, on a mule, and stood in the barn in the damp and the quiet, and put his palm against the post where the boy had been chained by a well-meaning neighbor. Then he had gone home and written the entry in his small clear hand, in the plain black ink he used for all of them, and under *Disposition* had put the word *Unattended.*

It was the word that mattered. He had put it in the Chairman's book, in the public ledger, in the hearing's minutes. It was not a word he had invented. It was the word he had spent forty years learning to see, and it was the word the hall's finding, with all its goodness, had not made go away.

He did not think of himself as a man who was glad when the world proved him right. But he knew precisely how many pages he had left to fill.

---

He was not going to the Salt Coast for the child.

He wished to be exact with himself about that. His clerk, Tam, who was young and kind and had read the broadsides, had asked him on the second day, with the hesitancy of someone who expects to be wrong: *the girl, sir. You are going to see the girl?* And Reth had said, *I am going to see the harbor.* It was true. It was also the sort of true thing that, when he said it, made him feel as if he had rubbed a coin between his fingers and found the worn place.

He had learned about the girl as the whole territory had, from a broadside. He had learned about the harbor months earlier, from a report.

The report had come to his desk in the Spire on a grey morning in March, in the ordinary packet of survey returns that arrived from the coastal parties, bound in the Guild's plain grey card. It had been a dull report. He had nearly set it aside. It concerned a four-man party's reconnaissance of the Salt Coast cliffs, on the third harbor's headland, for the Board's long-range inventory. It listed positions, bearings, magnetic anomalies. Under *Findings* was a short note in a surveyor's cramped hand:

*Forty-one discrete seams of an unusual character along the headland, within a three-mile span. Resonance readings consistent with intact marrow-glass of high grade. Anomalous: each seam exhibits a steady low-frequency periodic pulse, not matching tide or any known source. Recommend detailed survey under Charter 9, with test charges.*

He had read it three times. He had gone cold in his chair.

Reth was a Guild man. He had been a Guild man for forty-four years. He knew what *test charges* meant, and he knew what a periodic pulse was, because he had seen a periodic pulse once, in a mess hall at Hollow Cut, in the hands of a boy named Deshane. He knew what happened to a thing that was warm and slow and alive when a charge of black powder was set against its wall.

He had stopped the survey. He had done it that same afternoon, by a letter in his own hand, under his own seal, to the Board's clerk of works. *Charter 9 test charges on the Salt Coast headland are suspended pending my review.* It had felt like a small thing. A suspension. A pause.

The letter had gone out in March. The *Tally* had sailed in June. And in the last week of May, a courier in the Spire had brought him a second packet, from the same four-man party, from the same grey office, with a note in the clerk of works' tidy hand.

*Suspension received. Be advised that the party's sealed orders under Charter 9, dated the fourth of February, antedate the Chairman's letter and, per Board practice, may not be countermanded except by the full Board in session. The Engineer-Captain has been informed.*

---

He understood the Board. That was the infuriating thing. He had given forty years to it and he knew it to the bone: the patient accretion of authority, the way it layered permission upon permission until no single voice could stop the thing that a hundred voices had once agreed to start. He had come to the Spire and built the first public register in the territory's history. He had shaken it to its foundations for ten days in the Hall of Canon. And he had gone home to learn that the machine was too old for even the Chairman to turn off.

He had one hand free. He had the *Tally,* a hall letter, a ship's worth of authority, and three weeks' sailing time behind a party of strangers who had gone out ahead of him on a hall vessel with Maren's seal. He did not hate them. He wanted to be clear about that, in his small cabin, with the lamp swinging. He had not hated the strata boy, who was a man now. He did not hate the archivist or the Inquisitor or the young noble with the mended cuff. He thought they were very brave and very probably wrong, and he had never in his life been so afraid of being right.

*Forty-one seams. A hundred and nine, if the surveyor's pulse count is accurate.* He had done the arithmetic, in the hall, in the spring, in this cabin. It was not complicated. A waking by blast killed. The archive was quite clear on it. Seven in three hundred years, and every one of them dead in weeks. There was nothing else in the whole sorry record to set against it, and a man did not need to be a mathematician to know that a test charge set in the headland of the third harbor would not leave one body alone.

That was the figure that had brought him to sea. A hundred and nine. He kept it in his head like a splinter.

---

Engineer-Captain Halse came to the cabin on the twenty-first night, as expected, to ask for the order.

He was a square, well-fed, careful man of forty with sandy whiskers and an expression of patient reasonableness, and he stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands and said, "Chairman. I wondered if you had reconsidered."

"The survey will wait, Captain."

"With respect, sir. The orders are the Board's. I hold them under seal. They were issued in February, before your suspension. I have a duty to the charter." He said it gently. "The weather is turning. We have a short season. If we do not begin the test shots in the next fortnight, the autumn gales will close the roads, and the work will be delayed a year."

"A year would do no harm."

"A year is a long time, sir, in a survey of this character." Halse turned his cap round once. "If I may say. I have read the surveyors' reports. I do not think we know what we have found. It may be the greatest reserve in the territory. It may be a hazard. It would be irresponsible to leave it uncharted."

Reth looked at him. He could see, as clearly as he had ever seen anything, the good man in front of him: orderly, conscientious, and quite sincere. Halse did not want to kill anyone. He wanted to *know.* He had the Guild's own deep conviction, which Reth shared and had shared all his life, that an unmapped thing was a danger and a mapped one was safe.

*That is the trouble,* Reth thought. *There is no villain in this. There is only a very large, very reasonable machine.*

"You will not fire," he said, "a single charge without my written order. Not a test shot. Not a sounding. Do you understand?"

"I understand the instruction, sir."

"That is not what I asked."

A pause. Halse's face did not change. "I have heard it, Chairman," he said, evenly. "I will convey it to the party."

It was, Reth knew, a refusal. It was the politest he had ever received.

---

He sat for a long time after the Captain had gone.

Then he drew the private ledger toward him, the small one with the cracked spine that he kept, even at sea, in the lining of his coat, and opened it to the last page. There was the column on the right, headed in his small young careful hand, and in it a short list of names.

*Roshe. Lisbet of Vael. A boy called Ned, at Long Reach, who had drifted and been treated by a woman who knew the rite and was never licensed and had saved him anyway.* Three names. In forty years, three. He looked at them for a long moment, and thought of a hundred and nine, and of the nine pages of the old Ledger that he had never seen and could picture perfectly.

He wrote the date. He wrote, beneath it, *The Salt Coast. Third harbor.* He did not write a number. He left the space.

In the first grey light of morning, the lookout cried the headland. Reth rose and went on deck in his plain coat, with the leather book in his hand, and stood at the rail. The coast came up out of the sea as a long black wall, banded with honey. He could see the white houses climbing it like a held breath, and, faint along the clifftop, in the dawn, a row of tiny yellow lights.

He stood there until the lights went out one by one as the sun reached them.

*Please,* he thought, to nobody. It was the first prayer he had made in forty years, and he did not finish it.
