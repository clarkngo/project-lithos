# Chapter Four — What the Fragments Never Said

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Seraphina. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**SERAPHINA**

They gave her the oldest volumes in a room of their own, and a lamp that did not smoke, and an apprentice who was told to bring tea and not to speak.

She had read in worse places. She had read in a reliquary's cold vault with a clerk's eye on her back, and in a stone cell with a guard outside, and in a dry hollow in the Wastes by the light of a fire that was dying. She had never read in a room that moved. The tide-house rose and settled on its stilts every few minutes, a long gentle swell under the floor, and the lamp-flame leaned with it, and the old paper smelled of salt. By the second hour she had stopped noticing. By the fourth she was breathing in time with it.

Vesh had been explicit. *You may read what you like. You may copy what you like. You may not take the books from this room.* He had not said why. He had stood in the doorway in his best coat with his spectacles pushed up into his hair, and said, in the voice of a man who is saying one thing so as not to say another, "I would like to know what you see in them. I have read them for thirty years. I believe I have stopped seeing."

So she read.

---

The Deep Ledger did not keep one account. It kept two.

The first was what the harbor wrote down for itself, in columns, for three hundred years: every body drawn from every gallery, with a date, a tide, a depth, a name where one was known. It was grim and orderly work, and she understood it at once. It was the work of a people who had decided that the dead would at least be counted. The cause-of-death column used a short, sad vocabulary. *Drowned. Crushed. Fever. Fall. Exposure.* She turned the pages and let the words go by her.

The second account was in the margins.

It took her an hour to see it. It was not a record. It was a mark: a tiny upright stroke, in black ink, in the outer margin of certain pages, grouped in fives with a slash across. There was no heading. There was no explanation. The strokes appeared on page after page of the oldest volumes, in a dozen different hands over a dozen generations, so small and so regular that she had taken them for a binder's guide. When she held a page to the lamp, she saw that the strokes had not been made at the same time as the entry beside them. They had been added afterward, in a different ink, as if by someone who came back.

She counted a page. Eleven. She counted another. Four.

She set down her pen, and the pencil, and the tea she had not touched, and sat with her hands flat on the old paper for the space of three tides.

*Not now,* she told herself. *Read the entries first.*

---

There were seven.

She found the first by the cause-of-death column, because it was the only one she did not understand. *Waking.* It stood among the drownings and the falls as plainly as any of them, a word in a clerk's careful hand, as if it were a fever or a fall or a kind of weather. She looked at it for a long moment. Then she went back through every volume, patiently, a page at a time, looking for the word.

There were seven.

The first was two hundred and ninety years back, in the first volume still legible: *A boy, nine or ten, from a seam at the Weeping Stair. Found in honey stone, by the lamp-men, at low water. Lived eleven days.* The second, ninety years after, was a girl of six. *Lived nineteen.* A boy. A girl. A boy. Two entries, in two different centuries, noted *unknown, perhaps twelve.* The longest of the seven, in the volume for the year of the Black Tide, was a girl called by the clerks only *the Stair-child,* who had lived, by the record, for forty-seven days.

She read that entry three times. It was longer than the others. It was written in a different hand, a shaky, upright, curiously tender hand, and it did not use the columns. The clerk had written it as a letter.

*The clerk Ysolt Marr has asked to be recorded as having sat with her. The council has declined. I record it here regardless. She was found at the Weeping Stair, awake, with her hand on the wall. She would not eat for the first nine days. On the tenth, Ysolt sat in the room and did not speak, and on the eleventh the child took bread from her hand. She did not speak. She hummed, and Ysolt hummed back, and they did this every day for a month. On the forty-seventh day she was quiet. The clerk Ysolt Marr was removed from the harbor's service for conduct, having been found asleep at the child's side, and has not been seen since. Cause of death: waking.*

Seraphina put down the page. She found that her hand was not quite steady.

*Waking.*

The other six had been alone. She went back over the entries and read them again, with a cold attention that she recognized, with a kind of horror, as her father's. Every one had been found by men with lamps and ropes, and taken up, and taken to a locked room and given food and water and the harbor's best regard. They had all been fed. They had all been warm. They had all been cared for by people who had no idea how to care for them. Not one of them had been sat with. They had gone quiet, one by one, over days or weeks, in rooms with painted ceilings and pretty locks, and the harbor's clerks had written down the word and put the book away.

---

"You've found the margin," Vesh said.

He had come in so quietly that she had not heard the door. He stood just inside it, in the dark blue coat, with his hands behind his back and his face in shadow.

"I've found the margin," she said. "I don't understand it."

"No." He crossed the room and sat in the second chair, stiffly, like a man lowering himself into cold water. "Nor do I. I have been the Keeper nineteen years. I inherited the habit with the office. When a sleeper is found, a mark goes in the margin of the oldest volume, beside the first page on which the gallery is mentioned. It is a custom. Nobody can tell me where it began." He looked at his hands. "I have made fourteen of them. I make them at night, when the others have gone. I do not know what they are for."

"How many are there?"

"I have never counted."

"Vesh."

"I *have* never counted, Archivist." The voice cracked, very slightly, and he steadied it. "I have counted everything else in this harbor. Every net, every keel, every body. I have never counted the marks. It seemed that if I counted them, it would be a thing I knew."

She said nothing. She sat with the old paper under her palms and the tide rocking the room, and understood that the man in front of her was frightened in a way she had not seen in a person since she had last looked in a mirror. He was not frightened of the girl. He was frightened of what the book would say if he asked it. He had spent nineteen years being a good steward of a record he could not bear to read.

*I know that,* she thought. *I know exactly what that is.*

"Seven," she said, gently. "Seven waking deaths. I think you know that. I think you've always known the number and never let yourself say it."

"Seven." He closed his eyes. "Yes."

"And the margin is more than seven."

"I —" He stopped. "I believe it is."

---

She copied the entries by hand, all seven. She copied the Stair-child's letter word for word, and when she had finished, she sat for a long time looking at it, with the lamp burning low and the apprentice asleep on a stool by the door.

She had spent three years learning what the Synod's fragments said. She had learned the tethering rite from half a thing: from one torn page in a reliquary nobody read, and a shard, and a boy's body in a stone circle. She had believed, in all that time, that the rite was a medicine. It had been the only word she had for it. A cure for a wound, or, since it never cured, a way to live with one. It held a drifting mind back from the edge by giving it an anchor, a steady living presence, banked and still, to orient against.

An anchor. She had never once asked what a mind would have to be doing to need one.

She laid the Stair-child's letter beside her own notes from the reliquary, the half-remembered fragment, the working's opening lines, and read them together. She read the words *a presence held still, while the sleeper finds the shape of the waking.* She had translated that line, in the cold vault, as a figure of speech.

*While the sleeper finds the shape of the waking.*

She sat up very straight.

She did not have it. She wanted to be very clear with herself about that. She had a pattern, and a pattern was not a proof. But she had a cause-of-death column in which a Synod's worth of silence had been written out as a single word. She had seven children who had been taken from the stone and loved, correctly and carefully and entirely wrongly. And she had a line from a fragment that had always read to her like a metaphor, and did not read like one anymore.

Everyone had been so busy looking at the ending, at the drift, at the dying, that no one had asked what the stone had been *for.*

---

She counted before she slept. She did it on her fingers in the dark of the guest house, with the tide moving under the foundations and Corwin snoring gently through the wall, because she could not make her mind stop doing it.

The longest of the seven had lived forty-seven days.

The girl in the whitewashed room had been awake since early spring. She had been awake, by the best reckoning anyone had, for thirteen weeks. That was ninety-one days. That was nearly double the longest recorded, in three hundred years of books. She had survived a month beyond the one child who had been sat with, in a room where nobody had sat with her, in a harbor that had done everything wrong with all the kindness in the world.

*Why?*

She lay in the dark, listening to a pulse she could not hear, and was afraid in a way she had not been since the night her father had said *returned to the mountains.* It was not the fear of failing. She knew that fear and could stand in it. It was a different thing. It was the fear that the clock which had run out for seven children had not stopped for this one.

It was only that nobody knew how long it ran.
