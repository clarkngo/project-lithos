# Chapter One — Twenty-Two Days

The sea had no ground to read, and by the third morning Caelen had stopped trying.

For ten years the land had answered him before he asked. The mine had, and the Wastes, and the old range with its patient seam. Even the pale country north of the Wastes had gone mute under his feet only a little at a time, and he had learned to walk it by eye like anyone else. The ocean did not go mute. It simply never spoke. It moved under the keel in long grey swells with nothing buried in them, and when he put his palm flat against the deck rail and reached for the shape of what lay below, he found only cold water going down and down and down, and a sense of enormous indifference.

"You're doing it again," Rook said, from the bottom of the companionway.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at the floor like you're waiting for it to say something." Rook was the color of old tallow. He had a bucket in one hand and a heel of dry bread in the other, and he held both with equal suspicion. "It's a boat, Caelen. It's wood. It's not going to tell you anything. It's going to go up and then it's going to go down, and I'm going to die in the interval."

"You're not going to die."

"Tell my stomach." He sat down heavily on the lowest step. "Years in the Wastes, and I thought I knew every way a body could be miserable."

Caelen crouched in front of him and took the bread out of his hand, broke it in half, and gave him back the smaller piece. Then he took a strip of dried meat from his coat and set it on the step between them, the way he had once been taught to set down an offer. Rook looked at it for a long moment.

"That's cheating," he said.

"It's eating." He waited. After a while Rook took the meat, and chewed it with the grim concentration of a man defusing something, and kept it down.

---

The ship was called the *Patience of Aldous*, a broad-hulled hall vessel with a patched mainsail and a captain who spoke to the weather as though it were a junior clerk. Maren had arranged it in four days, which Corwin said was either a miracle or a threat. She had put a sealed letter of standing in Seraphina's hand and a purse of the hall's silver in Malakar's, and had stood on the quay in the grey dawn with her robe slightly too large and said, "I will be wrong about a great deal while you are gone. I should like you to know that I intend to ask."

Orell had not come. He stood on the quay beside her with his arms folded, a big grey man in an apron somebody had forgotten to take off him, and said that the house at Kesh Hollow had nine Captains in it now, three of whom were not yet sleeping, and that someone ought to be there who had worn the uniform. He had gripped Malakar's forearm once, hard. He had said nothing at all to Caelen, and then, at the last moment, had said, "Tell the girl somebody on this side of the water is holding a bed for her." Caelen had not known what to say, and so had nodded.

Lisbet was at the Spire, running the house with the green door. She had written them a letter of her own, two lines long, in the small clear hand she had signed a witness roll with. *Come back. The kettle is on.* Corwin had it folded in his breast pocket and took it out on the second day to read it, and on the fifth, and on the ninth, with the air of a man checking that a limb was still attached.

He had never seen open water. He stood at the bow every morning in his good grey coat, with the green ribbon in his buttonhole going stiff with salt, and watched the horizon with an expression of such naked, undefended wonder that Caelen sometimes had to look away. "It doesn't *end,*" he said, on the fourth day, to nobody. "I knew it didn't end. I've read the books. I had not understood that it doesn't *end.*"

"You'll get used to it," said Malakar, behind him.

"I hope not," Corwin said.

Malakar was the surprise. He had been at sea precisely never, and had been braced, Caelen thought, for the same dry misery Rook had, with the same stoic Warden's resolve. Instead he had walked up the gangplank, set his hand on the rail, and gone entirely still. By the second evening he was up in the rigging with the first mate, being shown how a topsail was reefed, with the intent, meticulous delight of a man who has spent twenty years among stone and file cabinets and has just been told that there is another way to be in the world. He had stopped saying *precisely* and started saying *oh.* He had a blister on his palm and refused to have it dressed.

"I had thought," he told Caelen one night, on the quarterdeck, with the stars going out like lamps down the whole length of the sky, "that I had a very good understanding of the size of things. That is what an Inquisitor's work is. One learns to measure." He shook his head slowly. "I should like it noted that I have been entirely wrong."

---

Seraphina did not sleep either. They found each other, most nights, at the stern rail around the middle watch, where the wake spread behind the ship in a long white scar that closed over almost at once.

She had the Salt Coast letter with her. She had read it so often the creases had gone soft as cloth, and she kept it in her coat pocket, where the shard had once ridden and her father's letter after it, and took it out only to look at the signature.

"*Ondrei Vesh,*" she said, on the twelfth night. "Harbor-Master and Keeper of the Deep Ledger. A man who writes a request for help in the passive voice and signs it with every title he has." She smiled slightly. "I like him already. He is frightened and he has dressed for it."

"What do you think we'll find?"

"I think that I do not know." She said it simply, and it cost her something visible. "I have spent three years being the person who knows what the fragments say. It is a very odd sensation to sail toward a thing that I have not read." She turned the letter over. "*She does not seem to have a language.* Do you understand what that means? Not that she is silent. Not that she is frightened. That in all of her waking, nobody has found the thing in her that speaks."

"She spoke to me once," Caelen said. "In the mine. Before anything else."

Seraphina turned.

"Not in words. There wasn't any word. There was a pulse, and I knew it, and it knew me. That was all there was." He watched the wake close. "I don't think she'll have language, Seraphina. I think she'll have that. And I think the first thing anyone has done to her is try to teach her to be something else."

---

He lay awake, when the others slept, and listened to the sea.

It took him until the sixteenth night to understand what he was listening to. The tide did not run on this crossing, not in the shallow way it did at a harbor. But the swell had a rhythm under all its violence, a long slow rise and fall that came up through the keel and the planking and the bunk-frame into his spine, and it was too regular to be wind. It was a pulse. It went on and on, slow and patient, as it must have gone on since before there were any people to hear it. He lay in the dark with his palm on the hull and felt the whole sea breathing, and was nine years old, and curled tight in a warm dark that held him, and had a name that was a small stone.

He did not move for a long time.

On the eighteenth day he told Rook, who had by then graduated to keeping down a whole biscuit.

"You're afraid," Rook said, flatly.

"I'm — " He stopped. "Yes."

"Of what? She's a kid, Caelen."

"I know what she is." He had gone over it a hundred times, in a hundred versions of the dark. "That's the trouble. I know exactly what she is. She's what I was, and I don't know what to say to her. Varrick had something. He'd say *come down, eat,* and walk off, and I'd follow. But I've been trying to remember what it was, and it wasn't kindness. It wasn't a method. It was a man who didn't look back to see if I was coming."

"So don't look back."

"She isn't going to follow a stranger down a ridge. She's in a locked room."

"Then you're not Varrick." Rook shrugged, a tired, ordinary, brotherly gesture. "You've spent a year thinking you have to be him. That's the trap, I think. You've got to be what the person in front of you needs, and you don't know yet what that is. So don't pick. Go in. Sit down. Wait."

"That's all?"

"That's what he did." Rook picked a crumb off his sleeve. "You said it yourself. He left meat on a stone and he waited. You think that was *nothing?*"

---

On the twenty-first night the wind changed.

It came round to the south and brought a smell with it, brine and weed and woodsmoke and something under it that was warm and mineral and sweet, and Caelen came up on deck without deciding to. He stood at the rail in the dark. The others were already there, silent in a loose row. Corwin had his face turned into the wind like a dog. Malakar had one hand on a stay. Seraphina stood very straight with her coat pulled close.

There was nothing to see. There was a low line of black along the edge of the world, a shade darker than the sky, and over it, faint and scattered, a few lights that might have been stars or lanterns.

But the warm sweet smell was the smell of the shard, of the Vault, of the chamber under a mountain. It came across twenty-two days of open water and settled in the back of his throat, and he understood that she could smell it too. She had been breathing it for weeks in a room above a harbor. It was the only thing in the whole strange world that she knew.

*Come down,* he thought. *Eat.*

It was not a speech. He turned it over and could make nothing larger of it. It was a sentence a man had said once to a boy in a tree, with dry meat on a flat stone, and it had been enough to change a life.

He put his hand on the rail and watched the lights come up out of the dark, one by one, and did not look away, and did not look back.
