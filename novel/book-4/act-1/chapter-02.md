# Chapter Two — The Deep Ledger

The third harbor was built on a stair.

Caelen saw it from the deck as the *Patience* came round the headland in the grey first light: a black cliff dropping straight into green water, and cut into its face, ledge over ledge, a town of whitewashed houses that climbed from the quay to the clifftop like a held breath. There were no streets. There were flights of black stone steps, hundreds of them, zigzagging up between the roofs, and fishing boats tarred dark as seals bobbing along the foot of the wall. The whole place smelled of brine and wet rope and woodsmoke, and under it, faint and sweet, of the warmth he had tasted on the wind the night before.

The cliff itself was banded. At low tide, with the sea drawn back from its foot, the black rock showed long streaks of honey-colored stone running through it like veins in meat, some as thin as a finger, some as wide as a door. He stood at the rail with his hand flat on the wood and felt the whole headland hum.

"It's everywhere," Seraphina said quietly, beside him.

"Yes."

"No wonder they stopped counting."

Nobody greeted them with a cheer. That was the first thing. The *Patience* came alongside a stone quay thick with people, fishers and net-menders and women with salt-cracked hands, and they all stood very still and watched the gangplank go down. Nobody shouted. A child began to speak and was hushed. Caelen came ashore behind Malakar with his coat open and the empty brass housing at his wrist where anyone could see it, and the quiet that followed him down the plank had a shape, and the shape was care.

He had been looked at by strangers for most of a year now, on the Spire road and in the Hall of Canon, and he had learned the difference between wonder and the other thing. This was the other thing. An old man by a pile of lobster pots touched two fingers to a mark scratched on the quay stones, a little line with a notch cut in it, and then looked away. A woman gathered a boy against her skirt. Nobody was unkind. Everybody was watching what he did with his hands.

---

The Brine-Reeve met them at the foot of the first stair.

He was a broad man of sixty in a salt-stiffened coat, with heavy lids and a face like something the weather had been working on a long time. Two clerks in blue stood a pace behind him. He did not smile, but he bowed, exactly as far as the visitors' rank required, and said in a deep slow voice, "Hask. Brine-Reeve of the third harbor. You are welcome. You will find us an honest people." A pause. "We have had practice."

"I'm Caelen."

"I know who you are, sir." He did not look at Caelen's chest, and the effort of not looking was obvious. "The wool factor carried your broadsides up the coast in the spring. There is a song, I understand. It is not a good one." His eyes moved over the others, one by one, pricing nothing, missing nothing. "The Harbor-Master asked for you. I did not. I want that said plainly on the quay, in front of witnesses, so that nobody afterward can say the Reeve of this harbor lied to his guests."

"That's plain," said Malakar, in the voice he used for documents.

"Good." Hask stepped aside and gestured up the stair. "Then you may go up. Vesh is waiting. You will find the house at the top of the third flight set aside for you. It has a good fire." He paused. "It has a good lock, also. You will forgive that. We do not know you."

It was, Caelen thought, climbing, the most honest hospitality he had ever been offered, and the most frightening.

---

The tide-house stood at the top of the stair on stilts, with the sea running under it twice a day, and its door was low enough that Corwin had to stoop. Inside, it was a single long room with a floor of black oak and tall windows on three sides. It smelled of ink, and lamp oil, and damp paper. Along every wall, from floor to rafter, were shelves, and on the shelves, lying flat, were books.

They were bound in oiled sailcloth, hundreds of them, each as thick as a man's forearm, with a year inked on the spine in white. At the far end of the room a lectern held one open, and beside it, in a straight-backed chair, a spare man in a dark blue harbor coat sat with his hands folded on his knee, waiting.

He rose as they entered. He was perhaps fifty-five, thin as a rake, with spectacles on a cord and a close grey beard trimmed with great care. The coat had brass buttons, each one stamped with a tiny tally mark. He had dressed, Caelen saw, for an audience. He had put on his best coat and brushed it, and then, plainly, had been unable to stop his hands from shaking.

"Ondrei Vesh," he said. "Harbor-Master of the third harbor, and Keeper of the Deep Ledger. I wrote the letter. You are the archivist, I think." He bowed to Seraphina. "And you, sir, I will not guess."

"Caelen."

"Yes." Vesh looked at him for a long moment through the spectacles, and something moved across his exact dry face and was put away. "I should like to see the letter of standing, if you will permit me. I do not doubt it. It is a habit."

Seraphina gave it to him. He read it twice, with the patient suspicion of a man checking a lock. He turned it over. He held the seal to the light, and smelled the wax, and compared the hand on the outside with a card of something in his pocket. Then he nodded and folded it away. "The Eldest of the Hall of Canon," he said. "A Custodian named Maren. I had not heard the name."

"She has not held the chair long."

"It shows. The letter is very clear." He said it without sarcasm. "I find that clarity in new officers is a kind of fear."

He turned back to Caelen, and his voice dropped.

"I will ask one thing, and I ask you not to be offended. The broadsides say that you were once what is in my third harbor. That you were found in the stone."

"Yes."

"Say it aloud. In this room. Not for me." He gestured at the shelves. "For the record. The Ledger does not accept what it has not been told."

Caelen looked at the books, hundreds of them, spine on spine, and understood what this place was. It was a cemetery. It was a cemetery that had written itself down, a harbor's whole long grief in white ink, every body drawn from every cliff. He had stood at a lectern in a vault in the Spire and been asked to say his name. He had not expected to be asked, here, to say what he was.

"I was found in the stone," he said. "Ten years ago, in a mine, at the bottom of a strata the Guild hadn't mapped. I was nine. I'd been there as long as I could remember." He swallowed. "I don't know who put me there, or why. I'd like to find out."

Vesh nodded slowly. "Thank you." He took off his spectacles and wiped them on his sleeve, and when he put them back on, his eyes behind them were brighter than they had been. "I should like it recorded that I wrote the letter because the coast's doctrine had nothing left to try. I should also like it recorded that I would rather not have."

---

He gave them terms. Of course he did.

They sat at a long table under the windows, with salt fish and good bread and a pot of black tea, and Vesh laid them out as he might have laid out a tide table. No one was to leave the harbor without the council's knowledge. No instrument, no charge, no tool of the Guild or the hall was to be carried into the galleries. The girl would not be moved. The girl would not be taken from the third harbor under any circumstances, by any authority, for any reason, and if the visitors were not prepared to swear to that, they might take the *Patience* home that evening, with the harbor's thanks.

"We swear it," said Seraphina.

"You haven't heard the rest."

"I know what the rest will be." She said it gently. "It will be that she is a person. We were not coming to take her."

"There are people who come to take." Vesh poured the tea with great precision. "I have watched them, Archivist. The Guild came eight years ago with a survey. The Synod came twice with a clerk who wanted to see the Ledger, and left with a list of what he wished to keep. I do not accuse you. I am explaining why the Reeve has put a lock on your door." A thin smile. "I am not the Reeve's friend, in general. But I find that I cannot fault the lock."

"We understand terms," Caelen said.

Vesh looked at him for a while.

"I believe you do," he said. "It is an odd quality, in a man of your history. I had expected something else."

"What?"

"Gratitude," Vesh said. "Or hunger. Most of the people who come to us from the great places have one or the other." He stood. "Come. I will show you the entry. Then I will send you to your fire, and tomorrow you will meet the child."

---

The Deep Ledger of the third harbor was kept in a book of its own. Vesh took it from a locked case, not from the shelves, a thick volume bound in new sailcloth and not yet gone grey. He laid it on the lectern and opened it to a page near the back.

It was ruled in columns. A date. A tide. A depth, in fathoms. A gallery, with a name: *the Weeping Stair.* A condition, in the neat angular hand of a harbor clerk. And there, in the middle of the page, under the heading **Name,** a line.

The line was blank.

Caelen read the entry through twice. *Third harbor. Dark of the year, low water, spring tide. Weeping Stair, seam six, thirty-one fathoms. One body, living. Child, approx. nine years. Found sealed in honey stone. Condition: sealed.* Below it, in a different, smaller hand, a later entry: *Woke, early spring. Condition: awake. Does not speak. Hums.*

He put one finger on the blank line. The paper was warm. It had been warm from the lamp, he told himself. It had been warm from the room.

"I could not write her a name," Vesh said, quietly, behind him. "I tried. It seemed a theft. Every body that has ever been pulled from these cliffs had a name already, whether we knew it or no. The Ledger records what it is told. It does not make up names for the dead." He stopped. "She is not dead. I do not know what the Ledger does with her."

"What do you call her?"

"In the book, *the Sleeper.* The clerks call her the girl." A very small breath. "She does not answer to either."

Caelen stood a while longer with his finger on the blank line, and below him, through the black oak floor of the tide-house, the tide came in. He felt it come: first as sound, a long hush under the stilts, and then as something else. The whole building rose and settled on the swell like a ship at anchor. The pulse he had felt through the hull for twenty-two days came up through the floor and into his boots, slow, regular, vast.

And under it, so faint that he thought at first he had imagined it, there was another. Higher. Thinner. Somewhere in the white houses above them, a small hum reached down through the cliff and found him, and held his note for one long breath, and let it go.
