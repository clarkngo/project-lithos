# Chapter Eight — The Cliff Road

It was Malakar who noticed the lamps.

They had left the town at first light, the three of them, by a path that began behind the tide-house and climbed north along the clifftop, and Caelen had gone with them only because the girl had told him to. She had told him in her way, with a small severe push at his shoulder and a word she had been practicing for three days. *Go.* And then, very faint, with a look of fierce effort: *Back.*

"Back," he had said. "I'll come back."

Tamsin had stood in the doorway with the wooden horse in one hand and said, "I'll stay. You have my word." It was the first time he had left the room in nineteen days. He had walked down the stair with his hands feeling too empty, and Rook had taken one look at his face, said, "You look like a man leaving his dog at the vet," and linked an arm through his without another word.

The cliff road was good. That was what he noticed first. It was packed shell and gravel, edged with white stones, drained by little stone gutters every twenty paces, and swept. Someone swept it. In a harbor where nothing was wasted, where the nets were mended till they were mostly mending and the boats were tarred till they were mostly tar, someone had taken the trouble, for what looked like generations, to keep a footpath along the top of a cliff clean enough to walk barefoot.

"Who uses it?" he asked.

"Nobody," said Malakar, who had been counting under his breath for a quarter of an hour. "That's the difficulty."

He stopped and pointed down.

---

They were small. That was the reason it had taken so long to see them. Set into the face of the cliff at irregular intervals, just below the lip, each in a niche of its own cut into the black rock, were little iron lamps. They were no larger than a man's two fists. They had glass fronts and tin hoods against the weather, and each one was lit. In the broad morning light the flames were almost invisible: a faint steady yellow, not wavering in the wind.

"There's one every — " Rook leaned out carefully, one hand on the wall. "Hell, there's *hundreds.*"

"Forty-one," said Malakar. "I have been counting since the headland. They are not evenly spaced. They cluster. There are six in the first half-mile and then none for three-quarters of a mile and then eleven, very close, in the dip beyond the old signal-post." He rubbed his jaw. "And they are all lit. At the third hour of the morning, with no one on the road. Somebody comes every night and trims them, and somebody comes every morning to check."

"A lighthouse?"

"A lighthouse points outward. These point in." He shook his head slowly. "I spent twenty years learning to read what a building is for from where the doors are. A person who lights a lamp facing the rock is not signalling to anyone. They are keeping something company."

Caelen looked down at the nearest. It was set into the cliff a body's length beneath his feet, an iron hood, a yellow flame, and behind and beneath it, a long vertical band of honey-colored stone, glowing very faintly in the sun.

He lay down on his stomach on the gravel, and put his palm flat on the edge, and leaned.

---

The cliff sang.

He had expected the pulse. He had been feeling it for nineteen days, in the town, in the harbor, in the long swell of the tide in the foundations of the house. But he had not been standing at the edge of it. Lying there with his arm over the drop and his cheek against the gravel, he felt the entire face of the headland as a single living thing. It was warm. It was slow. It was a thousand heartbeats at once, all very faint, all just slightly out of step with each other, like a field of grass moving in a wind. And beneath the beat, in his chest, came the thin high note of something else, something he had learned to know in a whitewashed room.

They were *humming.*

Not aloud. Not for any ear but his. A low continuous murmur, from every lamp, from every niche, from every band of honey stone along three miles of cliff, rising and falling together with the tide. He knew it. He had heard it in the dark under a mountain, in the first minute of his life. It was the sound a body makes when it is held.

"Caelen," Rook said. "Caelen. Your hands."

He looked. They had lengthened. Not much, a half-inch, the nails dull and thick. He could not feel it. He was crying, and it took him a moment to understand that, too.

"They're asleep," he said, in a voice that did not sound like his. "They're all — down there. There's one right below me, I can feel her. She's small. She's curled up. She's *dreaming.*" He pressed his palm harder against the rock. "There are people in the cliff, Rook."

Neither of them answered. Malakar had gone grey. Rook put one hand on Caelen's back, quite lightly, the way a person lays a hand on a horse that has begun to shake, and kept it there.

---

They found the first of the keepers a mile on.

She was sitting on an upturned crate in the lee of a drystone wall, an old woman with a face like folded leather and white hair cut straight across at the jaw, mending a net. She did not look up when they came along the road. She had a bone needle in her fingers, and a small tin oil-can at her feet, and a ring of keys at her belt, and she waited for the shuttle to pass through the mesh before she said, in a perfectly level voice:

"You've seen, then."

"We've seen," said Malakar.

"Good." She tied off the knot. "I'd rather you saw it from the road than found it by accident. We've had accidents." She lifted her eyes. They were pale and shrewd and not in the least afraid. "I'm Nessa. This is my stretch, from the signal-post to the dip. My mother had it before me, and hers before that. I trim twenty-two lamps. I've never missed a night."

"Twenty-two," Caelen said.

"There's sixteen sleepers in my stretch, give or take. They don't all have a lamp. The big pockets have two." She said it as another woman might have said she kept hens. "We call ourselves the lamp families. There's nine. It's not a thing we say in the town."

"Why do you do it?"

She looked at him for a long time, and her face changed.

"So they're not in the dark," Nessa said.

It was so simple that nobody spoke.

"I know it's foolish," she said at last. "They can't see a lamp through two fathom of stone. My mother told me that. But she told me that her mother said, and hers before, that you don't leave a sleeper in the dark. You give them a light, and you sit by them, and you tell them the tide. It's a promise, you see. Nobody wrote it down. The first founders made it, when they found the first of them, and said, *we'll not wake what we can't keep.* So we keep. We light the lamps. And we wait for the world to be ready." She looked down at her net. "It's been three hundred years. I don't think the world is ready. I think it's just very big, and very late."

---

The Brine-Reeve found them at dusk on the way back.

He came along the cliff road alone, on foot, in the salt-stiffened coat, and stopped a few paces off with the lamps coming on behind him one by one in the blue air. He did not look surprised. He looked, Caelen thought, like a man who has watched a house burn for a long time and now sees the roof go in.

"Nessa told me," Hask said. "She sent a boy."

"We weren't hiding it."

"I know you weren't. You walked along the open road. I should have posted a man." He paused. "I did not, because I thought: if they find it, they find it. Better by their own eyes than by a clerk's mouth." A heavy breath. "You understand why there is a lock on your door."

"Forty-one," Malakar said. "How many are there?"

"Sleepers? A hundred and nine, by the Ledger." Hask turned and looked down the long gravel road, shining faintly now in the last light, with its little yellow lamps burning in the dusk like a string of sparks. "I know the number because I have been Reeve twenty-six years and my father was Reeve before me and he told me, in his bed, on the last night. He said, *Hask. We are not the owners of this. We are the keepers of the door.* I did not understand him." He rubbed his face with one broad hand. "I think I do now."

"The Guild's been here," said Malakar.

It was not a question. Hask's head came up sharply.

"The stakes," Malakar said. He pointed up the cliff, at the clifftop above the nearest lamp, where Caelen now saw them: a row of short grey-painted posts driven into the turf at regular intervals, each topped with a little tin flag. "Those were not here in the spring. The paint is fresh. The holes have not weathered. Somebody has surveyed the length of this cliff, from the headland to the signal-post, in the last six months, and has taken some care to look like a fishery inspection."

"A Guild vessel anchored in the roads at the thaw," Hask said slowly. "Four men ashore, with brass instruments. We took them for the Salt Office. They said they were measuring the strand for a tariff." He stopped. "They were very polite."

"They always are," Malakar said.

Hask looked at the posts. Caelen watched his broad weathered face as the understanding went through it. Twenty-six years, his father's last words, the lamps, the swept road; and an afternoon, in the spring, when four polite men had walked the clifftop with their theodolites and gone home.

"They counted them," he said.

"Yes."

"They *counted.*" His voice cracked, and he got it back. "They have been in my harbor and counted my dead."

"They're not dead," Caelen said.

Hask turned to him, and for the first time looked him squarely in the face.

"No," he said, very quietly. "I know. That is the whole trouble."

---

Vesh unrolled the chart on the tide-house table that night, by lamplight, with Seraphina at his shoulder and Tamsin in the doorway. It was a cliff-section, drawn in ink on oiled linen, in a hand nearly three centuries old, with a tiny cross at every pocket. He did not need to be asked. He ran a finger down the length of it, silently, and laid beside it the oldest volume of the Ledger, open at the first page, with its black marks standing in the margin like a row of little fence-posts.

Seraphina counted them. It took a long time. She did it twice. When she had finished, she said the number aloud, in a voice so quiet that the tide almost covered it.

"A hundred and nine."

"Yes," said Vesh.

"The marks are the sleepers."

"Yes." He took off his spectacles, and wiped them, and did not put them back on. "I have counted everything in this harbor but them. I think I was afraid I would find that I had been keeping a nursery." He looked at Caelen. "I have been the Keeper of the Deep Ledger for nineteen years. I believed I was keeping the dead."
