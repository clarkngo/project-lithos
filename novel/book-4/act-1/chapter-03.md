# Chapter Three — The Honey-Colored Stone

The girl had been given the highest room in the harbor, and Caelen understood why before he reached the top of the stair.

It was the only room in the whole town that the tide could not reach. The house stood at the head of the fourth flight, a narrow whitewashed building leaning against the cliff like a tired man against a wall, and its single upper window looked out over the roofs and the green water to the open sea. Someone had thought about that. Someone had decided that a child pulled from thirty-one fathoms of dark ought to wake to a view.

"It was the Reeve's idea, the room," said Tamsin Orre, climbing ahead of him with one hand trailing on the wall. "He's not a cruel man. I want you to know that before you decide what you think of us. He's a careful one, and he took one look at her and put her where he could see the harbor and the harbor could see her."

"Can she see it?"

"She doesn't look." She stopped on a landing and turned. "She doesn't look at much. She looks at the walls, mostly. She puts her hands on them."

She was twenty-two, he guessed, or nearly. She had a tide-clerk's blue sash tied over a plain grey dress, the knot too tight, the ends frayed, and ink-stain to the second knuckle on both hands. Her hair was a short dark braid with a pencil stuck through it. She had a narrow, freckled, stubborn face, and she looked at Caelen the way Rook had looked at him at the stall in Hollow Cut: straight on, and not at all impressed. He liked her at once, and he was wary of that.

"The others will wait below," she said. "Vesh was clear. It's you, and it's me, and the door stays open. If she goes under the bed, you don't go after her. If she makes the sound, you don't speak. And if she comes toward you —" She hesitated. "I don't know what to tell you, if she comes toward you. She hasn't, with anyone."

"Not even you?"

"Not even me." A small hard breath. "I've been three months on that stair. I carry up the tray. She's never once taken it from my hand."

---

There was a bar across the outside of the door. Someone had wound blue ribbon around it, to make it look like a decoration, and it only made it worse.

Tamsin lifted it down without a word and propped the door wide with her hip, and Caelen stood at the threshold and looked into the room.

It had been made, with enormous care and no idea, into a nursery. The walls were washed pale yellow. Someone had painted stars on the ceiling, a hundred of them, gold on blue, with one of the clerks' careful numbers inked beside each. There was a small bed with a patchwork quilt, and a carved wooden horse with a missing ear, and a low shelf of picture books in four languages, none of which had been opened. A tray of food stood on the floor by the door, bread and boiled egg and a bowl of something warm, untouched, with a thin skin on it. The window had a lattice across it, pretty, wrought iron in the shape of waves. It was a lock.

The room was empty.

Then he saw the nest. In the far corner, under the window, every blanket in the house had been dragged into a heap and packed down hard against the wall, with a gap at the top just wide enough for a small body. And on the walls above it, in a wide scatter at the height of a small child's reach, were handprints.

They were pressed into the whitewash. They had not been painted; there was no color to them. They were smooth, shallow depressions, like prints in clay, as if the plaster itself had softened for a moment and taken the shape of a palm and then hardened again. Dozens of them. They were slightly browner than the wall around them. When he stepped nearer, he felt it before he saw it: heat, a faint steady warmth, coming off each one.

"She does it when she's frightened," Tamsin said, from the door. "Or lonely. I can't tell which."

He did not answer. He had stopped three paces from the nest. His heart was going hard and slow. A hum had started in the room, so soft that he took it first for the sea, the long tide-pulse under the whole town. But it was higher than the sea and it was not steady. It rose and fell, a small, thin, wandering note that wound through the blankets like thread through cloth.

He had last heard it in the dark under a mountain. He had been inside it.

*Sit down,* he thought. *Wait.*

He sat.

He did it slowly, cross-legged on the bare boards with his hands loose on his knees, a good arm's length beyond the edge of the nest. He did not look into the gap. He looked at the wall, at the nearest of the handprints, and let his breath go long.

---

For a long time nothing happened. The hum wound on, soft, uneven. The tide ran in under the house. A gull went past the window and its shadow swept across the quilt. Caelen did not move. His left knee had begun to ache and he let it.

Then the blankets shifted.

It was a very small movement. A fold sagged and rose. A single hand came out of the gap, the fingers long and fine, the nails cut short and ragged, and rested on the floor, and he saw that the skin of it was faintly honeyed, a tint under the ordinary brown, like candlelight through a cup. The hand stayed there a while. Then it drew back.

He still did not look. He heard her breathing now, quick and shallow, and the faint rasp of wool on wool, and then a long, long stillness.

When she came out, she came out low.

She came out on all fours, fast, pressed to the floor like something that expected to be struck, and stopped halfway across the boards with her weight on her hands. She was small. She was smaller than he had imagined, thin as a fence-rail in a grey smock somebody had cut down from a man's shirt, with her hair hacked short around a face that was all eyes. They were very large, and they were not dark. Even in the pale daylight from the window they held the light and threw it back, a faint green-gold sheen like a cat's in a doorway.

Caelen made himself breathe. The old animal in him said *run,* and the old animal in him knew exactly what she was seeing, because he had been there.

She did not look at his face.

He had braced for that. He had told himself it would be the first thing, the hard bright assessing stare of someone deciding if a stranger was safe. But her eyes went past his face as if it were weather. They went to his chest.

They stayed there. Her head tipped, a quarter-inch, then another. Her nostrils flared. The hum in her throat, which had never stopped, changed.

---

It was not a thing he did. He would be sure of that afterward, lying awake, going over it. He had not reached for the coal. He had not lit it. For years he had held it banked and sullen behind his sternum, and he held it so even now, unlit, the heat of a stone left out in the sun.

But it answered her.

He felt it stir. He felt a small, bright, wordless thread of heat run up out of the bank like smoke from an ember, and the note in her throat and the note in his chest met. They did not meet like a sound and an echo. They met like two hands. For one long breath they held the same pitch, hers thin and high, his deep, a perfect octave apart, and the whole whitewashed room seemed to ring. The handprints on the wall brightened. Beside the nest, the floorboard she was touching began, very slowly, to warm.

Then she moved her note. A half-step, no more. And his followed, as though she had pulled a string.

*She's leading,* he thought, with a cold drop of understanding. *She isn't answering me. She's testing what I'll do.*

She moved it again, and he followed again, and a third time, a small deliberate dance of tones, and somewhere in the third he understood what was happening, and it was worse than anything he had braced for. She was not finding out whether he was friendly. She was finding out what he was. She was reading the banked coal, the hunger beneath it, the long cold history of every Shift it had paid for, the way a doctor reads a pulse. She could feel all of it. Everything he had spent ten years learning to hold behind his teeth, she had picked up in one breath, as if it were a smell.

*She knows.*

She knew what he would become if he let go. She knew it the way he knew the shape of the dark in a flooded mine. And she was sitting there on the boards, ten feet away, with her eyes on his chest and her head tilted, humming it back to him.

He did not move. His palms were wet. The coal behind his sternum, which he had banked every hour of every day for a decade, pulsed once, hard, and he held it.

"Caelen," Tamsin said, very low, from the door. "Your hands."

He looked down. His fingers, lying on his knees, had lengthened by half an inch. The nails were gone grey and thick.

He closed his eyes. He did not reach for the stone of his name. He did not need to. He thought of a man's unhurried boots, and of a strip of dried meat on a flat rock, and of nothing at all, and let the heat sink back down into the bank, a breath at a time, until his hands were only hands.

When he opened his eyes again, she had come three feet closer.

She was sitting on her heels in the middle of the floor with her palms flat on the boards, and her eyes were no longer on his chest. They were on his face. They were the strangest eyes he had ever seen, green-gold and wide and utterly without fear, and it came to him with a slow, enormous clarity that she had not been afraid of him, in the whole of that long exchange. She had been afraid of the room.

She tilted her head the other way. She hummed one clear note, higher than before, and held it.

It was a question. He had no words for it, but it was a question, and he knew the answer, because he had been waiting twenty-two days to say it.

*Yes,* he hummed back, with his closed mouth, in his chest, as he'd once hummed in the dark. *Me too.*
