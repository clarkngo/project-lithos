# Chapter Seven — Names for What She Is

The first word she learned was *stay*, and he had not meant to teach it.

It happened on the twelfth morning. He had gone down the stair at dawn to wash and eat something that was not bread, and when he came back up, the door was open and she was standing in the middle of the room with her arms stiff at her sides, rigid as a post, and the nest behind her torn half-apart. She had not cried. She had not made the sound. She had simply stood and waited, with the dreadful patience of a creature that expected to be left, and when he came in she looked at him and her whole body let go.

"I'm back," he said. He crossed the floor slowly and sat. "I'm here. I'll go and I'll come back. I'm not going. *Stay.*"

He had not planned the last word. It was simply the shortest one he had. She tilted her head, and her mouth moved, and a sound came out that was not quite a sound. It was a shape, a held breath with an edge on it. *Sthhay.*

He stared at her.

"Again," Tamsin said, from the door, in a whisper. "Say it again."

"Stay."

*Sthhay.* Her lips pulled back from her teeth as if the shape tasted odd. She did it a third time, and a fourth, and by the fifth it was nearly a word, thin and hoarse and breathy and entirely her own, and she had her hand pressed flat to the floor between them as if to hold it in place.

He looked away so she would not see his face.

---

It was how he had been taught, and he had forgotten that he remembered.

Varrick had not sat him down with a book. He had not drawn pictures or made lists. He had put an Aethel-Core in Caelen's palm and said, *this is marrow,* and let the weight of it do the work. He had named a thing while it was heavy in the hand. He had said *conduit* with the brass cold against his own wrist. He had never once said a word in the abstract. A word was something you held.

So Caelen taught her the same way, with his hands.

*Bread* came on the thirteenth day. He broke a heel of the harbor's good rye in two, and put half in her palm, and said it, and she said it, and tore it with her teeth. *Cold* was the window glass. He laid her hand against it in the early morning and held his own beside it, and she snatched hers away with a hiss, and then, slowly, put it back. *Hot* was the porridge bowl. He turned his burned palm over so that she could see the white scar across it, the curve of the rim, and pointed to his hand, and then to the bowl, and said it, and he saw her understand.

That was the hard one. She sat looking at his palm for a long time. Then she reached out and, very carefully, with one finger, touched the edge of the scar.

"I'm sorry," he said. He didn't know whether she would understand, and he said it anyway.

She said nothing. She stroked the scar once, from end to end, with the pad of her finger, as a person might stroke a sleeping animal. And she hummed. It was not the thin wandering thread of her first days. It was a short low note, almost a murmur, and he felt it in the burn like a cool hand laid on it. The skin did not heal. But the ache went out of it, and stayed out of it for the rest of the day.

He did not say anything about that to Tamsin.

---

It went like that for a week. He learned that she would take a new word best in the morning, when the room was warm and the tide was out, and that she hated being asked. She hated pointing games. She hated flat objects held up and named, and she hated the picture books, which she regarded with a stony contempt that Tamsin found hilarious. She would learn a thing only if it was *done.* *Open* was a door. *Close* was the same door. *Wet* was the washing basin, a lesson that ended with all three of them soaked to the elbow. *Mine* he did not teach her. She taught herself, around the second week, by taking the wooden horse with the missing ear and carrying it everywhere with an air of such ferocious proprietorship that nobody dared to touch it.

Rook came on the sixteenth day. Caelen had asked him not to, and he came anyway, and sat on the top stair outside the door with his elbows on his knees and a paper twist of candied peel in his fist, and did not come in.

"She's looking at me," he said, in an undertone, after a quarter of an hour.

"She does that."

"Like I'm a loaf."

"She's never seen anyone with a face like yours."

"I have a great face." But he held out the peel, flat on his palm, on the threshold, and sat there with his hand out for twenty minutes without once complaining. At the end of it the girl crossed the floor, quick and light, and took one piece, and sniffed it, and ate it. Then she looked at Rook with unmistakable severity and said, "Bread."

"No," Rook said. "That's peel."

"Bread."

"It's *peel,* you little —" He stopped. His face did something unguarded, a crumpling, and he put the heel of his hand to his eyes. "Oh, hell. Hell, Caelen. She said it. She said a word to *me.*"

"I know."

"It's the wrong word."

"It's the right one," Caelen said, softly. "It means she wants more."

---

He taught her the cliff on the nineteenth day.

He had not meant to do it. It was a window-lesson, nothing more, a quiet morning with the harbor spread out below like a toy: the roofs, the green water, the tarred boats nodding at the foot of the wall. He had pointed to the fishermen and said *men,* and to the gulls and said *birds,* and she had repeated them with her new hard breathy care. Then she had leaned out over the sill, farther than he liked, and stopped.

The tide was out. The cliff stood bare to its root in the pale light, black rock streaked top to bottom with long honey-colored bands, glowing faintly where the sun came through them. She was looking at it with her whole body. Her breath had stopped. Her hand, on the sill, had gone white.

"That's stone," he said, gently. "*Stone.*"

She did not answer. She had gone entirely still, the way she had on the first day, with her eyes on his chest, except that now they were on the cliff. She stared at it for a very long time. Then she turned her head and looked at him, and he saw in her face something he had no word for in any language, a dawning, terrible, unclouded comprehension, as of a child who has just seen, for the first time, the shape of the thing she came out of.

He crouched beside her.

"That's what held you," he said. He kept his voice low. "That's what you were in. You, and me. We were in the stone. Do you understand? Me. *Me.*" He put his palm flat against his own sternum. "Caelen. Caelen."

She watched his hand. She put her own palm on her chest, a smaller copy of the gesture, and made a face, a small, helpless, furious grimace of someone who has just found out that the world has a missing piece.

"You don't have one," he said. "Yet. That's all right. I had one. I kept it. I held onto it in the dark, all by myself, because it was the only thing that was mine." The words came out of him slowly, one at a time, each one costing him something. He was not sure he was speaking to her anymore. "It was a stone. It was small and warm. I carried it for ten years. I'm telling you, because I think somebody should have told me it was allowed to be yours."

The room was very quiet. The tide began to turn under the house.

She put out a hand. She laid her palm on his chest, over the coal, and he did not flinch. She pressed. Her eyes closed. And she hummed, a long, slow, searching sound that went down into him as the sea had gone down under the *Patience,* through the planking and the bone.

It was not the note she had been giving him for days. It had a shape in it. It rose, and bent, and rose again, and he felt it close around something in his breast that had no name. A word. He knew it was a word. It was not a word he knew. It was not from the harbor tongue, or the Spire's, or the Wastes'. It was older than any of them, and it had come up out of somewhere beneath language, like the first breath of a body that has been under water a long time.

He did not know what it meant. But he felt what it did. It was a hand at the back of his neck. It was a door left open. It was the voice of a thing that had been alone in the dark and was no longer.

When she opened her eyes, they were wet.

---

He told Seraphina that night, in the guest house, over a cold supper neither of them wanted.

She listened with her whole face. When he had finished she took a stub of pencil from her coat and a sheet of paper, and asked him to hum it. He tried. It came out wrong; it would not hold in his mouth. She asked him again, and again, until she had a rough scrawl of curves on the page, like the track of a bird in sand.

"I don't know it," she said, at last. "I've read every Lithoi fragment the reliquary held, and several that it didn't. I have never seen a sound like this written down." She turned the page toward the lamp. "But I think it is tonal, Caelen. Not music. A word that depends on pitch the way ours depends on vowels. Which means that it has a grammar. Which means that she has a language."

"She said she didn't."

"Vesh said she didn't. He was wrong." Seraphina's eyes were very bright. "She never lacked words. She lacked anyone who could hear them."

He looked at the paper. He had spent ten years of his life believing he was alone in the dark. He had been a fool, and it did not matter. He could feel her, even now, in the white house above the harbor, humming his note back to the sleeping town, and for the first time he thought that perhaps the question his life had been asking was not *what am I.* It was *who else.*
