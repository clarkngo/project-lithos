# Chapter Ten — The Name She Chose

They came for her at dawn on the morning of the second spring tide, and they came with a bell.

Caelen heard it from the guest house before he was fully awake: a clear cold chime, struck slowly, once for every step of the stair. It was the tide-bell, the one the harbor rang for the drowned. He was on his feet and dressed before the third stroke, and when he reached the white house at the head of the fourth flight, the procession was already waiting outside the door.

There were twelve of them in the blue sashes of the tide-house, in two ranks with a gap between, and at their head, thin and upright in the grey light, stood Clerk-Superior Ibb. He held a small iron hand-bell in front of him like a candle. Behind the clerks, at a courteous distance, stood the council, and behind the council, filling the stair and the roofs and the narrow lanes and clinging to the cliff like barnacles, stood what looked to Caelen like the entire town.

"This is the second tide," Ibb said. He did not raise his voice. "The Naming was deferred once, by the Reeve's ruling, to give the stranger time. The time has been given." He bowed, slightly, to Caelen, with real courtesy and no apology. "I would not do this at the door. But the chapel is cold, and the tide will not wait."

"She doesn't want to go," said Tamsin.

She stood in the doorway of the white house with her back to the room and her hands wrapped in her sash, small and furious and shaking. She had not slept. She had a smear of ink on her cheek.

"She is nine years old, Tamsin Orre. She does not know what she wants."

"She knows exactly what she wants." Her voice cracked. "She wants everyone to stop wanting things from her."

Caelen looked past them at the crowd. He saw Hask in the front rank, grim and still, with a face like an old stone. He saw Vesh, in the blue coat with the tally buttons, white to the lips. He saw Seraphina, Malakar, and Corwin, standing together on the third landing; and Rook, who had pushed through to the door and stood at Caelen's shoulder, saying nothing, with one hand loose at his side and the other resting on the hilt of a knife he had promised not to draw.

He did not know what to do. That was the plain truth of it. He had walked out of a mine, and a forest, and a burning lane, and he had never in his life been asked to stand between a child and a whole town that loved her.

*Don't decide for her,* he thought. *That's the only thing you know.*

He turned round.

---

She was sitting up in the middle of the floor with the wooden horse in her lap, wide awake, in the grey smock, with her short hair standing up on one side. She had heard the bell. He could see that. She had heard every stroke of it, and she was holding herself with a stillness he knew, the stillness of an animal that has already decided what it will do if it cannot get away.

He crossed the floor and sat down in front of her. He didn't say anything for a while.

"They want to take you somewhere," he said, finally. He chose the words with great care. He used the ones she had. "Big house. Many people. They want to give you a name."

*Name.* She mouthed it, soundlessly. Her eyes moved to his chest and then, slowly, up to his face.

"I don't want you to go," he said. "I want you to stay. But I don't — " He stopped. He took a breath. "It's yours. It isn't mine to say. If you go, I'll come. If you stay, I'll stay. If you want to run, I'll —" He couldn't finish that one. "Whatever you choose. It's yours."

She looked at him for a very long time.

Then she stood up, put the wooden horse down carefully on the quilt, and walked past him and out onto the threshold of the white house, where the clerks and the council and the whole silent harbor could see her.

---

It was so quiet that he could hear the gulls.

She stood there in her cut-down smock in the first level light, so small against the great pale cliff that she looked like a mark made by a pencil. The tide was at its height. Below the stair the green water slapped at the foot of the wall. A thousand faces were turned up to her, and none of them made a sound.

She looked at them. She looked, with great deliberation, from one face to the next, as though counting. Ibb with his bell. Hask with his heavy stone face. Vesh, holding himself together by the buttons of his coat. Her eyes rested on each one for a moment, and moved on.

Then she looked at the cliff.

He felt her do it. He felt it in his own chest, a sudden drop in the air, as though the whole town had breathed out. She tilted her head back, and the green-gold light came into her eyes from the stone. And she opened her mouth, and sang.

---

It was a single note.

It was not loud. It was low, and clear, and absolutely pure, and it began somewhere at the bottom of her chest and rose. It rose through the white house and the stair and the roofs, through the iron lamps in their little niches, and out over the water. It did not waver. It held, perfectly level, for a breath, and then two, and then it bent. It turned over on itself, once, like a wave, and climbed. It rang out against the face of the cliff, and the cliff answered.

He heard it come back. He heard all of it. The honey-banded stone along three miles of headland took up the note and gave it back to her, a thousand voices, a hundred and nine, deep and soft and slow. Every lamp along the cliff road flared at once, a long bright ripple of gold like the lighting of a string. In the street below, a dog began to howl and was silenced. A fishwife dropped her basket. On the third landing, Corwin made a small broken sound and put both hands over his face.

She held the note until she had no breath left. Then she let it fall, and the cliff held it a moment longer, and let it go.

In the silence afterward, she turned and looked at him. Her face was wet. She was not smiling. She was entirely, solemnly sure.

*That,* her eyes said. *That's the one.*

---

"Did you hear it?" Tamsin said.

She was whispering. She had come across the floor in a kind of daze and stood at his elbow with her black book out and the pencil in her trembling hand. "Did you hear it? Caelen? It — I can't — " She had gone white. She stared at the open page as if it might bite her. "How do I write that?"

"I don't know."

"It's got no letters. It's — she's — " She turned and looked at the girl, who was standing on the threshold with her hands at her sides and her head up, wholly composed, a small figure in grey with the whole cliff behind her. "That's her *name.* Isn't it? That's her own."

"Yes."

"The Ledger needs a word."

"Does it?"

Tamsin opened her mouth. She closed it. She looked at the pencil. And then, with great slowness, she turned back to the page, and set the point to the paper, and drew.

It took her four tries. She drew a curve, long and low, that climbed in the middle, and bent over, and rose to a final soft peak. Beside it she wrote the interval in the neat small tide-clerk's hand she'd been trained to, the pitch and the time, as though she were recording a bell. She looked at it for a long time. Then she tore the page out, carefully, along its stitching, and carried it down the stair, through the silent crowd, to the tide-house.

---

Vesh was waiting at the lectern.

He had not moved from the front rank. He had walked down ahead of her without a word, and was standing in the long room with his spectacles in his hand, and the great Ledger lay open under the window. It was open at the back. It was open at the page with the blank line.

Tamsin laid the torn sheet beside it. She smoothed it flat with the heel of her hand. For a moment nobody breathed.

"I will not write it," Vesh said. His voice was unsteady. "I cannot. It is not my place."

"It's not a theft," said Tamsin.

"No." He looked at her. He looked at the curve she had drawn: the little rising line, so small on the white paper, and the numbers beside it. His mouth worked. "No. I do not think that it is."

"The Ledger records what it is told."

"Yes." A shaky breath. "It does." And he took up the pen. It was a plain steel pen, the one he had used for nineteen years, and he dipped it, and wrote on the blank line under *Name,* in his neat, exact, shaking hand, the shape of a note. Not the letters. The curve. He drew it as Tamsin had drawn it, long and low and rising, and when it was finished, he put the pen down, and took off his coat, and laid it on the lectern, as a man lays down a coat on the quay of a harbor he has been standing guard on for years.

"There," he said. "She is entered."

---

Caelen stood at the window of the tide-house afterward and watched the harbor fill again with ordinary sound.

The crowd was breaking up. Ibb had gone back up the stair with his bell, very slowly, an old man, with a clerk on either arm. The council was talking in low urgent voices, and Hask stood apart, at the head of the quay, with his arms folded, looking at the cliff. The lamps had gone back to their small gold. Nessa was out on the road, a tiny dark figure with an oil-can, trimming wicks.

He had done it. He understood that with a kind of numb, shy astonishment. He had given a child the one thing that a man had given him once, in a rusting shelter, with a fire between them. Not a name. A name was not a thing you gave. The thing was the right to have one. Varrick had never said it. He had said *terms,* and *arithmetic,* and *it's not charity,* for nine years, because he had not had the words. But he had done it every day. He had walked down a ridge and not looked back.

*You were never a debt,* Caelen thought. *You were never anything but this.*

He put his hand flat on the glass.

She had come to stand beside him. He had not heard her. She stood with her chin barely over the sill, in the grey smock, and he looked down into her face and saw that it had changed. It was open. She was looking at the sea.

"Stay," she said, in her small hoarse voice.

"Yes."

"Caelen." It came out wrong. It had too many breaths in it. "Caelen. *Stay.*"

He could not speak. He nodded.

Far out beyond the headland, in the clear morning, at the edge of the world where the green went into grey, a sail stood up out of the sea. It was a long low shape, three-masted, with no figurehead, and it was flying a pennant that he did not need to see closely. It was the colour of ash.

The girl did not look at it. She was looking at him.
