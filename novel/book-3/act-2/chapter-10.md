# Chapter Ten — The Debt in the Open

He slept for eleven hours, and when he woke the city had changed its mind about him.

Caelen understood it before he opened his eyes. There was a quality to the silence in the guest chamber, a listening quality, the hush of a house that has been told to be quiet for someone's sake. He lay for a while under the heavy blanket, feeling the old cold still banked deep in his marrow, the ache in his shoulders, the faint tremor in his hands that would take three days to leave. Then he sat up, and found that Rook had been asleep in the chair by the door with a knife across his knees, and had not moved.

"They're outside," Rook said, without opening his eyes. "Since dawn. I told them you were sleeping. They said they'd wait."

"How many?"

"I stopped counting at the plaza." A dry breath. "It's not like the lane, Caelen. They aren't asking for anything. They just want to see you're all right."

He went to the window. The hall's east courtyard lay below, gravel and pale stone, and beyond its low wall, filling the great plaza, standing in the cold bright morning in rows and clusters and silent families, were more people than he could have named. They had not pushed. They had not shouted. They stood with their hands at their sides, looking up at the window, and when he appeared in it there was a single motion, like wind through wheat, as several thousand people took off their hats.

He stepped back from the glass. His face felt hot.

"I don't know what to do with that," he said.

"You don't have to do anything." Rook had opened his eyes. His face, in the grey morning light, looked older and gentler than Caelen remembered. "That's the thing you keep missing. They're not asking you to be their miracle. They saw you hold a Warden's hands in front of everybody, and they saw the Warden live. That's all." He shrugged. "You could just wave."

Caelen went back to the window. He lifted a hand. It was a small awkward gesture, and the plaza answered it with a sound he would not forget, not a cheer, but a long low breath, as though the whole city had been holding it for ten days.

---

Kell found him in the garden at noon.

The hall's cloister garden was a small walled square of gravel and pale winter roses, and Caelen had escaped to it with a cup of tea nobody had asked him to finish. She was sitting on a stone bench in her plain grey coat with a bundle of papers in her lap. She rose when she saw him and then, apparently thinking better of it, sat again.

"I have been dismissed," she said. "From the Board. As of this morning. For *testimony contrary to the interests of the Guild.*" A thin dry breath. "I have been asked to surrender my seal and my desk, and to leave the Spire within the week."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be." She looked, for the first time since he'd known her, something like light. "I was Auditor of Deferred Accounts. It's a title designed to be forgotten. I find that being dismissed for cause is considerably more honest." She set the papers on the bench between them. "I wanted to speak to you before I go. I have something to ask, and something to tell."

"Tell first."

"Reth will not stop." She said it flatly. "You understand this. He has accepted the finding, publicly, with grace, because it is the only intelligent thing to do. And he will return. Not in the hall. In the fringe, in the settlements, in the hospices. He will begin gathering names. Cases of failure. A fraudulent camp here, a practitioner's error there, a child who died in a barn while somebody chanted a half-remembered working. Every one of them true. He will bring them back to the hall in a year, or in five, and ask it what it plans to do." She looked at him. "He will not need to lie. That is the whole of the danger."

"I know."

"Do you?" A small unhappy smile. "I am not sure that any of us do. He is not wrong that some of the things the finding permits will go badly. And when they do, he will be there with the book, and the hall will be tired, and Maren may not be in her chair." She drew a breath. "I am telling you so that you do not mistake this for a victory. It was not. It was a beginning. A very good one. It will need looking after."

"And what did you want to ask?"

"Whether the hall's houses of treatment will need someone to keep their books."

Caelen stared at her.

"I am not a good woman," Kell said. "I am a good auditor. It is the one thing I have ever been entirely honest at. I know every way a ledger can be made to say a thing it does not mean. I have spent twenty years learning them, mostly to my discredit." Her hands, folded in her lap, were quite steady. "The finding says the houses are to be funded from the hall's own treasury, and their rolls kept public. Somebody will have to keep the rolls. Somebody who will not be persuaded, or bought, or quietly promoted. I would like to offer myself. Without salary, if necessary." A faint dry smile. "I understand that I have no credit to trade on. But it seems to me that a woman who has spent her life building the hidden version might be of some use at guarding the open one."

It took him a moment to find his voice.

"I'll put it to Maren," he said. "I think she'll say yes. I think she might be relieved."

"That is all I ask." She rose, gathering her papers, and paused. "You were right, in the hall. About the door. I did not think I would say that to you." She inclined her head, very slightly. "I hope I shall be a better doorkeeper than I was a director."

---

He went to see Roshe that afternoon, in the hall's infirmary wing, in a narrow room with a single window that looked out over the courtyard.

The Captain sat propped against a bolster with his heavy hands lying open on the blanket and his eyes fixed on a point in the middle of the air. He had shaved. Somebody had brought him a clean shirt. The licensed housing lay on the table beside the bed, dull brass gone dark with a decade's wear, and he was not looking at it, in the exact way that a man does not look at a thing he cannot stop seeing.

"Captain," Caelen said.

"Just Roshe." His voice was a hoarse thread. "I've no rank left. I surrendered my commission this morning. I don't think they'll refuse it."

Caelen sat on the room's one chair. Neither spoke for a while.

"I've worn it eleven years," Roshe said, at last. "It was issued for the field. For containment. We're all meant to wear one, the Captains of the eastern garrisons, in case a subject Shifts and we've to meet it. Nobody told us what it does to the wearer." His eyes finally moved, and found the brass on the table, and stayed. "I began to feel it about the fourth year. A sort of — leaning. On the edges of things. I told myself it was fatigue. I went on wearing it. I signed Greyfen in the seventh year, and I didn't sleep for a week, and I told myself that was doctrine too."

"Nobody warned you."

"Nobody *knew.*" A dry cracked laugh. "Do you understand? That's what they can't — that's what I can't get past. A whole rank of Captains, every one of us wearing a thing that was slowly eating us, and killing the drifting in the name of a doctrine that said the drift was the subject's failing. And the whole time it was us." He put his hands over his face. "I killed a woman for what I had already begun to become."

Caelen said nothing. There was nothing to say. He sat with him in the small grey room, in the ordinary afternoon, and let the man weep, and after a time Roshe took his hands away, and looked at him, red-eyed and hollow.

"Will you tell them?" he said. "The Wardens. What it does. Every Captain in the order is wearing one."

"Malakar will," Caelen said. "And Orell. They'll have it before the hall by tomorrow. I think Maren will suspend the issue of the housings till it's understood." He paused. "I'll ask them to tell it in your name, if you'll let them. Not to shame you. So nobody can say it was a rumor."

Roshe was quiet a long time. Then, very slowly, he nodded.

---

The note arrived that evening, on plain grey paper, without a seal.

It was left at the guest chamber's door by a small clerk who did not wait for an answer. Caelen opened it standing, and read the single line, and then read it again.

*I have kept a second ledger for forty years. It has a column at the right that had no entries until ten days ago. It has two now. I thought you ought to know which one of them I did not expect. — R.*

He held it for a long time. Behind him, in the room, the others were talking in low voices, Corwin and Lisbet by the hearth, Seraphina with Ilse at the table, a soft murmur of tired ordinary sound. Rook was asleep in the chair again. Malakar and Orell had gone out to the corridor to talk to a Warden Commander in a voice too quiet to overhear.

He thought of a small plain man in a locked-drawer house, writing the first name in a column he had never had a use for. He thought of how, in the end, it had been the man who signed a mother's death who had earned the second.

It was not a victory. It was not even, quite, an understanding. But it was a fact that had not existed before, and it lay in his hand like a small warm stone, and he folded the note along its crease and put it in the pocket of his coat, beside a brass token he had once refused to throw away.
