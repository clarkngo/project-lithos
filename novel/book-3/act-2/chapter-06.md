# Chapter Six — Seraphina's Answer

She did not sleep the night before, and neither did he.

Caelen found her at the guest chamber's window at three in the morning, sitting with her knees drawn up under her borrowed gown and a single unlit candle in her hand, looking down at the black courtyard of the hall's east wing. She did not turn when he came in. He sat on the floor beside the wall, a few feet off, and said nothing, and after a time she began to talk, in the low even voice she used when she was reciting something she had already learned.

"He is in the gallery. He has taken the same seat every day. Fourth row, at the aisle. He does not speak to anyone, and he does not look at the floor. It is the posture of a man attending his own trial from the wrong side."

"You could sign it," Caelen said. It was not advice. It was only a thing he wanted her to hear said aloud, by someone who would not be angry.

"I could." She turned the candle in her fingers. "I have gone through it a hundred times. A door opens. The Wardens cannot close it. Every family I have not yet reached has a name on a list to point to. All it costs is a page." A breath. "And I think of the woman in the doorway, with the girl's hand held tight. *Better, not cured.* If it were her son, would she ask me to sign?"

"No."

"No. She would ask me to tell the truth and let her decide what it was worth." She set the candle down. "That is the thing about my father. He thinks the truth is a cost I am being asked to bear on behalf of other people. He has never once considered that it might be a thing they are owed."

---

The hall was full that morning, fuller than Caelen had seen it. The public galleries had been packed since dawn. Word had gone through the Spire like fire through dry grass, a whisper from the Sarn house, a rumor from the lower ring, that the archivist would speak to the House's offer. They sat shoulder to shoulder on the high benches, in the silk of the upper rings and the plain wool of the lower, and the vast grey room hummed with a low unbroken note like a hive.

In the fourth row, at the aisle, a tall man in dark blue sat with his hands folded on his knee. Halloran stood a pace behind him, straight as a post.

Seraphina crossed the floor alone.

She wore no gown of the House. She wore the plain brown coat she had worn on the Wastes road, stained at the cuff with camp smoke, and her hair was tied back with a bit of cord. She walked to the lectern and stopped, and put one hand flat on the wood, and Caelen, watching from the gallery's front rail, thought he had never seen anyone so quiet and so entirely unafraid.

"Name and office," said the clerk.

"Seraphina. Formerly of the House Ilvane, and of the reliquary staff of the Office of Canon." She paused. "I do not know what my office is now. I will say only that I am the woman who took a shard from a sealed case and carried it into the Wastes, and that I am not sorry."

A ripple ran through the hall. It was not quite a gasp. It was the sound of a great many people deciding to remember a sentence.

---

She began, as Malakar had begun, with what she had done.

She told them about the archive, and the three years of cataloguing, and the fragments she had found in a room the Synod had labeled *unrecoverable ruin.* She told them about a treatment called the tethering rite, which was not a cure and had never claimed to be, and which the Synod had held sealed for three generations while families buried their children. She was precise and dry and quite without ornament. Several of the Custodians in the horseshoe had bent forward, unconsciously, as if to catch every word.

Then she turned. She did not turn toward the horseshoe. She turned to the fourth row, at the aisle, and looked directly at her father.

"I have been made an offer," Seraphina said. Her voice did not shake. "It was made to me privately, in a house I love and cannot live in, by a man who has never in my hearing lied to me. It was a generous offer. It would have given the rite recognition in the Synod's own record. It would have given the House's protection to the families who depend on it. It would have cost one thing."

Lord Ilvane's face did not move. It was the face of a man being read aloud.

"It would have cost the truth about my brother." She let it sit. "I was asked to say that Tomas died abroad, of a fever, in the course of an honorable journey. I was asked to say that I left the Spire in grief, with my House's blessing. It is a good story. It is nearly true. And I am going to tell you the rest of it, because I would rather be an honest woman with no protection than a protected woman who has learned to say that name in the passive voice."

She drew a breath. In the silence, Caelen could hear a page turn somewhere in the gallery, and the small dry creak of a bench.

"My brother's name was Tomas," she said. "He was born two years after me. He was funny. He could not keep a secret to save his life, and he kept one anyway, for a season, and it killed him. He bought a conduit he could not afford from a man who did not ask questions, and he wore it in secret for six months. It was a Talon Vector, an Ember Cadence. He Shifted in the gardens at night. He did it because it was the only time in his life that he was not being watched, and he was sixteen, and he had never in his life been free."

Somewhere, very far back in the gallery, a woman made a small sound.

"He began to drift in the spring. I saw it before anyone. I did not know the word. I only knew that my brother had stopped looking at things properly, that his eyes rested on the window a beat too long. I told my father. My father sent for a physician, and the physician said *nerves.* We were told to wait. We waited. And in the autumn they took him out of the house at dawn in a covered carriage, to be *returned to the mountains for treatment.* That is the phrase. I want the hall to hear it, because it is the phrase this House used, and the Synod used, and every House in this city has used for three generations."

She looked at the horseshoe. "It means *killed.* Quietly. By people who loved him. Who could not find a way to keep him, and could not bear to be seen to send him, and so used a sentence so gentle that nobody had to hear what it said."

---

The silence lasted a very long time.

It was Maren who broke it, though not with words. She simply set down the stylus she had been holding, and folded her hands, and looked at Seraphina with an expression Caelen could not read at that distance, and might not have wanted to.

"The hall enters the archivist's testimony in full," she said, quietly. "In particular the name. The hall will remember it."

Seraphina inclined her head. She had turned back to the fourth row. She said, to her father, in a voice he alone was meant to hear, and which carried, in that vast stillness, to every corner of the vault:

"I am not refusing your offer because I do not love you. I am refusing it because I do. You kept his name four years. You said it in the study, and I felt it go through the house like a bell. I do not think you want it forgotten. I think you want to *survive* it. But there is a way of surviving a thing that is only a slower way of losing it. I have watched it in this city all my life. I would rather Tomas were remembered as what he was."

Lord Ilvane sat very still. Then he closed his eyes.

And in the silence, behind him, someone stood.

---

It was Halloran.

He rose from his place in the fourth row's end without a word, without a sign, a lean immaculate man in grey wool, and he took a step into the aisle. He was trembling. Caelen saw it from the rail. It was the fine constant tremor of a man who had ridden six days beside a coach, saying nothing, waiting for a stranger to tell him the truth.

"With the hall's permission," he said. His voice was high and thin in the great space. "I am not on the roll. I am a steward of the House. I have no standing."

"The hall recognizes anyone who asks," said Maren. Very gently.

"Then I would like to say that it is true." He drew himself up. "What the archivist has said. I was a junior footman in the household at the time. I carried the trays to the east wing, the last four weeks. I was not permitted to enter. I set them on a table outside the door, and I collected them, and they came back untouched." His voice broke, and he steadied it with visible effort. "On the eleventh of October, at a quarter to five in the morning, I helped to carry his trunk to the covered carriage. It was very light. I have thought about that trunk every day since. I was told to forget it. I have not."

He bowed, not to the Custodians, but to Seraphina, low and slow, with the courtesy of a man performing the only act of defiance he had ever been trained for.

"The young master was called Tomas," Halloran said. "I thought the hall ought to know that someone in the House remembered."

Then he sat down again, in the aisle, on the cold stone, and Caelen understood that his knees had simply stopped holding him.

Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. Lord Ilvane's eyes were still closed, and a single tear had gone down his long composed face and stopped at the corner of his mouth, where it stayed, like a thing that had been there for four years and had only now been permitted to show.

Caelen looked at Seraphina at the lectern. Her hand was still flat on the wood. She was looking at her father with a face that had gone quite empty of everything but a great tired tenderness.

It was, he thought, not victory. She had not beaten anyone. She had only said a name in a room where it could not be unsaid, and let it stand.

That was all any of them had ever managed. He thought it might be enough.
