# Chapter Nine — The Widening Door

On the tenth morning the hall was so full that people stood in the aisles, and the air under the high grey vault was thick with breath and wool and held silence.

Caelen took his place in the gallery's front row between Seraphina and Rook. Malakar sat behind them, hood back, and Orell at his side. Corwin had given his good coat to Lisbet, who sat very straight with her hands in her lap, and the two of them looked, together, like a portrait of people who had run out of things to lose. Kell was there too, alone at the end of a bench in her plain grey, and beyond her, in the Wardens' block along the wall, a sandy-haired captain sat with his elbows on his knees and his eyes on the floor.

Roshe had not been the same since the fourth day. Caelen had noticed it without meaning to. He came to every session, and sat at the far end of the second row, and did not speak to anyone. His face had gone slack and grey. Twice Caelen had seen his hand press flat against his own left forearm, under the sleeve, as if to hold something still.

He noticed it again now, and something small and cold turned over in his stomach, and he did not yet know why.

---

Reth spoke last. He was brief.

"I have listened," he said, "for nine days. I have heard my petition attacked by the finest witnesses I could have wished for, and I have not been persuaded that they are wrong. Nor have I been persuaded that I am." He stood at the lectern with his hands folded, small and grey, without paper. "I will say only this. I have kept a book for forty years. It has in it the names of the dead I have been able to find. It does not have the names of the ones I could not. I know that a register is not a mercy. I know that a license can be a leash. And I know that if this hall decides to do nothing, a thousand names will be added to the book this year, and another thousand next, and no one will be responsible for any of them."

He looked out over the horseshoe, and for the first time Caelen saw something plain and raw in the small grey face, not quite grief, not quite anger, only the exhaustion of a man who had done the same sum for forty years and had not once been able to make it come out kindly.

"I do not ask the hall to trust me. I ask it to decide who it would rather be, when the names are read."

He sat down. It was very quiet. Then Maren rose from the horseshoe's center, small and straight, with a single sheet in her hand.

"The hall will vote on a finding," she said. "I will read it, and the hall will amend it, and —"

She stopped.

A sound had come from the Wardens' gallery.

---

It was a low sound, wet and strange, not a cry. It was the kind of sound a body makes when it is trying to hold something in and has begun to fail.

Caelen was on his feet before he knew it.

Roshe had risen from his bench. He stood swaying in the second row with one hand clamped on his left forearm, and his face had gone from grey to a terrible slack white. His eyes were open. They were fixed on nothing. And under the grey sleeve, where his hand gripped, something was moving, a small slow ripple, like a fish under ice.

"Captain," said the man beside him, sharply. "Captain Roshe —"

"*Contain it.*" A voice from the Wardens' block, high with panic. "He's Shifting. Draw. Draw on the —"

Steel rang across the vault. Caelen saw two dozen grey-and-brass figures rise at once, hands at their belts, a wall of raised weapons closing on a single swaying man, and he understood in one cold clear instant what a containment order looked like from above. It was not cruelty. It was fear, and doctrine, and the plain unbearable arithmetic of a room full of people.

"*Stand down!*"

It was Malakar. He had come over the gallery rail without a word, and he stood in the aisle below in his plain dark coat, with his hands open and empty, and his voice filled the hall the way it had once filled a burning lane. "By my own authority as formerly Commander of this order, and by the protection of this hall — stand *down.* That man is not a threat. He is a patient."

"He's drifting," someone shouted.

"Yes," said Malakar. "And you are about to kill him for it."

Nobody moved. The weapons wavered. In the Wardens' block, a young officer's hand shook on his hilt.

Caelen went over the rail.

---

He had never crossed a room so slowly. It was thirty strides from the gallery stair to the second row of the Wardens' block, and every one of them was a decision. He heard Rook shout his name. He heard Seraphina, behind him, say quite clearly, *go, I'm coming.* He heard a great grey sound rising around him like a tide, the sound of nine hundred people watching one man walk toward another with his hands out.

He reached Roshe. He put both palms flat against the man's chest, and he let the coal light.

It came up in him hard, hard and fast, the old wrench and burn, his skin drawing tight across his shoulders, his hands lengthening on the cold grey cloth with a low grinding pressure he felt in every tooth. He did not hide it. There was nowhere to hide it. Nine hundred faces watched the strata child's fingers go long and wrong on a Warden's uniform, and he heard, distantly, a woman in the gallery begin to weep.

"Roshe." His voice came out low and wet. He kept it steady. "Look at me. Not through me."

The captain's eyes did not move. Under Caelen's hands his chest heaved, and something huge and patient and utterly indifferent to his name was rising through it, the same vast hunger Caelen had faced in a chamber under a mountain, wearing the face of a man who had signed a mother's death in the passive voice.

*A licensed enforcement housing,* Caelen thought, dimly. *Issued to Captains of containment. Ten years of wearing it.* He could see the brass at Roshe's wrist, dull with use, a fine thread of frost creeping across it. No one had ever asked what the wearing cost. No one had ever asked whether a man who spent a decade killing the drifting might, himself, be drifting. It was so obvious that it took his breath. It had been sitting in every ledger in the territory the whole time.

"I can't," Roshe whispered. "I can't hold it. Every time I — every *time* —"

"You don't have to hold it alone."

He held the coal steady. He did not spend it on strength. He spent it on being the only fixed thing in a room that had gone entirely loose, and he felt the hunger lean on him, patient, enormous, reaching for the last warm answering thing. He let it lean. He did not give.

Seraphina's hand came down on Roshe's back. Her voice began, low and precise, in the working he had heard a hundred times in a stone circle, and it was strange to hear it in that vast grey place, small and clear and unashamed. Then, behind her, another voice took it up, and another. Odo. Ilse. Two archivists from a camp in the Wastes, standing in the Wardens' block in front of nine hundred witnesses, murmuring a rite the Synod had kept sealed for three generations.

It took eleven minutes. He would remember them all his life.

---

It ended the way it always ended, not with a moment but a slow easing. Roshe's chest stopped heaving. The frost went out of the brass. The dreadful unblinking stillness in his eyes gave way, by degrees, to something that had a person in it, and his knees went, and Caelen and Seraphina eased him down onto the bench between them.

"I'm sorry," Roshe said, hoarse, and began to weep, in a great ugly helpless shudder that shook his whole heavy frame. "I'm sorry. I signed it. I signed her name. I didn't know. I didn't *know* —"

"I know," Caelen said, and did not stop holding.

He came back to himself slowly, on his knees on the cold stone floor, with the coal guttered out behind his sternum and a cold that went down through his marrow like a well. He was shaking. Rook was beside him. Somebody had put a coat around his shoulders. He looked up.

Nobody had moved. Nine hundred people stood in the great grey vault in a silence so total it seemed to have weight. Along the Wardens' block the weapons had been lowered, one by one, and a hard-faced officer was staring at his own hand as if he had never seen it. Across the floor, small and grey and quite still, Reth stood at his side table with his leather book open before him and a pen suspended over the page.

He was not writing. He was looking at Roshe. And on his small plain face was an expression that Caelen had not thought to see there: the raw, undefended look of a man who has just watched the answer to a forty-year question arrive in the shape of a stranger holding a man's hands.

---

Maren read the finding an hour later. Her voice shook, and she let it.

*That no register of persons shall be kept save in public, and open to inspection by those it names. That no person shall be subject to detention or containment for unlicensed Resonance without a hearing and a name. That the hall shall establish and fund, from its own treasury, houses of treatment open to all who ask, and shall train in them, publicly and on the roll, any who wish to learn. That no practice of the rite shall be a crime.*

"That is the hall's finding," she said. "It is provisional. It will require amendment. It is, I believe, not what Chairman Reth asked for, and it is not what the archivist asked for either. It is only the first thing this hall has ever decided in public." She lifted her head. "The vote is called."

They counted it aloud, one by one, in the vast silence. Twenty-two for. Nineteen against.

Two votes. Caelen heard Corwin let out a sound that was half a laugh and half a sob.

"The finding is adopted," said Maren. "Provisionally." She set the paper down, and her hands were trembling, and she did not try to hide it. "It will be tested. It will be attacked. I do not know if it is right. I only know that I could bear to be wrong about it."

Across the floor, Chairman Reth closed his leather book. He stood, and bowed to the hall, small and courteous and unbowed, and said in his dry clear voice: "The Board accepts the finding. It will return." A pause. "With names."

He turned, then, and looked across the floor at Caelen, kneeling in a borrowed coat with his hands still faintly lengthened, and inclined his head, once, not in defeat, not in respect, but in something Caelen had no word for, and would carry a long while.

Then the hall broke, all at once, into a sound like a great door opening.
