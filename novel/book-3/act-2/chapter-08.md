# Chapter Eight — What the Doctrine Costs

Ilse spoke first.

Nobody had asked her to. She had come down at dawn on the ninth day, red-eyed and rigid, and put her own name on the witness roll in a small round hand, and Seraphina had watched her do it without a word. When Maren called the fringe party's testimony, it was Ilse who rose from the gallery's front rail and crossed the great floor alone, in her one good dress, with her freckled face set like a fist.

"I am Ilse," she said. "Archivist, by courtesy. I trained under Seraphina. I have been ten months at the camp at the edge of the Wastes."

Her voice shook in the vault. She kept going.

"I want to speak for the Chairman's petition." A stir ran through the tiers. Caelen felt it in his chest like a dropped stone. "I want that to be plain. I know what my friends will say. I have heard them say it all night, and they are kind, and they are probably right about a great deal. But I have turned away two hundred and six people since the lane opened. I have their names. I would like to read them."

She did. It took nine minutes.

She read each one in the flat careful voice of someone counting stitches: a name, an age, a settlement, a date, and the words *told to return.* She did not embellish. She did not look up. When she reached the end she stood a moment, hands trembling on the wood, and said, "That is what a rite without a hall looks like. It looks like a list of people I said no to. I would like the hall to hear it before it decides what a leash is worth."

She went back to her seat. Nobody applauded. Caelen watched Seraphina, in the front row of the gallery, close her eyes, and saw her lips move, and understood she was reading the names back under her breath.

---

Kell came next, and no one had expected her.

She entered from the tribunal's side door in plain grey, without an escort, and mounted the lectern as if it were a scaffold. She gave her office in a clear dry voice: *Auditor of Deferred Accounts, Eastern Territories, Mining Guild.* She looked, Caelen thought, like a woman standing before her own audit.

"I am here to speak against the petition of my own Board," she said. "I want the hall to note that I have been advised not to."

The Guild's gallery went very still.

"I have read this petition as carefully as I have read anything in my life," Kell said. "It is, I believe, honest. Chairman Reth has never in my experience lied, and I do not think he has lied here. I believe he means to make the register public. I believe he means an oversight council. I believe he means every word." She let the pause hang. "I would ask the hall to consider what I meant, a year ago, when I made an arrangement with Custodian Voss for a controlled fraction of the second seal."

A murmur. Across the horseshoe, an old Custodian leaned forward.

"I called it a fraction. I called it a controlled compromise. I believed it. It was, I thought, the most modest thing I had ever proposed. And I would like the hall to know exactly how long it took a modest thing to become a precedent." She turned a page she was not reading. "It took eleven days. The Synod's own liaison had a clause. The Guild's engineers had a draft. Within a fortnight, a *survey allowance* had become a standing entitlement, and nobody in either institution could remember who had first called it a fraction. That is what a charter does. It does not stay the size you make it. It stays the size of the hand that keeps it."

She looked, not at the horseshoe, but at the man at the side table, small and grey, with a leather book before him.

"Chairman Reth's oversight council will have nine members. I have no doubt they will be excellent. I would ask the hall who will be the tenth, in twenty years, when the council has been reformed for efficiency and the fringe seats have been found unnecessary. I would ask who keeps the list, and who keeps the keeper." Her voice, for the first time, cracked very slightly. "I built an arrangement in the dark and told myself it was small. I have learned that there is no such thing as a small thing made permanent."

She stepped down. Reth did not look at her. He was writing something in his book, and his pen did not pause.

---

They called Caelen at noon.

He had not rehearsed. He had tried, and it had come out wrong every time, and in the end Seraphina had said only, *say what you think, Caelen, and be prepared for it to cost you,* and he had thought that this was the most useful thing anyone had ever told him.

He walked to the lectern. It felt colder than he remembered. The light from the high clerestory fell in long grey bars across the floor, and forty-one Custodians looked down at him from their pale tiers, and behind them, across the whole vast vault, the gallery of the Spire held its breath.

"I don't think Chairman Reth is a bad man," he said.

It was not what he had meant to begin with. It came out anyway, and he let it stand.

"I think he's right about the dead. I think he's right that four hands on a load means nobody carries it. And I think that if I were the man who'd read all those names, I'd want what he wants." He looked at the small grey figure at the side table, who had, for the first time, set down his pen. "I want to say that first, because I don't want anyone to think I'm arguing with someone I don't respect."

He drew a breath.

"But I've been offered this before." A quiet ran through the hall. "Not by him. In a place you haven't heard of. A door in a mountain that I'd closed, and a Custodian, an old woman, very tired, who stood there and told me the Synod would take away the thing that made me dangerous, in exchange for the door staying shut. She called it *peace.* She meant it. I believed she meant it. And I said no."

"I said no because the thing that made me dangerous was also the thing that let me do everything I've done since. You can't cut the danger out and keep the person. That was the whole of my argument, then, and I don't think I had the words for it. I do now."

He turned to the horseshoe.

"Chairman Reth has offered you a mercy with a license on it. I want to tell you what that is." His voice was quite steady. "It's a leash. A good one. Well made, softly padded, held by kind hands. But you know it's a leash by one test. It's the one my friend Varrick used to teach me. You ask: *who can take it off?*"

"If the answer is *the family,* it's a gift. If the answer is *the council,* it's a lease. And a lease is a thing you can lose."

---

He spoke for twenty minutes, and it was not, afterward, a speech he could have repeated. He remembered pieces. A mother with a hand on a girl's shoulder. A forge. A stranger who'd asked to be held still so he could die as himself. He said that the rite was not a discovery. It was a practice, old as the Lithoi themselves, and it had lived for three generations in silence because someone had decided that people were safer not knowing.

"You've heard from Ilse," he said. "She's right. There are two hundred and six people she couldn't help. That's the cost of what we have, and I won't pretend it's small." He met the gaze of the old Custodian who'd leaned forward. "But I'd ask you to consider what you'd be buying. A hundred trained practitioners, licensed, funded, directed. And every unlicensed one — every mother who learns it from a neighbor, every archivist in a settlement too small to have a hall — a criminal."

"So don't license it." He said it plainly. "Fund it. Pay Ilse. Pay Odo. Build the houses. Put food in them, and beds, and someone to say when to rest. Train every soul who asks, in public, on the roll, with the fragments open to whoever wants to read them. Don't guard the door. Pay for it to be wider."

He looked at the small grey man at the side table.

"Chairman Reth wants the mercy to have a hand on it, so somebody's responsible. I think that's a decent instinct. But I think the hand ought to be the family's. And I think if the hall asks *who keeps the list,* and the answer is anyone but the people on it, you will have built the most careful, most compassionate cage this territory has ever known. And they'll thank you for it, and they'll walk into it, and it'll be twenty years before anyone remembers it was a door."

He stopped. He had run out of things to say. The vast hall was so quiet he could hear, very faintly, the wind at the high windows.

"That's all," Caelen said. "I'm sorry it wasn't better."

He stepped back from the lectern, and it was not until he reached the foot of the gallery stair that he realized his hands had been shaking the whole time, and that the sound behind him was not silence, but a great slow indrawn breath, like a tide beginning to turn.

---

Reth found him on the steps again, in the blue evening, as he had that first noon.

He came alone. He stood beside Caelen for a while looking out over the plaza, at the lamplighters going their rounds, and it was a long minute before he spoke.

"*Fund the door, do not guard it,*" he said. "I have been forty years in this work. I have never once heard it put so simply."

"You could adopt it."

"I could." The small grey face was unreadable in the dusk. "I have been considering it since about the second minute of your speech. It would be rather elegant. It would also mean that no one is responsible when the door fails. When a practitioner without a license kills someone. When a fringe camp turns out to be a fraud." He turned his head. "I have the names of the children who died in such places, Mister Caelen. There are more than you think."

"I know."

"Do you?" A pause. "I do not think you do. I think you have seen the good ones. The ones that worked." He said it without cruelty. "I think you will discover, in the years ahead, how many of the others there were."

Caelen thought of a stone circle. Of a man asking for a season. Of the two hundred and six names Ilse had read, and the small crosses he imagined in the margins of a book he'd never seen.

"Then we'll have to keep asking who's counting," he said. "And I'd rather it were all of us."

Reth looked at him a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled, small and dry and very tired.

"That," he said, "is precisely what I am afraid of."

He went down the steps into the lamplight, a small plain man with a leather book under his arm, and Caelen stood watching him go, and thought that he had never felt so entirely, so painfully understood by an opponent.
