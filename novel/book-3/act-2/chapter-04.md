# Chapter Four — What the Hall Requires

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Custodian Maren — a controlled exception, given to someone genuinely undecided. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**MAREN**

Nobody had taught her how to be afraid in a robe.

She had been taught the doctrine, at nineteen, in a plain stone classroom where a gentle old Custodian read the canons aloud and asked whether anyone had questions, and no one ever did. She had been taught the forms. She had learned to sit in a stall of the Hall of Canon without moving her hands. She had learned that a Custodian did not say *I do not know.* A Custodian said *the matter is under consideration,* and the difference between the two sentences was the difference between an institution and a person.

She said it now, alone in her cell-like room in the upper tier at midnight, to the dark. "I do not know."

It sounded like a small sin. She said it again.

---

The Eldest lay in a narrow bed in the infirmary wing, in a room that smelled of camphor and cold water, and she looked like something a river had left behind. She had been the most formidable woman in the Synod for thirty years. Maren had never once seen her raise her voice. She weighed, now, perhaps as much as a child.

"You are awake," the Eldest said, without opening her eyes. The voice was a thread, but it was still perfectly clear. "I heard the door. Sit, child. You are hovering."

Maren sat. The chair was hard.

"I took the chair," she said. "The acting seat. I did not mean to. I stood up, and it was as if someone had said my name, and then I was in the middle of the floor."

"Yes. I heard." A ghost of dry amusement. "It was very well done. Quite unforgivably well done. I had expected you to sit in the third row and let Voss's former allies fill the silence, and instead you have made the hall hear witnesses." A long slow breath. "You have made the hall say the word *living.* I have waited thirty years for someone to say that word."

"You could have said it."

"I could not." The eyes opened. They were very pale, very steady, and entirely without illusion. "That is what you must understand about the chair. I have sat in it thirty years, and I am not certain that I have ever *decided* anything. I let the doctrine decide. It is very restful. One is never wrong, only correct or unfortunate." A breath. "I told the hall you were to act because I am too tired to be uncertain in public. I chose you because you are afraid. It is the only qualification I have ever trusted."

Maren found that she was gripping the arm of the chair. "What should I do about Reth?"

"I do not know."

"You are the Eldest."

"I am a dying woman who has spent thirty years pretending to know." The thread of a smile. "I know that he is not wrong about the dead. I know that the boy is not wrong about the list. I know that there is no clause anywhere in the canon that covers a man who is right and dangerous at once." She closed her eyes. "You will have to write one. I am sorry. I would spare you if I could."

---

She did not go directly back to her room. She walked, instead, down through the wings, past the copy rooms and the stacks, to a low door at the end of a long dim corridor she had never entered.

The records vault of the Office of Canon was a cold, dry, windowless place, and the woman sitting at a long table under a single lamp did not look up.

Custodian Voss had been reassigned to the vault the winter before, and it had been a small quiet cruelty, of the kind the Synod excelled at. It was a room of ledgers. There was no one in it to persuade. Voss had aged, in a year, the way a wall ages in weather. She was thinner. Her robe hung a little slack. She was cataloguing a stack of case files in a small square hand, and she did not stop until she had finished the line.

"Custodian Maren," she said. "I wondered when you would come."

"I did not want to."

"No. That is why you have." Voss laid down the pen. Her eyes, when they rose, were exactly as Maren remembered them: cold, exact, exhausted. "You want to know whether I would have done the same. Whether a woman who had spent thirty years believing in the doctrine would, in your place, have made the hall hear a mother's testimony. The answer is no."

"I did not ask."

"You were going to." A tilt of the head. "I have watched the hearing from the vault's ventilation shaft. It is a remarkable acoustic accident. I have heard every word."

Maren sat down opposite her. Neither spoke for a while. The lamp hissed. Somewhere far away in the stone a pipe ticked.

"I did not come to be forgiven," Maren said finally. "Nor to forgive you. I came to ask what it costs."

"What?"

"To be certain." She heard how her own voice cracked on the word. "You were so sure, at the end. You held the line until it broke. And I think, now that I sit where you sat, that I would give a great deal to be as sure. I hate how much I want it."

Voss looked at her for a long time. When she spoke, it was in a voice Maren had never heard from her: not the doctrinal cadence, not the cold flat certainty, but something plain and small and very tired.

"I was eleven," she said. "I watched a man I loved kill three people, and my mother, in a garden. I have told this to one other person in my life." A dry breath. "Certainty was not a virtue, Maren. It was a wound I had learned to hold like a blade. It served me because it kept me from feeling the wound. That is all it ever was."

"And doubt?"

"Doubt is not a virtue either." Voss picked up the pen, and set it down again. "The Eldest thinks it is. She has spent thirty years calling her own paralysis wisdom. I would not have said so, a year ago. I have had time to consider it." She met Maren's eyes. "Neither certainty nor doubt is the point. The point is what you can bear to be *wrong* about. I could not bear to be wrong about the garden. It made me monstrous. You must decide what you can bear to have been wrong about, and then you must act as if it were true, and be ready to be corrected."

"That is not a doctrine."

"No," Voss said. "It is only what I wish someone had told me at nineteen."

---

She walked back through the cold corridors in the dark, alone, with the words going round in her head like a coin she could not stop turning.

*What can you bear to be wrong about.*

She could bear to be wrong about the register. She realized it slowly, halfway up a stair, with her hand on the rail. She could bear to have slowed a petition that would have saved lives, if she had slowed it. She could bear to have asked one witness too many. She could not bear, she thought, to have signed her name to a list of the drifting and learned later that it had been used to find them.

It was not a philosophy. It was only a place to stand.

In the upper tier her small room was cold. She lit a lamp and sat at the narrow desk, and drew a sheet toward her, and wrote at the top in a small square hand: *A finding, provisional. To be entered upon the hall's close.*

She looked at it for a long time. Then she wrote, beneath it, the first line of the first clause she had ever composed in her own name:

*That no register of persons shall be kept save in public, and open to the inspection of any whom it names.*

Her hand was shaking. She let it. There was no one in the room to see.
