# Chapter Three — The Network, Visible

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Malakar. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**MALAKAR**

They had given him a lectern of pale wood and a glass of water, and he found that neither was of the slightest use.

He stood in the center of the Hall of Canon on the fourth morning of the hearing, his hood thrown back for the first time in a year, and the vast grey room went silent around him in the exact way a Warden garrison went silent when a man was about to be sentenced. He had stood at the edge of that silence eleven years. He had never once stood inside it.

Custodian Maren had made the ruling the night before, and it had been read to the hall at dawn: that all persons giving testimony in this hearing were under the protection of the hall's precincts, and that no arrest, detention, or recovery order of any civil or military authority would be served within them until the hearing's close. It had been, Malakar knew, the boldest thing she had yet done. It had cost her three of the eldest Custodians' votes in the preliminary count. She had not looked at him when she read it.

"Name and former office, for the record," said the clerk.

"Malakar. Formerly Inquisitor-Commander, Resonance Enforcement, Eastern Territories. Renounced by my own order more than a year ago, for cause." He heard his own voice fill the vault, level, unadorned, and was faintly astonished by it. "I am here in my own name."

---

He had prepared a statement. It sat in his coat in his own hand, precisely ordered, three pages, defensible on every line. He did not take it out.

"I want to tell the hall what I did," he said, "before I tell it what was done."

He told them about a boy of nine years, pulled from a mountain, and the file on his desk, and the sentence he had begun to form and had overwritten. He told them about a burning lane at Hollow Cut, and a forge-keeper who had died in front of him, and a cordon he had ordered to hold non-lethal that had not. He told it in the flat voice he had once used to read sentences into the record, and he heard the hall listening the way a stone listens to water.

"I have spent a career documenting force," he said. "Every parameter, every justification. I could put that record before you now and it would withstand any tribunal you could convene. Every act I ordered was within doctrine. That is the thing I wish the hall to understand. I was never a rogue. I was a *good Inquisitor.* I did precisely what the doctrine required, and it killed a forge-keeper, and it would have killed a child, and it required nothing else of me."

A murmur moved through the tiers. Across the hall, in the grey-and-brass gallery, a man in a Commander's braid shifted in his seat.

"I did not leave the order because I stopped believing in law," Malakar said. "I left because I discovered that law had no clause for what I was looking at. And I had spent eleven years training myself never to look at what the clauses did not cover."

He drew a breath. It shook slightly. He let it.

"I have a colleague who will testify after me. His name is Orell. He served under me, and after me, and he has seen things I did not. I would ask the hall to hear him with the seriousness it has given me, though I suspect I have not earned it."

---

Orell rose from the gallery's front bench and came down, and it was a strange thing to watch: a big grey-haired man in a plain coat, walking without ceremony across the vast dark floor, with a pale, tight, steady face.

He said his name. He said his rank. And then he said, into the silence, in a voice that trembled only once:

"Greyfen."

Malakar had told him to begin there. It was the only place to begin. Orell described the flooded cellar, the woman, the boy, the containment officer's order. He described the report he'd read, afterward, at a desk at Kesh Hollow, in the same careful clause-stacked language Malakar himself had once written in. *Subject contained.* *Collateral loss: one.* He read it aloud, from memory, word for word, and the hall listened to a mother's death in the passive voice.

"The officer who signed that report," Orell said, "was Captain Roshe of the Kesh Hollow garrison. He is seated in the Wardens' gallery, at the far end of the second row."

Heads turned. A heavy, sandy-haired man in a captain's braid had gone the color of tallow.

"Captain Roshe has since been promoted," Orell said. "I do not say this out of malice. I say it because I served beside him for six years, and he is not a cruel man. He is a careful one. He did precisely what the doctrine asked. It is the doctrine I am here to accuse. But he signed it, and he was promoted for it, and I would ask the hall to note that nobody has ever asked him whether he could sleep."

"Objection," said a hard dry voice from the gallery. The Warden Commander in the braid had risen. "The witness is a deserter. His testimony is —"

"The witness is under this hall's protection," Maren said. She had not raised her voice. She sat in the center of the horseshoe, small and very straight, and her hands lay flat on the table before her. "The Commander will sit. If the Commander wishes to answer, he may do so in his own turn, on the roll."

The Commander sat. His jaw worked.

---

She came in on the second half of the morning, through a small side door, between two tribunal guards who did not touch her.

Petra was a small, thin woman of forty, with a clerk's ink-stained fingers and gray in her hair that had not been there a year ago. She wore the plain brown of the tribunal cells. She walked slowly, a little lopsided, and when she reached the center of the floor she stopped and looked around the vast grey hall with the dazed exact attention of someone who had spent sixteen months in a room the size of a cupboard.

Malakar had not seen her since before her arrest. He had not been sure she would come. He stood at the lectern's edge with his hands clasped tight behind him and did not let his face do anything at all.

"Name," said the clerk.

"Petra Halsey," she said. Her voice was a dry, cracked thread. "Clerk, Long Reach garrison, until my arrest. I was charged with falsifying orders." She stopped, swallowed, and looked up, and her eyes found Malakar's across the floor. "I did. I want the hall to know I did. I misfiled thirty-one recovery orders in eight months."

She spoke for twenty minutes. She named the orders, one by one. She named, from memory, the families whose names had been on them. She did not weep, and she did not falter. She only laid it out, in her small precise clerk's voice, like a woman laying out a very long hem: this was a shepherd's daughter, and she is alive; this was a brick-layer, and he is in Greyfen's south quarter, and he has not been touched.

"The tribunal deferred my case for sixteen months," she said, at the end. "I was told there was a backlog. I was told there was no hurry." She looked at the Warden gallery, briefly, and away. "I understood, in the fifth month, that they were waiting for me to be forgotten. I would like it noted that I am not the first."

She stopped. In the great hush, Malakar heard a small sound behind him: Orell, letting out a breath he had held for a year.

Then a voice spoke from the horseshoe, low, and steady, and so utterly free of ceremony that it took him a moment to recognize it as belonging to a Custodian.

"Thank you, Petra Halsey. The hall enters your testimony in full." Maren's hands were quite still. "The tribunal is directed to produce, within three days, the complete record of the case against you, and the reasons for its deferral. If it cannot, the hall will draw its own conclusions."

It was not a verdict. It was not even a ruling. It was only a small hard thing dropped into a very deep well, and Malakar heard it strike the water. It would not be forgotten. He had spent eleven years learning the weight of paper, and he knew, in his bones, what it would mean when a hall of forty-one Custodians wrote a line in its own record and did not let it be struck.

---

He left the hall by the side door, into a blue and windy noon. Orell was already there, sitting on the wide steps with his elbows on his knees and his big hands hanging, staring at the plaza and the tall bright city like a man who had woken from a long fever.

Malakar sat beside him. For a while neither spoke.

"They'll come for us," Orell said. "Not in the hall. Outside it. After."

"Yes."

"You knew that when you started."

"I did." Malakar looked at his hands. They were very steady. "I would like to say, Orell, that I have never in my life felt more afraid. It would be a lie. I feel almost nothing. Only a sort of enormous quiet, as though I had been carrying a beam for eleven years and set it down."

"It's not down," Orell said, gently. "You've just put it where somebody else can see it."

"Yes. That is what I meant."

Across the plaza, near a column, a woman in a plain brown dress stood with a tribunal guard, being handed a cup of water by a clerk in Guild grey. She was looking at the sky. Her face was thin and lined and utterly astonished, as if she had forgotten it went on that far.

Malakar looked at her a long time. Then he did a thing he had not done in eleven years of service. He put his face in his hands, and let it be seen.
