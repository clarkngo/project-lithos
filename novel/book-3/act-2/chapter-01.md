# Chapter One — No Longer Fringe Business

His testimony took eleven minutes.

Caelen had rehearsed it for two nights, under Corwin's patient direction, in the tall thin house, and had thrown every word of it away on the stair. He stood at the lectern in the vast grey hall with forty-one Custodians looking down at him like a court of stone, and found that all he could say was what had happened.

He told them about a mine, and a blast, and a woman screaming that there was something in the seam. He told them about a man named Varrick who had left dried meat on a stone. He told them about a forge, and a man who had asked to go out as himself. He did not tell them what the rite was, or what he was, or whether he thought a register was a good idea. Maren asked him, gently, at the end, whether he wished to add anything.

"Only that I've never been a case," Caelen said. "I was always a person somebody let live. I don't know how to be the other thing. I'd rather not learn."

He stepped down. It was not, he understood a moment later, a small silence that followed him. It was the kind of silence that meant something had been written into a room.

---

By the next morning there was a line outside the green door.

Rook saw it first, from an upstairs window, and called Caelen up without a word. They stood together at the glass. Below, in the narrow lane between the laundry lines, thirty or forty people waited in a loose double file that stretched out of sight around the corner. They were not a crowd. That was what unsettled him. They were quiet, and patient, and they held themselves the way people held themselves outside a physician's door: mothers with hands on the shoulders of children, a man with a bandaged forearm, an old woman with a folded letter.

"How many?" Caelen said.

"Fifty since dawn. Corwin's neighbor counted." Rook rubbed the back of his neck. "There are three wardens at the end of the lane. Not doing anything. Just there. Watching who comes."

"They want something."

"They want *you*, Caelen." Rook's voice was very careful. "That's the difference. In the Wastes, they came for Seraphina because she had the rite. These are here for the boy from the mountain. They think there's a kind of — I don't know. A blessing in it."

Caelen went down. He stood in the doorway and looked at their faces, and it was the worst thing he had ever done, because he saw at once what they were asking.

A young woman stepped forward, her infant in her arms. "Sir. My husband — they say he's drifting. He hasn't slept in four days. He doesn't know me. Will you come? Will you just —" Her voice broke. "Just look at him?"

He had nothing. That was the thing. He had a coal behind his sternum and the discipline to hold it. He had a hard-won ability to be steady for a person in the worst hour of their lives, and it cost him, every time, a piece of himself he could not exactly name. He could not do it for fifty. He could not do it for five. And he could not tell her so without crushing something in her face he was not certain he could bear to crush.

"I'll come," he said. "Not today. I'll come to him, and Seraphina will come, and we'll do what we can. But I'm not — " He drew a breath. "I can't heal him. I want you to hear that first. I can only hold still while someone else tries."

"That's enough," the woman said, very softly. "That's — it's more than anyone has offered."

---

They spent the day like that. Seraphina and the two archivists from the camp, whom Corwin had somehow smuggled into the city by night, worked the lane in shifts, taking names, writing addresses, sorting the desperate from the merely afraid. Rook carried water. Corwin argued with a magistrate's clerk about a permit to block a public street. By evening they had a ledger of a hundred and sixty households and a growing certainty that none of them could keep it.

He understood, at some point in that long blue dusk, what Corwin had meant by *the Spire doesn't kill you.* It did not hunt him. It did not need to. It simply gathered, and pressed, and asked, in a thousand polite voices, for a little more than he had, and it went on asking until he was made of nothing else.

That night a footman in the dark green livery of House Sarn delivered a card of heavy cream stock, embossed in silver, to the green door.

*The Lady Sarn requests the honor of the company of the strata child at supper on the fifth evening. A small gathering. A few friends who admire your work.*

"It's not an invitation," Corwin said, reading it over his shoulder. "It's a claim. If you go, you belong to her table. If you refuse, you have refused a Sarn, which will be repeated by morning."

"What would she want?"

"To be seen with you," Corwin said, and shrugged. "And to ask you to show them. Just briefly. A small thing. A gesture. It's always a small thing." He paused. "The Spire loves a wonder. It has been starving for one for three generations."

Caelen looked at the card for a long time.

"I'll go," he said. "But not to show anyone anything."

---

He went, on the fifth evening, in Corwin's second-best coat, with Rook as an escort and Seraphina at his side in a gown she had brought from the House and worn as if it were armor. It was a beautiful room. It was warm, and golden with candles, and full of soft voices and very expensive silence, and the Lady Sarn, a woman of sixty with a voice like polished glass, took his hand in both of hers the moment he arrived.

"My dear boy," she said. "How brave of you. I have followed everything."

They gave him a chair near the fire and a cup he did not drink. They asked him about the Wastes and the mountain. They were kind, and curious, and listened with the particular rapt attention of people who had never in their lives been hungry. Somewhere near the second course, a young man in a blue coat leaned across the table and said, lightly, as if it were nothing:

"Do tell us, Mister Caelen. Is it true you can do it without a conduit? Just — as you are? I do so long to see it."

The candlelit room went still. Every glass paused. Rook's hand, beneath the table, closed hard around Caelen's wrist.

He thought of a tree, and dried meat set on a flat stone. He thought that what Varrick had taught him, above everything, was that a request presented as a gift is still a request. He set down his cup.

"It hurts," he said, quietly. "Every time. It burns through the food you've eaten and leaves you shaking. It's not a thing I'd do for supper. I'd ask you not to ask me to."

The young man flushed. "I meant no offense."

"I know." Caelen looked around the table, at the soft faces, the warm gold light. "I don't think any of you did. That's why I'm telling you. You'd never have asked if you thought it cost anything."

For a long moment nobody spoke.

Then the Lady Sarn set down her glass, very slowly, and said, in a voice that had gone quite different: "Thank you." She looked at the young man in blue. "I believe, Aurel, that is the first honest sentence spoken at my table in some years."

---

They walked home by lamplight, through the terraced lanes, all three of them tired and quiet. It was not until they reached the corner of Corwin's street that Caelen stopped, with a cold prickle at the back of his neck, and turned.

There was no one behind them. Just the long lantern-lit stairs, and the dark windows, and the laundry hanging still in the windless night. But he had spent nine years among wardens and ambushes, and he knew the difference between an empty street and a watched one.

"You feel it too," Seraphina said, very low.

"Since the Sarn house."

"Two of them," Rook said flatly. "One at the end of the lane, one on the roof of the tannery. Neither trying particularly hard to hide." He paused. "That's the part I don't like. They want us to know."

Caelen thought of a grey-coated man with a leather book, and a locked drawer, and a question about who kept the list. He thought of a line of families outside a green door, and the nine years he'd spent learning that being invisible was a kind of safety, and the plain weight of what he had traded it for.

He had never been hunted by people who only wanted to count him. It was a colder thing than any Warden's blade. There was no ground to read, no dark to slip into. There was only the long lit street, and the watching windows, and the plain fact that from now on wherever he walked, someone would be keeping the tally.

"Come on," he said, and turned toward the green door. "We have a hall to get to in the morning."
