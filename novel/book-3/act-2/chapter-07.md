# Chapter Seven — Reth's Gambit

The amended petition was delivered to the hall's clerk at noon on the eighth day, and by two o'clock every person in the Spire who could read had heard what was in it.

Corwin brought the printed copy to the guest chamber himself, breathless from the stair. He laid it on the table between them without comment, and Caelen bent over it beside Malakar, and Seraphina stood at his shoulder with her arms crossed, and for a long minute nobody spoke.

It was a shorter document than the first. It was also, Caelen saw at once, a great deal better.

*The Board, having heard the testimony of the hall, amends its petition as follows. That no register of persons shall be kept save in public, and open to the inspection of any whom it names. That an oversight council be established, of nine members, three from the fringe. That the Office of Treatment established under this charter shall be directed by a practitioner of the method herein described, and shall be authorized to train, license, and maintain as many practitioners as the territory requires.*

"He's taken your clause," Corwin said, to Malakar, low. "The public register. It's Maren's, she put it to the hall in open session on the seventh day, and he's written it into his own petition as if it were his."

"It was always going to be," Malakar said. "He is not a stupid man. He heard what the hall wished to hear and gave it to them, and now he has removed the strongest objection anyone had." He tapped the third clause. "And has offered the rite a home."

"A director," Seraphina said, very quietly. "*Directed by a practitioner of the method herein described.*"

"That is you."

"I know who it is."

---

He came to them that evening, uninvited, and it was, Caelen thought, the boldest thing he had done.

The knock on the guest chamber's door was small and precise. Malakar opened it, and there stood Chairman Reth in his plain grey coat, hat in his hand, with no clerk, no escort, and the mild attentive expression of a man calling on a neighbor.

"I hope I do not intrude," he said. "I thought it better to say it in person than to have you read it in a broadside."

Nobody moved. It was Seraphina who said, at last, "Come in, Chairman."

He took the offered chair, and refused wine, and sat with his hands folded on his knee. Caelen, watching, saw for the first time what the man was like in a room. He was not commanding. He did not fill the space. He simply attended to it, one person at a time, in a way that made you feel obscurely that you had been misjudged.

"I have listened to your testimony, Archivist," Reth said. "I listened to every word of it. I have been in this business forty years, and I do not believe I have heard a person say anything more true in a public room." He said it without irony. "I want to tell you that I was moved. I will not pretend otherwise."

"And yet you have amended the petition to make my work a division of your Guild," Seraphina said.

"I have amended it to make your work a division of the *hall.* The Guild funds it. The hall directs it. You direct the hall's directorate." A faint dry smile. "I confess the distinction is somewhat fine. But it is real. I could not, in conscience, propose anything else."

"You could propose nothing."

"I could." He looked at her. "And there would be a dozen more camps like yours in a year, and a hundred in five. And each of them would be someone's private mercy, trained by one exhausted woman, and each of them would burn through its practitioners in a season. I have the numbers. I have watched it happen to the eastern hospices." He turned his grey eyes on Caelen. "Your archivist has treated, by my count, three hundred and eleven people in two years. The territory has, by my estimate, forty thousand who need what she gives. It is not a matter of *whether* the rite will be organized. It is only a matter of whether it will be organized by people who love it, or people who don't."

---

It was, Caelen thought, an extraordinarily good argument. He could not find the place where it broke.

"Chairman," he said. "May I ask something?"

"Please."

"The rite is unlicensed now. Anyone can learn it. They can teach anyone." He kept his voice steady. "In your charter, who could do it?"

"Licensed practitioners of the Office of Treatment."

"And anyone else?"

Reth was silent a moment. "That would be an offense," he said. "It would have to be. A working of that depth, in untrained hands, can kill. I have read the reliquary fragments. I do not say that lightly."

"So the two archivists at the camp. Odo and Ilse. They learned it from Seraphina. If they treated a family in the Wastes without a license, they'd be breaking your law."

"They could be licensed."

"And if they weren't."

"Then yes." Reth did not look away. "They would be liable."

"And the families who came to them."

"Would be protected, as they are not now." He said it with quiet finality, as a man says a thing he has said many times to himself. "I know what you are asking, Mister Caelen. Whether it is a door or a leash. I would say to you only what I have said in the small hours to myself: that a system with no leash has no door. It has only luck. I have seen what luck does. I would rather build a mercy with a hand on it, and a name, and a place to put a complaint, than a mercy that depends on which stranger happened to find which camp."

He stood, and bowed, courteously, to each of them.

"I do not ask for an answer tonight," he said. "The vote is on the tenth day. I ask only that you consider whether the perfect might not be the enemy of the several hundred people who could be reached in a year, and the several thousand in five. I am sorry to have taken your evening."

He went out, and the door closed with a very soft click.

---

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That was the hardest thing anyone has ever said to me," Seraphina said, at last. She had sat down. Her face was very pale. "And I cannot tell you where it is wrong."

"It's not wrong," said a small voice from the doorway.

They turned. It was Ilse, the younger of the two archivists, standing with her hands twisted in her apron. Neither of them had heard her come in. She was twenty-six, sharp-boned and freckled, and she had worked sixteen hours a day for most of a year without complaint, and she was crying quietly, without appearing to know it.

"I'm sorry. I was on the stair. I couldn't help hearing." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "He's right. About the numbers. Do you know how many I've turned away? Since the lane opened? Two hundred and six. I wrote them down. And Odo's back is going, and you can't do it every day, Seraphina, you *know* you can't, it took you three days to walk after the last one." Her voice cracked. "If there were a hundred of us. If there was a house, and food, and someone to say when to rest. I would sign anything."

"Ilse," Seraphina said, gently.

"I would. I know what it is. I know it's a leash. I'm not a fool." She lifted her chin. "But I've held a woman's hand while her husband was — while he *went,* and I couldn't help him, because there was nobody else to do the working. I would put on a collar if it meant I could stop saying *come back next month.*"

She turned and went out, and her footsteps went quickly down the stair.

---

They did not sleep much that night, and the argument, when it came, was not what Caelen had feared. Nobody shouted. It went on for hours, quietly, in the low light of a single lamp, all of them tired and honest.

"She's not wrong," Malakar said. "I have seen this argument in every institution I have served. The people who most want the leash are the ones who have been asked, longest, to hold up the sky alone."

"I know," Seraphina said. Her eyes were shut. "That is what makes it monstrous. I am going to have to say no to a woman who has given the better part of a year of her life to my work, and tell her that I am doing it for her own good."

"You might be wrong," Caelen said.

She opened her eyes.

"I mean it. I've thought about it all night." He was sitting on the floor, back against the wall, the way he had sat in a hundred camps. "I don't know that he's lying. I don't know that the oversight council won't work. I think it might. And if I'm wrong, a lot of people are going to have gone without something they could have had." He breathed out. "But I keep thinking about Varrick. About what he told me, the first night. *I don't do charity. I do terms.* He meant it. He never once let me think a gift was free. And I think that's the thing I can't put down. A mercy with a price tag on it that somebody else sets, and somebody else changes, and somebody else can take away. It's not a mercy. It's a lease."

Seraphina looked at him for a long time.

"Then we will have to say so," she said. "In a room full of people who are dying for it not to be true. In a way that a woman who has given her life to my work will not hate us for." A short unhappy laugh. "It is fortunate that we have only two days."

Outside, the single high bell of the Spire rang the third hour, thin and clear. Somewhere below, on the stairs of the east wing, Ilse was still awake. Caelen could hear her, faintly, pacing.
