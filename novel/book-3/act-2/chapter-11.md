# Chapter Eleven — What the Spire Remembers

Winter held the city for another two months, and in that time the finding became something other than paper.

It did so quietly, the way a thing takes root. There was no proclamation. There was a clerk in the hall's east wing with a ledger and a public roll pinned to a board in the plaza, a plain wooden board with a slot for names. On the first morning three people stopped to read it. By the end of the month a queue had formed. It was the strangest thing Caelen had ever seen, a line of ordinary people in winter coats, standing in the cold to read a list of the houses of treatment the hall had promised to fund, and to add, in their own hands, their own names.

*Let it be seen,* the first line of the roll read. Maren had written it herself.

The hall did not agree on much. It had split, in the weeks after the vote, into three loose parties, arguing in the corridors and the copy rooms with the fierce unpracticed energy of an institution that had discovered it had opinions. The finding was amended eleven times. It was attacked from the Guild's benches and the Wardens' and, unexpectedly, from a faction of old Custodians who thought it did not go far enough. But it held. It held because a clerk with a slate and a board in a plaza had made it a thing people could stand in front of and read.

Custodian Maren was confirmed as Eldest in the second week of the thaw. It was not an election, exactly. The old Eldest had died in her sleep in the last hour of February, and her chair had stood empty three days, and when the hall met to fill it, no one else would stand. Maren had wept publicly, briefly, and then said in a clear steady voice: *I will do it, and I will be wrong, and I would like it recorded that I asked to be told.*

---

The houses opened in spring.

The first was Corwin's. It was the tall thin house in the lower ring with the green door, and it had never, in his mother's lifetime, held more than four guests at once. In the second week of March a carpenter knocked out the wall of the front room and set a long table there, and a woman from the hall's treasury came with a sack of coin and a roll of paper, and Ilse hung a hand-lettered sign over the door in her round small hand. It said, *Come In.*

Lisbet ran it. Nobody had appointed her. She had simply turned up on the first morning in her plain dress, with her hair tied back and her hands not quite steady, and said that she had a certain amount of practice at being the person who was waited on, and would like to try the other thing. She learned the working from Seraphina, slowly, across a season. She was very bad at it at first. She was very good at everything else: the food, the beds, the patient administrations of a household that had learned, at a cost, what it was to be seen.

Caelen went there often. He would sit in the kitchen with a cup of something hot, and watch the door open and close, a stranger and a stranger and a stranger, and none of them was afraid of him. It was the odd thing. Nine years of being unremarked, and one winter of learning to be looked at, and he had found, at the end of it, that the ordinary weight of being known was a thing you got used to. It was rather like a coat.

---

There were smaller changes. He learned them in pieces.

The Warden order suspended the issue of enforcement housings, pending review. It was Malakar's doing, or Orell's, or Roshe's, or the hall's, or the plain terrible force of a Captain's hands in front of nine hundred witnesses; Caelen could never afterward say which. An inquiry was convened. The inquiry found, after a fortnight, that of the twenty-nine Captains of the eastern garrisons, eleven showed signs of drift, and four of those were beyond what the rite could reach. Nobody spoke to them of containment. A house of treatment was opened for Wardens, in the old barracks at Kesh Hollow, and it was Orell who ran it, a big grey-haired man in an apron, teaching men who had once worn his own uniform how to hold a working steady.

Petra Halsey went home to Long Reach in the second month. The tribunal had produced the record of her case, as the hall had directed, and the record proved to contain a single page. It was returned to her, sealed, with a formal apology in the passive voice. She kept it framed on her kitchen wall, she wrote to Malakar, *as a reminder of what it looks like when a clause is honest.*

Halloran left the House Ilvane in April. He did it courteously, with a month's notice and a letter of thanks. He had been offered a place, he told Caelen, as a clerk of the rolls in the hall's east wing, and had accepted it. *One likes,* he said, *to be of use to a record that does not require one to forget.*

And there was the matter of the reliquary.

It came in the last week of March, in the form of a heavy iron key on a leather cord, wrapped in a sheet of plain paper, and sent by hand to the green door. The paper bore a single line in a small square hand Seraphina recognized at once, and named aloud.

*The Office of Canon holds four hundred and twelve fragments of Lithoi-era methodology. They are catalogued. They have not been read. I had thought it a waste of a good archivist to leave them so. — Voss.*

Seraphina had held the key in her palm a long time. She did not say anything. She only closed her fingers around it, and stood at the window, and after a time Caelen saw that she was crying, quietly, without any of the careful court architecture, for a woman who had once locked her in a cell and had now, in the only way she knew how, unlocked something else.

---

The Ilvane garden was very cold in April, and the crocuses had begun to show.

He went there on the last day of the month, at Seraphina's asking. It was a small formal square behind the great house, walled in pale stone, with a fountain that did not run and a single old apple tree in the corner. Her father was waiting under it. He wore the dark blue coat he had worn in the hall, and he stood with his hands folded, and it took Caelen a moment to understand that he was not alone.

There was a small stone set into the ground at the tree's foot, and someone had cut a name into it.

It was a plain stone, not grand. It had been cut, Caelen saw, that week: the edges were still pale. The letters were plain and unornamented. *Tomas.* And beneath, in a smaller hand, a date, and beneath that: *Beloved. Not forgotten.*

"I had it made in February," Lord Ilvane said. His voice was very dry and very quiet. "I have been a long time deciding to let anyone see it." He did not look at his daughter. He looked at the stone. "The House has been advised, by its lawyers, that I ought to say nothing about the hall. That the wiser course is to let the matter fade."

"And?" Seraphina said. Her voice was very gentle.

"I find that I have no talent for fading." A pause. "I have written to the other Houses. All nine. I have told them what happened to my son. I have said that I sent him away to be killed, gently, because I was afraid, and that I would not have it be said of the House of Ilvane that it had survived him." He drew a long breath. "Seven have not answered. Two have written to say that they, too, had a child."

Seraphina made a small sound. She crossed the frozen ground between them and stood at his side, not touching him, and together they looked at the stone.

"It is a small thing," her father said.

"It is the whole of it," said Seraphina.

Caelen stayed at the garden's gate and did not go in. There were things a person ought not to watch. He looked instead at the tall bright tower rising beyond the wall, pale and clean in the thin April light, and thought, without quite meaning to, of a boy in an amber dark who had once known only a pulse.

The Spire remembered, now. It was a strange new sensation, like a joint that had been set wrong and had begun, painfully, to heal straight.

It would not last. Reth would be back with names. Kell would keep her books, and Maren her chair, and someone would learn, sooner or later, how to make a public roll say what it wished. None of them were fools, and none of them would be spared. But for now a small stone lay under an apple tree, with a name on it, and it was not being forgotten, and that was a thing the world had not contained a year before.

He turned, and walked back down through the terraced lanes toward the green door, where a light was burning, and somebody had set out a place for him at a long table.
