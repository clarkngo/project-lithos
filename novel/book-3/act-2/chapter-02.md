# Chapter Two — Ilvane's Price

The invitation came on cream stock the color of old bone, and it did not name Caelen at all, which was how he knew it was meant for him.

*The Lord Ilvane requests that his daughter attend him at supper on the sixth evening, and that she bring whomever she considers a friend.*

"He has never said *friend* in his life," Seraphina said. She held the card in two fingers, at arm's length. "Not once. I could not tell you if it is a courtesy or a warning."

"Maybe he's learning," Rook said.

"Maybe he is drafting." She set the card down on Corwin's table with great care. "Father does not invite. He arranges. Whatever is served at that supper has been arranged since the day the courier rode south."

She went. So did Caelen, in the second-best coat, alone, since Rook had declared flatly that he would sooner eat with wolves and that somebody ought to stay and watch the lane. It was a long walk up through the rings, and the further they climbed, the quieter the streets became, until the only sound was their own footsteps on pale stone and, far above, the single high bell counting the evening.

---

The dining room of House Ilvane was not large. Caelen had expected vast. It was a long narrow room with a table set for three, and a fire, and on the far wall a single tall window that looked out over the whole glittering slope of the city. There were no servants. Halloran stood at the door, immaculate, and withdrew when they entered.

Lord Ilvane rose. He inclined his head to his daughter exactly as far as courtesy required, and then, to Caelen's surprise, a fraction farther to him.

"Mister Caelen. I am glad you came. I confess I had wondered whether you would."

"I go where she goes."

"Yes. So I have gathered." Something in the long composed face moved, and was still. "Please sit. I keep an old table. I would rather the food not be cold when we quarrel."

They ate. It was a very good soup, and a roast of something Caelen could not name, and the wine he did not touch. Lord Ilvane spoke of the weather, and the season, and the state of the roads, and never once looked at the papers stacked at his elbow beside a small flat leather case. Caelen watched him do it. He recognized the skill. It was Varrick's skill, the one Varrick had used at a shelter fire nine years ago: the art of making a hard thing wait, calmly, until it was the only thing left in the room.

When the plates had been cleared, the Lord folded his hands.

"I have a proposal," he said. "I would like to lay it out in full, plainly, and I would like you both to hear it before you answer. I will not be offended by a refusal. I will be rather offended by a hasty one."

He opened the case.

---

It was three documents, and he laid them out in a row, as a man lays out cards.

The first was a letter of provisional recognition, on the heavy grey paper of the Office of Canon, signed at the bottom by a Custodian whose name Caelen did not know. It acknowledged the archivist's method of treatment as "an operative practice deserving of formal study," and directed the hall's clerk to enter it on the roll of recognized rites.

"Custodian Aldous owes my House a small favor," Lord Ilvane said. "He has agreed to sign this on the second morning after the hearing concludes, provided my daughter's standing is properly established. It is not a mandate. It is a beginning. But a recognized rite cannot be quietly outlawed by a Warden order. It has a name on a list of things the Synod has acknowledged exist."

Seraphina had gone very still. "That is what I have wanted," she said, in a small, controlled voice. "Since the first night. Recognition. A door the Wardens cannot simply close."

"I know," said her father. "I have watched you want it for two years."

The second document was a formal grant: a stipend, a house in the lower ring for the archivists' use, the House's protection extended over any who studied the method. The third was a single sheet, written out in a clerk's neat copperplate.

"This," Lord Ilvane said, touching it with one finger, "is the testimony I would ask you to deliver."

Seraphina read it. Caelen watched her read it, and watched the color go out of her face line by line.

"*I, Seraphina of House Ilvane, Archivist,*" she read aloud, quietly. "*Left the Spire following the death of my brother, Tomas, of a fever contracted abroad. I undertook the work of the rite in his memory, and with the knowledge and the blessing of my House.*" She stopped. "There is no mention of Tomas's conduit. Nor of the mountains."

"There is no need for it."

"There is no *mention* of it."

"That is what I said." Lord Ilvane did not look away. "The House can support your work under one condition. That it not be founded on a wound the House cannot bear to have examined. If you speak of Tomas's Shifts, of what was done to him, of what we did — I cannot protect you. And I cannot protect the rite. Reth's petition will make of it a scandal. A mother's drifting son, a noble House that hid him. Do you see? It becomes not a treatment. It becomes a crime, and your name is on its lintel."

"And if I tell the truth."

"Then you will be brave, and correct, and unprotected." He said it without cruelty. "And the rite will be a rumor with a scandal attached to it. I have watched this city bury better things than that."

---

Caelen had said nothing for a long time. He was watching the fire, and the small dance of light along the table's edge, and the sheet of paper lying between them like a small pale door.

"May I ask something," he said.

"Of course."

"If she signs it. If she stands up in that hall and says he died of a fever." He kept his voice even. "What happens to Tomas?"

Lord Ilvane's hand, on the table, stilled.

"He is remembered," he said, at last. "In the House. As a young man who loved his family and died of an illness."

"That isn't what happened to him."

"No."

"Then he's not remembered. Somebody else is."

The Lord looked at him, and for a moment Caelen saw something behind the composure, a small dry hurt, very old, like a bone that has healed crooked. "You are very direct, Mister Caelen."

"I was taught by someone who thought lies were more expensive than the truth." Caelen held his gaze. "I think that's the whole of what I know. I'm not trying to be cruel. I'm trying to understand what you're asking her to pay."

"I am asking her to pay what I paid," Lord Ilvane said. Very softly. "I sat in a corridor while my son was taken to a carriage. I did not walk beside it. It was the most cowardly thing I have ever done, and the most necessary, and I have never been able to reconcile the two. I have built a life around not reconciling them." His hands opened, palms up on the white cloth. "I am not a good man. I am a careful one. I have tried to be careful on her behalf as well."

Seraphina stood. She did it slowly, and pushed her chair in, and put one hand flat on the back of it.

"I will not answer tonight."

"I would not ask you to."

"But I want you to know," she said, and her voice was neither the court's nor the camp's, but something between, unsteady and clear, "that I heard what you said in the study. That you kept his name for four years. I believe that was true. I believe you love him." She drew a breath. "And I believe that is exactly why you are so afraid of the day someone says it aloud."

He did not answer. He simply looked at her, and it was the look of a man being seen, which Caelen thought, watching it, must be the worst thing that had happened to him in a very long time.

---

They walked down through the rings in silence, the bell counting the hour above them, the city's lights spread below like a scattered net.

"He is not wrong," Seraphina said, at last, on a landing where the stair turned. "That is the terrible part. I have thought it through. If I sign, the rite is recognized. The Wardens cannot touch it. Every family I have not yet reached gains a door. And all it costs is Tomas."

"It costs more than Tomas."

"Does it?"

"It costs you the thing that made you go to the Wastes in the first place." Caelen stopped, and looked at her. "You didn't take that shard to give the Synod a better story. You took it because they'd told you nothing could be done for him, and it was a lie. If you sign that page, you've told the same lie. Just kinder, and with a stipend."

She was quiet a long moment, her hand on the cold rail.

"There is one more thing," she said. "Father did not say it, but I saw it in the room. He was not merely offering." Her mouth twisted. "He was *afraid.* I have never seen it before. I do not think it is the hall. I think it is Reth."

"Reth came to see him."

"I would wager my life on it." She started down again. "And I would wager he offered him the exact same bargain, in a different coat. Something for the House, in return for the House's silence. And that Father has come to us because he has begun to understand, at last, that silence is a currency Reth can afford to buy in bulk."

The wind moved along the terrace, carrying the smell of stone and lamp-oil and, very faintly, rain. Far below, the green door of a narrow tall house glowed with a small amber light. Someone was waiting up. Caelen found that he had begun, in the space of a fortnight, to think of that as an unforced kind of blessing, and he hurried, a little, to reach it.
