# Chapter Five — What Corwin Paid

The writ came at dawn, carried by a man in the deep red of Vael and flanked by two Wardens who looked as though they would rather be anywhere else.

It was a beautiful document. Caelen would remember that afterward: the heavy cream sheet, the seal of the House pressed in dark wax, the flowing lawful hand. It was a petition of guardianship, lodged by the Lord of Vael under the Synod's old statute of *domestic protection*, in respect of his daughter Lisbet, nineteen years of age, of unsound condition, presently resident in a household not of the family's choosing. It asked that she be transferred to a licensed facility in the fifth ring for observation and care, at the family's expense, effective at noon.

Corwin read it standing in the narrow hall, with the green door open behind him and the cold morning coming in. He read it twice. Then he handed it to Malakar, who had come down early and stood at the foot of the stair in a dark coat, and said, in a perfectly level voice, "Tell me it's not enforceable."

"It is enforceable," Malakar said, after a moment. "It is entirely lawful. That is what makes it so well done."

"Noon," Rook said, from the doorway of the front room. He had a knife on the table beside him. He was not looking at it. "We've got until noon."

"We've got until they come to the door," said the Warden. He was a young man, very correct, and he stood on the step with his eyes on a point above Corwin's shoulder. "I'm sorry. I've no quarrel with any of you. I'm to wait outside until the hour, and then present the order, and if the girl comes quietly there'll be no difficulty." He swallowed. "I'd take it kindly if she did."

He withdrew to the lane. The green door swung shut. In the silence the four of them stood and listened to the small sounds of a household waking, a kettle, a stair creaking, and the quiet unhurried step of a girl who had been told she would be taken.

---

They held the council in the kitchen, because it was the only room without windows onto the street.

Lisbet sat at the end of the table in her plain dress with her hands folded, very pale and very steady. She had listened to the whole of it without speaking. She looked, Caelen thought, like a woman being told the date of her own trial.

"There are three courses," Malakar said, and the exact clipped cadence of an Inquisitor had come back into his voice, though it was gentler now, worn soft at the edges. "I would like to lay them out plainly, because I have spent a career making such choices without the people concerned present, and I do not intend to do so again."

"Go on," Corwin said.

"The first is flight. We take her out of the Spire tonight and run for the Wastes. It is the easiest, and the most reliable, and it would confirm every accusation the petition makes. She would be a fugitive. So would you."

"No," Lisbet said, quietly.

"The second is sanctuary. Custodian Maren's order protects all who give testimony from arrest or detention within the hall's precincts until the hearing's close. If your sister is entered as a witness, she may not be taken. She would live in the hall's guest quarters, under the protection of the Synod, and could not leave the grounds until the hearing ends. Perhaps eight days." His eyes went to Corwin. "The cost is that she would testify. In public. Against your father's writ, in your father's hearing, on the record."

Corwin's hands tightened on the table's edge. He said nothing.

"And the third," said Malakar. He paused. It was, Caelen realized, the first time he had seen the man hesitate before a sentence he had already decided to say. "The third is that we let them take her."

The kitchen went very quiet.

"For a week," Malakar said. "Ten days at most. A lawful writ, enforced, in daylight, in front of the whole street. A girl of nineteen taken by Wardens into a locked ward on her father's petition, while a hearing on a registry of the drifting proceeds three hundred yards away. It would be the most eloquent testimony the hall could hear. It would show, without a word spoken, precisely what a register does. It is, I believe, the strongest thing we have."

"You'd use her," Rook said.

"I would use what is available," said Malakar. "That is what I was trained to do. I am not recommending it. I am setting it on the table because if I did not, someone at Chairman Reth's side certainly would have thought of it, and I would rather we chose it deliberately than had it done to us by accident."

---

It was Lisbet who spoke first.

"I would do it," she said. Her voice was very small and quite clear. "If it would help. I would go to the ward. I have been in worse."

"No," said Corwin.

"Cor."

"*No.*" He was on his feet. The chair had fallen back behind him. His face was white and furious and very young. "You do not get to do that. You do not get to offer yourself up as a — as a speech. I have spent four years watching them decide what to do with you in rooms I was not allowed in. I have watched a physician say *nervous condition* to my face. I will not stand in my mother's kitchen and hear you volunteer to be a *point.*"

"It would work."

"It would work, and it would be obscene." He put both hands on the table. "I am sorry, Malakar. I know you meant it honestly. But I have been the family's convenience for twenty-four years, and I will not be the man who spent his sister to win an argument. Not even this argument. Not even if it wins."

Nobody spoke. Caelen looked at the young man's shaking hands, and the ruined chair, and the girl at the end of the table who had gone very still. He thought of Ansel, in a smoke-filled lane, saying *this isn't going to be one you have to carry the blame for.* He thought of a scavenger saying *don't spend yourself foolish trying to fix a thing that was never yours to fix.*

He thought that there were two ways to be spent. There was the way a person chose, freely, with their eyes open. And there was the way another person chose for them, and called it a cause.

"We take her to the hall," he said.

They all looked at him.

"Sanctuary. She testifies if she wants to. She doesn't if she doesn't. That's up to her. But she doesn't go to a locked ward so that the rest of us can be right." He met Malakar's eyes. "I know what it costs. If the argument were the only thing that mattered, you'd be correct. But I've watched people spend themselves for me my whole life, Malakar. I would like, once, to be part of a thing where nobody is spent by anyone else's arithmetic."

Malakar was silent a long time. Then, slowly, he inclined his head.

"I put the third course on the table," he said, "hoping someone would refuse it."

"Did you?"

"I have been living with the other version of myself for a year, Caelen. I would like to stop being surprised by which one speaks."

---

They went at half past ten, all of them, by the back lane, with Lisbet between Corwin and Rook and her chin lifted very slightly.

The Wardens at the front of the house were still waiting, formal and miserable, when the small procession appeared at the end of the lane and went up the shallow stair to the hall's great doors. By the time the young Warden understood what he was seeing and shouted, they were already inside the precinct. The doors were heavy. The two guards in the Synod's undyed grey who stood at them looked at the Vael writ, then at the Custodian's stamp on the witness register, and quietly, decisively, crossed their halberds.

The Warden stood on the steps with the order in his hand. He looked at Lisbet, small and straight in the doorway, and something went across his young correct face that Caelen did not care to name.

"It's a lawful writ," he said, to no one.

"Yes," said Lisbet. "I know. I read it."

---

The price arrived that evening, in the form of a second letter.

It was not on cream stock. It was a single line, in a large square hand, on a plain sheet, and Corwin read it at the window of the guest chamber they had been given in the hall's east wing, with the last light going red across the towers behind him.

*You have chosen. You are no longer of Vael.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be.

"It's what he told me at the door," Corwin said. He was smiling faintly, and it was terrible to look at. "I thought I'd been ready for it. It turns out you can prepare for a thing for four years and still be entirely unready." He folded the paper once, along its crease, and set it on the sill. "The name, I suppose. And the house is Mother's, so I keep the house. And I have nothing else."

"You have a sister," Rook said, from the door.

"I have a sister." Corwin looked toward the small inner room where Lisbet slept, curled on a cot, with the light of a single lamp on her face. "That is more than most people manage to keep in this city. I think I would call it a fair price." His mouth twisted. "I only wish it did not feel like such a bargain."

Caelen stood at the window's other side and did not say anything. He had learned, across two books' worth of losing, that some things were not meant to be answered. He looked out over the terraces, at the long fall of lamplit stairs, and the tall bright tower with its single high bell, and thought about the names his own hand had written in a book, and the people who had paid for them.

He would not spend anyone else's life to win. He decided it there, at the window, with the dusk going grey. He didn't know whether it would cost him the hall. He only knew it was one of the few things he was sure he could bear.
