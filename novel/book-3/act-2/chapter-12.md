# Chapter Twelve — The Glass Wastes, Wider

The letter came in the first week of May, on a morning so clear that the whole plain below the Spire lay open like a map, and it came, oddly, not to the green door but to Maren.

She sent for him at noon. He went up through the rings alone, past the plaza's board with its queue of quiet readers, through the great doors of the Hall of Canon and along a cold corridor he had never walked, to a small round room at the top of a stair where a woman in an undyed robe sat at a table with her hands flat on a single sheet of paper. She looked older than she had a season ago. She looked, he thought, like someone who had begun, at long last, to sleep less and see more.

"I did not want to give this to a clerk," Maren said. "I did not want it put on any roll until I had decided what it was."

The paper was thick, and tinged faintly blue, and it had been folded so many times that the creases had gone soft. The seal on it was one Caelen didn't know, a spiral of small gulls. The hand was fine and slanting and strange to his eye, and at the top it named a place he had never heard, *the Salt Coast, third harbor,* and a signatory, *Ondrei Vesh, Harbor-Master and Keeper of the Deep Ledger.*

"It came by sea," Maren said. "Twenty-two days, with a courier who was, I gather, very seasick. It is addressed to the hall. But it is really addressed to you."

He read it standing.

*To the Synod of the Spire, and to whoever among you bears the name Caelen. We have read your finding, as it was printed in the eastern broadsides and carried to our coast by a wool factor. We are a small people, and far, and we have kept our own counsel about the deep places for a long time. We do not write lightly.*

*Six months ago our own mine at the third harbor broke into a seam that our surveys had never charted. We found a child there. She is perhaps nine years old. She was sealed in a stone the color of honey. She has been awake for five weeks. She does not speak our language. She does not seem to have one.*

*She burns through food in an hour. She does not sleep. The healers cannot approach her, and our own doctrine has no word for what she is. We have read that you were once such a child. We do not know if this is true. We have nothing else to try.*

*Please come, or tell us what to do. Ondrei Vesh.*

---

He read it twice, and then a third time, and the small round room seemed to lean very slightly around him, the way a ridge leans when the wind drops.

"A second," he said, at last. It was barely a voice.

"I did not know there could be," Maren said quietly. "I have read the canon. I have read every gloss on the *grown, not touched* doctrine that the reliquary holds. All of them treat it as a cautionary story. A myth to frighten Guild crews." She turned her hands over on the table. "I think we have been very stupid, Caelen. I think a very great many things we called myths were only things nobody had thought to look for."

"She's a child."

"Yes."

"She's alone." He heard his own voice go rough. "In a place where nobody speaks her language. Where they don't have a name for it. She's been awake five weeks."

"I know."

He remembered a rain that had struck him like a blow before he understood what it was. A forest, and a spider mending its web, and a man's unhurried boots. He remembered having no word for anything, and being fed, and being followed, and how it had felt to hear a stranger say *terms, not charity,* and understand it as the kindest sentence anyone had ever spoken to him.

"I have to go," he said.

"I thought you would say that." Maren smiled, faintly, and there was no triumph in it. "I have the hall's leave to offer you a ship. And a letter of standing, and whatever gold the treasury can spare. It is not much. I would have liked to give you more." A breath. "You will not go alone."

"No," Caelen said. "I don't think I will."

---

He walked out to the Spire's western wall that evening, alone, with the dusk coming down.

It was a long high rampart of pale stone at the edge of the upper ring, from which the whole plain could be seen, the fields and the winding road and the green haze of the river valley, and far off, at the very rim of the sky, a low line of grey that might have been cloud and was not. He had come to it a dozen times in the spring. He knew what it was. He stood with his hands on the cool stone and looked toward it, and found, to his surprise, that he was not homesick.

The Wastes lay silver and unhealed at the horizon, as they always had. Somewhere under them, a scavenger's grave and a brass-fitter's lay side by side on a hill. Somewhere, a forge sat cold. Somewhere the rig-bones stood in the wind like the ribs of something enormous that had never fallen. And here he was, at the edge of a city that had spent three generations forgetting its own wound, looking back at the place where his own had begun, and feeling, for the first time in ten years, that it was not the only place he belonged.

He had thought, in the beginning, that a home was a fixed point. A ridge, a fire, a place you could name on a map. He'd been wrong. He had been wrong about a great many things, and the wrongness had been the shape of the whole long road, and he thought that Varrick would have laughed, and Fenn would have said something dry about arithmetic, and Ansel would have said, *well, you're thin as a whisper, come and eat.*

Home was who came looking.

They found him there, one by one, as they always did. Rook first, without a word, settling against the wall beside him. Then Seraphina, with the letter in her hand, which she had already read, and a small tired smile. Then Malakar and Orell together, an unlikely pair, still somehow exactly right. And last, breathless from the stair, with his good grey coat over one arm and a green ribbon in his buttonhole, Corwin, who did not say *you'll be needing a guide,* but stood there with an expression so plainly saying it that nobody had to.

"Ships," Rook said, at last, flatly. "I've never been on one."

"Nor I," said Malakar.

"The letter says twenty-two days," said Seraphina. "And that the courier was very ill. I find I am looking forward to it far less than I ought."

"The Salt Coast," Corwin murmured, with a strange lit hunger in his voice. "I've only ever seen it on maps."

Caelen laughed. It was a small startled sound, and it went out over the rampart and was lost in the wind. He looked at the faces around him, tired and lined and unaccountably bright in the failing light, and felt the small warm stone of his own name settle in his chest, worn smooth, exactly as heavy as it had always been.

It was not the same ridge. It was not the same country. There was no scarred silver plain beneath him, no rusted rig-bones, no patient hiss of ash. There was only a wide green world going dim and gold, and a road running out of it toward a coast none of them had seen, and a child at the end of it who had not yet been told that she was not alone.

"All right," he said. "Then let's go and tell her."

He stepped down from the wall, into the gathering dark, and did not look back to see whether they followed, because he had long since stopped needing to check. But he heard their footsteps, all of them, falling in behind him on the pale stone, and it was the best sound he knew.
