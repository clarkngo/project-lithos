# Chapter Nine — The Correction

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Chairman Reth — a controlled exception, following the discipline set by Malakar's and Voss's chapters in earlier books. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

**RETH**

He kept two ledgers, and only one of them was for the Board.

The first sat in the front room of the small house in the fourth ring, open on a slanted desk, in the neat impersonal columns the Guild had taught him forty years ago: shipments, licenses, tariffs, weights. The second lived in a locked drawer of the bedroom's night table, a slim leather-bound book with a cracked spine, and he had never shown it to anyone. Not the Board. Not Kell, who had come the closest to guessing. Not his wife, in the four years of their marriage before she died of a cough that no licensed physician in the eastern territories had been able to name.

That night, when the day's business was done and the clerks dismissed, he unlocked the drawer and took out the small book and sat at the desk with it, and let himself be, for a quarter of an hour, no one in particular.

He had begun it at twenty-six, in the Hollow Cut accounts office, on an evening he could still smell.

There had been a cave-in at the fourth level. A crewman named Deshane, unlicensed, nineteen, had put his shoulder to a fallen beam and lifted it. He should not have been able to. Reth had been in the mess hall when they carried him up, and he remembered how the men had gone quiet, and then how they had gone away from him, in a long slow circle, because they had seen what the beam-lifting had done to his hands. He had lived nine hours. During the ninth a Warden containment officer had arrived with a folded order and a manner of terrible courtesy, and the crewman had stopped being a man who had saved four miners and become a case number.

Reth had entered him in the loss ledger that night. He'd done it in the ordinary column, in the ordinary hand. *Crew loss: one. Cause: unlicensed Resonance. Disposition: contained.* The four miners' wages, he noticed, had a column of their own. Their families' compensation had a column. Deshane had a line.

He had gone home and opened a new book. He had not really known why. He had only known that a ledger that had no column for what had actually happened was not a ledger. It was a way of not knowing.

---

He turned to the first page now and read it as he sometimes did, not for the names but for the handwriting, which was young and very careful, and slightly ashamed.

Deshane. Eighteen names after him, that first year. Then a hundred. Then a hundred more, and the handwriting steadier, and the columns growing: age, cause, disposition. And later, in the margins, a small mark he had begun to add without ever deciding to: a tiny cross beside the ones who had died *unattended*, alone in a barn or a cellar or a roadside ditch, of a thing that could have been slowed, or held, or seen coming, if any authority anywhere had been responsible for seeing.

There were three hundred and forty of those. He knew the number by heart. It was the number that had brought him to the Spire.

He did not think of himself as a man who wanted power. He thought of himself as a man who had done the arithmetic in a locked drawer for forty years, and found that it came out the same way every time. The territory did not need a better mercy. It needed a *single* one. A hand that would be held responsible. A ledger where every person had a line, and every line had someone who would answer for it.

He had said so, in the hall, and it had been true. That was the peculiar torment of him: that in forty years he had never once found it necessary to lie.

---

His clerk, Tam, brought tea at nine and lingered.

"Chairman. The Custodian Maren has sent word she will admit testimony from the fringe party beginning the third day. The archivist and the strata boy. And the former Inquisitor, and a Captain Orell, on the fourth."

"Yes," Reth said. "I had thought she would."

"She did not consult you."

"She did not. She is not obliged to." He sipped the tea. "She is twenty-nine years old and has been given a chair she does not want, in a hall that has never been asked to decide anything. She is frightened. I would think less of her if she were not." He set the cup down. "I am somewhat surprised she has courage. I had thought her the more pliable of the two younger Custodians."

"Shall I have her watched?"

"No." The word came out sharper than he'd intended, and he was faintly ashamed. "I do not want her intimidated. I want her persuaded. There is a difference, and it is the whole of the point."

Tam bowed and withdrew. Reth sat a while with the tea going cold.

The strata boy. He had seen him for less than a minute, on the steps, in the blue shadow of a column. A lean man in a patched coat, twenty or near it, with hands that never quite rested and eyes that tracked a room's exits without seeming to. He had the look of someone who had spent a decade being unremarked and did not yet know how to be otherwise. He had also asked, on first meeting, precisely the right question.

*Who kept the list?*

Reth had carried that a long time. Not because he had no answer. Because it was, he thought, the only serious objection anyone had ever raised to his life's work, and he had not raised it himself.

---

He knew what was wrong with the register. He was not stupid. He knew it in the small hours, in the locked drawer, with the cracked spine open under his hand. A list of the drifting could be a net or a leash. It could be the first honest census of a hidden grief, or it could be a way to know exactly where to send a containment order. It depended entirely on who held it.

He had told himself the Synod would hold it. That an institution with forty Custodians, and a tradition of doubt, and a new acting Eldest with a shaking voice, would be an institution that could be trusted to remain accountable. He had told himself this for four years. He was aware that he was telling it to himself now.

He drew a clean sheet toward him, and began to write, in a hand no longer young, a list of the things he could offer that would make the register safe. A charter clause. A public rolls. An oversight council. Each one, he knew, was a small concession that made the whole a little less clean. He had never yet found a way to make a system both accountable and simple. He was beginning to suspect there wasn't one.

But he would not abandon it. That was the thing the strata boy could not know. Reth had seen Deshane's hands. He had seen the four miners' wages entered in a column and the boy's life in a line. He had spent forty years in a locked drawer entering the difference, and he would not now stand in a hall and say *perhaps the fringe's improvisation is enough.* It was not enough. It had never once been enough. It had left three hundred and forty small crosses in the margins of a book.

He looked at the sheet. Then, slowly, he turned to a fresh page of the small leather ledger and drew a new column, at the far right, in the neat young careful hand he had never fully unlearned.

He had never had a use for it before. He headed it, with a kind of terrible tenderness: *Survived.*

Under it, for the first time in forty years, he wrote a name.
