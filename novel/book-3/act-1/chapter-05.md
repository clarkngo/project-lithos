# Chapter Five — The Spire Road

The House had sent a coach, and Caelen refused to ride in it for four days.

It was a long, lacquered, blue-dark thing with a thread of silver on its doors, drawn by four grey horses no one in the Wastes had ever fed properly. It came for them at the edge of the contested ground, sent by the courier's word, with a steward riding beside it on a bay mare. The steward was a lean, immaculate man called Halloran. He did everything with precise, quiet courtesy, and Caelen came to understand that this was how House Ilvane disagreed with things.

"The archivist rides inside, naturally," Halloran said, the first morning. "You and your companion, sir, are welcome to. Or to walk. The House has no strong preference."

"Then we walk," Caelen said, and Rook, who had been eyeing the coach's cushions with grim fascination, said nothing at all.

So they walked, the two of them, beside the horses, and Seraphina rode alone inside in a silence they respected. Halloran rode a little apart. He watched, Caelen noticed, without appearing to watch, the way a man reads a room he has been told to count.

---

The Wastes let go of them on the second day, and this time Caelen was ready.

He had known the feeling once already, the ground going mute under his feet, the wordless certainty he'd carried since the mountain simply ceasing to answer. It didn't frighten him now. It only made the road plain. The country north of the contested edge was ordinary, hedged, cultivated, laid out in the neat grid of land that had never been mined to the bone, and he walked it the slow honest way anyone did, reading the sky and the mud and the faces.

It was the faces that changed.

At the first village they stopped in, a woman in the doorway of a bakery looked at Caelen for a long moment and then said something to her daughter, quietly, and the girl came out with a small round loaf wrapped in cloth. She held it out without a word. He took it, because refusing seemed worse, and the woman said, "For the road, sir," and would not take money.

"She doesn't know you," Rook muttered, when they were past. "She can't."

"She knows something."

By the third village he understood. The bread was not a gift. It was a kind of recognition. Word had traveled, the way it always traveled, faster than any of them, and the word had a shape. *The strata child, who closed the door in the mountain. Who sat with the drifting until they were themselves again.* He heard it in a tavern yard, in a low rough voice over a fire, and he stopped walking. Someone was singing a song.

It was not a good song. The rhyme scheme wandered and the tune was borrowed from a harvest carol. It named him, and it named Varrick, wrongly, as a soldier, and it made a hero of a starving boy in a tree. A man with a fiddle stopped when he saw Caelen standing in the lane, and his face went through three things in a second: delight, embarrassment, awe.

"I didn't mean —" the fiddler began.

"It's all right," Caelen said. He heard how it sounded and could not make it sound different. "It's a good tune."

He walked on with his ears burning, and did not speak for a mile.

---

"You could hide," Seraphina said that evening, when they'd made camp in a dry meadow off the road. She had at last consented to walk with them for an hour, and they sat near the fire with the coach looming grey in the dusk. "A hood. A different name. It might serve you until the hall."

"Would it?" Caelen said.

"No." She considered him. "It would serve you until the first person who wanted to ask you something. And in the Spire, someone always does."

He turned the loaf in his hands. He'd eaten half of it and put the rest in his pack, unable to say why. For nine years he had learned to be forgettable. He had taken pride in it, quietly, in the way a craftsman takes pride in a joint that doesn't show. Now the joint showed. A stranger in a doorway could see it from across a lane.

"I don't know what to do with it," he said, at last. "Being looked at. In the Wastes, if someone looked at me for too long it meant something was wrong. I keep waiting for the wrongness."

"There isn't any," Rook said, from the other side of the fire, unusually gentle. "That's the strange part. They just — like you. Like you're theirs. Like you did something for them personally."

"I didn't. I closed a door. I held a man's hand."

"You did those things for people," Rook said. "Which is what it looks like from outside. It doesn't matter that you didn't know their names."

Caelen said nothing. He thought of Varrick saying *terms, not charity,* a debt worked out as they went. He thought of a bakery loaf and a girl's careful hands. He did not know what the debt was, or who was owed, or what it would cost when it came due.

---

On the fifth evening the steward rode up alongside him at the head of the column, and Caelen realized Halloran had been waiting for exactly that opportunity.

"May I ask you something, sir?" the steward said. "Not in the House's name. In my own."

"You can ask."

"Whether it is true." Halloran kept his eyes ahead, on the darkening road. "What they say of the Lord's son. The young master Tomas. I was a junior footman in the household at the time. I was not permitted to see him, but I carried the trays. At the end they stopped needing me to carry them." His jaw moved. "I have never been told what happened to him. I have only been told to forget I asked."

Caelen looked at the man in the grey wool, correct and immaculate and shaking almost imperceptibly on his saddle.

"That isn't mine to tell," he said. "It's hers. But I'll tell you this. She has never once forgotten. Not for a day."

"No," Halloran said, very quietly. "I did not think she had." A long pause. "The House will not thank me for it. But I would like her to know that someone in it didn't want to be told to forget."

He touched his heels to the mare and dropped back, and did not speak to Caelen again that day.

---

They saw the Spire on the sixth morning, from the crest of a long ridge where the road came up out of the fields.

Caelen stopped. Rook stopped beside him. Even Seraphina, who had ridden past that ridge a hundred times as a girl, climbed down from the coach and stood at the cliff's edge with her hand at her mouth.

It was a tower. He had heard the word in stories and never understood it. It rose out of a plain of pale stone and glittering roofs, ring on ring, terrace on terrace, a whole city climbing itself toward a single needle of white that vanished into the low cloud. No smoke hung over it. No ash. No rusted ribs of dead machines. It stood in the morning light as clean as a tooth, and there was something in that cleanness, some bright unhurt quality, that made the back of his neck go cold.

He had spent his whole life among wounds. He had never seen a place that looked as though it had never been hurt.

"Everything in it is built on top of something else," Seraphina said quietly, beside him. "That is the thing I never understood as a girl. That you could live at the top of a city and never once see what it stood on."

Caelen nodded, slowly. He had no words. He looked at the pale terraces and the far small glimmer of a hundred windows, and understood that whatever waited for them in that tower, it would not be a wound he could read with his hands.

Down the road behind them, Halloran had dismounted and stood bareheaded in the wind, and the coach sat waiting, and the horses stamped, indifferent.

"Ready?" Rook said.

"No," Caelen said. It was the most honest word he had. He shouldered his pack, with the last half-loaf of a stranger's kindness still tucked at the bottom, and started down toward the city.
