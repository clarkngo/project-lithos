# Chapter Three — Reth's Correction

The broadside reached the camp before the Guild did, pinned inside a bundle of dried resin-bark by a broker in Underlow who had decided, for reasons he did not explain, that Seraphina ought to have it for free.

Rook found it on the third day of their preparations, folded small against the bundle's bottom. He carried it to the fire and read it standing, jaw working, and then handed it to Caelen without a word, which in Rook was its own kind of sentence.

It was printed on the cheap grey stock the Guild used for tariffs and notices of weight. The heading ran across the top in the plain block letters of a ledger clerk. *A PETITION TO THE SYNOD OF THE CENTRAL HALL, FOR THE UNIFORM DISPOSITION OF LITHOI REMAINS AND THE LICENSING OF RESONANCE THEREFROM.* Beneath it, in smaller type, four hundred words that Caelen read twice, slowly, the way Varrick had once taught him to read a contract: for the thing it said, and for the thing it was built to make him believe it said.

*Whereas the present disposition of Lithoi remains is inconsistent, contested, and productive of harm. Whereas unlicensed practice endangers those who attempt it and those who surround them. Whereas the several authorities of Guild, Warden, and Synod hold overlapping and contradictory claims, to the ruin of any orderly policy. The Guild therefore petitions the Synod to establish a single enforceable standard.*

There followed a list of provisions, and every one of them sounded like mercy. Uniform licensing, so that no family need fear a Warden's doctrine for a mother's desperate Shift. Guild-funded conduits, subsidized, for the poor. A registry of the drifting, so that none would be left unseen. At the bottom, in a hand different from the clerk's, one signature.

*Reth, for the Board.*

"It says it wants to fix Greyfen," Rook said, low. "Right there, second clause. A licensed process so a woman drowning in a cellar doesn't get killed for saving her boy."

"It does say that," Caelen said.

"Then what's wrong with it?"

Caelen folded the sheet along its crease and did not answer for a moment. He was still learning how to say the thing he felt in his chest whenever Seraphina read a Synod clause aloud. It was the same instinct that had once told him a strip of dried meat left on a stone was an offer and not a gift.

"A registry of the drifting," he said. "They'd have a list of everyone like Fenn. Everyone like me."

Rook went very still.

"And who keeps the list?" Malakar said, quietly, from behind them. He had read the sheet over Caelen's shoulder. "It does not say. That is what an Inquisitor learns to look for first. Not what a document promises. Who it hands the keys to when it is done promising."

---

They rode to Underlow the next morning, the two of them, Caelen and Rook, to buy the salt and rope and oiled hide a journey to the Spire would require, and because Caelen had taken to carrying a small brass token in his coat pocket since a woman in grey left it on a stone in the spring. He had not planned to use it. He had only stopped being able to leave it behind.

He did not have to. She was waiting at the trading post's edge, sitting on an upended crate beside the broker's stall, in plain travelling clothes, with none of the composed arrangement he remembered.

"I hoped you'd come here," Kell said. "I did not think you'd come this quickly."

"You're not Director anymore," Caelen said.

"I am Auditor of Deferred Accounts, Eastern Territories. It is a title the Board invented in a week, and it has a desk in a room with no window." She said it without self-pity, as she'd once said a price. "I asked to see you before I was sent out of reach. I did not think you'd take my calling it a favor."

"Is it?"

"It is information." She looked at Rook, then back. "I would like it noted that I have no ledger for this. I am not offering it for your terms. I am offering it because I have spent a year reading the Board's correspondence, and I am the only person left in your world who has read all of it."

Rook's hand had moved, without his deciding it, to his belt. Kell watched it, unruffled.

"You closed a seal I could not have forced," she said. "In your way, and not the way I planned. I made an arrangement with Custodian Voss that I told myself was a controlled fraction, and it collapsed the moment anyone looked at it. I have been reassigned to the audit of my own mistakes. It is thorough, and it is fair, and I find I do not resent it." A thin dry breath. "That is not why I sent for you."

"Then why," Caelen said.

"Because Reth does not think I failed by being too patient." She let that sit. "He thinks I failed by being too small. I bargained for a survey allowance. I asked a Custodian for a licensed fraction. Reth reads that and sees a woman who spent a year on the wrong question, and he has gone to change the question."

---

She spoke for a long while, low, over the noise of the market, and Caelen understood as she talked why the Board had needed a week to find a title small enough to keep her in.

Reth was not, in her telling, a hungry man. That was what frightened her about him. He had come up through the Guild's Hollow Cut accounts, in the years when the settlement still shipped marrow-glass by the cartload, and he had spent a career reading numbers of the dead: crew losses, unlicensed Shift deaths, drift cases filed and dismissed. He believed, with a completeness she could not fully mock, that the territory's chaos killed more people than any single authority could. Warden doctrine, Synod silence, Guild extraction, fringe mercy. Four systems, four sets of rules, and a ledger of bodies at the bottom of all of them.

"He does not want the Vault," Kell said. "That is what everyone who has tried to stop him keeps misunderstanding. He would be embarrassed by the Vault. It is a treasure. He wants a *system*. A single register, a single license, a single enforceable standard for every Aethel-Core in the territory, and for every body that carries one. He wants to close the fringe."

"Close it how," Caelen said.

"Registry. Inspection. Subsidy for the compliant, and prohibition for the rest, applied evenly." She looked at him. "There will be no Wastes left to hide in, Caelen. That is the whole design. You spent nine years surviving because nobody with authority wanted the ground badly enough to watch it closely. Reth wants to watch all of it. Not from greed. From arithmetic."

Rook made a small sound, half-laugh, half-something worse. "That's Fenn's word."

"It is everyone's word who ever spent a life counting the dead," Kell said. "It is what makes him dangerous. He isn't wrong about the arithmetic. He is only wrong about who has the right to be the one carrying it."

Caelen thought of a ledger that did not say who kept the list.

"Why tell us?" he said. "You could sit in your windowless room and let it happen. It would cost you nothing."

"It would cost me the one thing I have left." For the first time something moved behind the composure, and it was not calculation. "I built an arrangement in the dark and told myself it was a compromise. Then I read the reports of how that seal was closed, and I understood the difference between a controlled fraction and a promise kept. I would like, before they finish putting me in a drawer, to have done one thing that was not a ledger." A small dry breath. "I would also like Reth to have to answer for his correction in front of witnesses. And you, I believe, are going to be one."

"We've been invited to the Spire," Caelen said. It was not a question, and she did not answer it as one.

"I know," she said. "That is why the broker gave Seraphina the broadside. I asked him to."

---

They rode back at dusk. Caelen said little, and Rook said less, and both of them let the wind carry what the road could not.

"She could be lying," Rook said, near the camp. "All of it. Setting us up for Reth. Sending us to the Spire to be counted."

"She could." Caelen watched the low fires of the camp come into view, the forge dark among them. "But I don't think she is. I think she finally told the truth about something and doesn't know what to do with her hands."

Seraphina met them at the western marker. She had heard the shape of it before either could speak, in the set of their shoulders, and she took the broadside from Caelen's coat and read it in the dying light with her lips pressed white.

"A registry of the drifting," she said. "Guild-funded rites, administered through licensed authority." She let the paper fall to her side. "Do you know what that is? It is Voss's arrangement, made permanent. Made *law*. And it is dressed, this time, as exactly the thing I have spent two years trying to put into the world."

"Then we go," Caelen said. "The hall, and your father, and Reth. All of it lands in the same place."

"It does." She drew a breath. "I have written the courier's answer. It says that I accept, and that I will bring companions, and that they are not to be treated as retainers." A short unamused breath. "I did not mention Tomas. I decided that was the reply I would give him. Let him wonder which door I chose."

She folded the broadside once and tucked it inside her coat, beside the letter, where the shard had once ridden.

In the morning the courier rode north with her answer. Caelen watched him go until the horse and its fine grey rider shrank to nothing against the flat silver line of the Wastes, and understood, without needing anyone to say it aloud, that the country behind them was already closing.
