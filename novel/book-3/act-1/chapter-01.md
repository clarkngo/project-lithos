# Chapter One — Weeks, Not Months

The forge was still warm at first light, which was how Caelen knew Fenn had not slept.

Ten months had worn the camp into a different shape than the one Varrick had left it. The rise above it carried a low mound now, turned earth gone hard and grey under the autumn, and the path to it had been trodden clear without anyone deciding to tread it. Two of the archivists Seraphina had taught, a stooped man from the southern settlements and a woman in her mid-twenties, had built themselves a lean-to beside the infirmary and stayed. The clearing behind the largest structure no longer belonged to one exhausted woman. It belonged to whoever could hold a working steady, and on the best mornings that was three people instead of one.

Fenn had watched all of it from the forge's doorway, a stick in his good hand and a stool he no longer pretended he didn't need.

"You're up early," he said, not turning, when Caelen's shadow crossed the threshold.

"You're up still." Caelen crouched beside the cold end of the bench, where a housing lay half-finished in its vise, brass dulled to the soft gold that came before polish. "Seraphina said weeks. That was ten months ago."

"She said weeks, not months, and I've been insulting the arithmetic ever since." The wry note was still there, worn thin as a coin. "I'd like it entered somewhere that I did it out of spite."

He had been dying by inches for the better part of two years, and none of them had found a way to say the word to his face that he hadn't already said first, lighter, so they'd have nothing left to lift. The permanent hitch in his shoulders had drawn up into the base of his neck. His hands still held steady on brass. That was the last thing his body would give up, he'd told them once, because it was the only part of him that had ever been trained to be honest.

---

Caelen felt it in the middle of the morning, while they worked the last fitting together.

He had learned the shape of the warning in Fenn's body across a year of nights, and it did not arrive the way a flare arrived. There was no wrongness in the air, no low wet catch in the breath. It came the way frost comes to a window, one fine line at a time. Fenn's hands slowed on the file. His eyes lifted to Caelen's face and held there, a beat too long, and this time the effort of dragging them away did not work.

"Ah," Fenn said, very quietly. "That's the one."

"Not yet." Caelen already had the coal lit behind his sternum, small and banked and entirely still, the same fixed point he'd held in a stone circle for a stranger with a bundle of brass tools. His skin drew tight across his shoulders. He kept it exactly that tight and no tighter. "Not yet, Fenn. Look at me. Not through me."

"I am looking." The old brass-worker's dryness surfaced through the fear, and Caelen loved him for it with an ache that had nothing left of arithmetic in it. "I'm just not sure the rest of me's going to keep doing it. Seraphina told me straight, at the start. The rite holds it back. It doesn't send it home."

"Then don't go home yet. Stay here. Stay in the forge."

He held the anchor, and the hunger came for it.

It did not come the way it had come at the camp's outer marker the night three strangers walked in with blades. Nothing in it wanted a fight. It came slow and patient and almost gentle, the vast old undertow Caelen knew from the inside, reaching for the last warm thing in the room that still answered to a name. It found his stillness and leaned on it, and he understood, bracing against the weight, that Fenn was not fighting it. He was letting it come close. He was choosing, deliberately, the terms on which he'd meet it.

"I don't want to go out as that," Fenn said. His voice had gone thin, and every word cost something visible. "The thing it makes. I've seen what it makes. I'd like to go out as the man who fit housings badly for cheap and finished them right anyway. Can you hold that for me? Just that? However long it takes?"

"Yes," Caelen said, and did not know, saying it, whether it was true.

---

It took most of an hour, and it was the hardest thing he had ever done without moving.

Seraphina came and sat behind Fenn's stool with her hand flat against his back, murmuring the working under her breath, softer than any rite she had performed. Rook came and stood in the doorway and did not speak. The two archivists hovered outside, uncertain, until the older man ducked in and set a kettle on the coals, simply because somebody needed to do something with their hands, and no one sent him away.

Caelen held. The coal behind his sternum burned steady and small, and the cold gathered at the edges of him the way it always did, a debt accruing that he'd pay in shaking afterward. He did not spend it on strength. He spent it on being solid, the one fixed thing in a room where everything else was loosening.

Fenn's breath slowed by degrees. The unblinking stillness in his eyes softened, then cleared, then simply went gentle. He looked at the half-finished housing in its vise, and he looked at the kettle, and at Rook in the doorway, and last at Caelen.

"It fits," he said. "Tell whoever wears it. It fits properly."

"I'll tell them."

"Good." His hand found the file and did not lift it. "Didn't spend it on anything grand. Did you notice? Nobody to fight. Nothing to save. Just this." Something like a laugh caught in his chest. "Varrick would've said that's the worst arithmetic in the world."

"He'd have been wrong," Caelen said.

"He usually was, about that particular sum." Fenn's eyes closed. "Stay a while."

Caelen stayed. He held the coal steady until there was nothing left in the room for it to hold, and for a long minute afterward he did not let it go, because letting it go meant the forge would go quiet.

---

He came out of it on his knees, hollowed, shaking with a cold that went all the way to marrow, and he did not remember lying down. Rook's arms were around him. They had been there some time.

"He went as himself," Rook said, low, wrecked, saying it the way Fenn would have wanted it said, plainly. "That's what you gave him. I want you to hear me say it. Not the thing it makes. Him."

Caelen nodded. He had no other answer. Across the forge Seraphina sat with her hand still flat against Fenn's back and her face wet, not moving, not speaking, counting nothing at all.

They buried him on the rise the next morning, not far from the older mound. Caelen set the finished housing in the ground with him, its brass gone to that soft polished gold at last, and knew as he did it that it would never be worn by anyone, and that Fenn would have laughed.

Afterward he stood at the western marker a long while, the way he'd stood there every evening since another grave, and found that the grief was not the same as the last one. It did not arrive as a wave or a weight. It arrived as a place in the day where a voice used to be, complaining mildly about arithmetic.

*Weeks, not months.* Somewhere behind him the forge sat cold for the first time in a year, and out on the flat silver edge of the Wastes the wind moved through the rig-bones with the sound it always made, patient, unhurried, in no way concerned with what the camp had lost.

He turned back toward the fire, because there were still people at it, and that was the whole of what any of them had ever known how to do.
