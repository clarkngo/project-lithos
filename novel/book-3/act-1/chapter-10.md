# Chapter Ten — What Corwin Risks

The message came to the green-doored house at dawn on the second day of the hearing, and it was not addressed to Caelen.

It was addressed to Corwin, in a hand so square and expensive that the paper seemed to carry a smell of ink and money, and it was delivered by a footman in the deep red livery of House Vael, who stood on the doorstep with his gloves folded and looked at no one. Corwin read it in the front room, standing, with tea going cold on a table beside him, and Caelen watched his face empty of expression the way a room empties of sound.

"My father," he said. "Requesting my presence at the family table this evening. To *discuss my recent associations.*" He set the letter down very carefully. "It is the first time he has written to me directly in three years."

"You don't have to go," Rook said.

"That is the thing about a summons in this city." Corwin's voice was light. "It is never a question of whether one has to. It is only a question of what one is choosing to lose."

---

He went, in the end. Caelen walked him as far as the fourth terrace, where the road rose toward the upper rings, and stood in the shade of a colonnade and watched him climb until the crowd took him.

He returned near midnight, and he did not come in through the green door. Rook found him sitting on the front step in the dark, coat open, hands hanging between his knees, and helped him in without a word.

It took Corwin a long time to tell it, and when he did he told it flatly, the way a man tells a story about someone else.

His father had not shouted. There had been no scene. The family had sat down at a long table under a great dim chandelier, and his father, an immense and quiet man in the deep red of Vael, had asked whether it was true that Corwin was hosting the strata child at his mother's house. When Corwin said yes, his father had inclined his head, and turned to the family's steward, and said: *Please note that the House no longer recognizes any association with the second son's household.* And that had been all.

"I keep my mother's house," Corwin said. "That isn't Vael's. They can't take it. But the allowance is gone. My seat at the table. My name on the family's letters of introduction, which, in this city, is the same as not having a name." He almost laughed. "I've been disinherited by minute."

"Corwin," Caelen said.

"That isn't the part." The lightness went out of his voice entirely. He looked up, and his eyes in the dim front room were red-rimmed and very steady. "The part is Lisbet. I saw her, afterward. They keep her in the east wing, with a nurse. She is better than she has been in two years, and I sat with her an hour, and she asked how the strange man from the wastes was. She had heard. She's read the broadside. She said..." His voice cracked. "She said she'd like to meet you. She wants to know if it's true you held a man so he could die as himself."

Rook made a sound and turned away.

"And my father," Corwin said, "is going to move her. Tomorrow, or the next day. The House Physician has recommended registration. A licensed facility, up in the fifth ring. *For her own protection.* He told me so at the door." His hands closed into fists on his knees. "If Reth's petition passes, it will simply be the law. If it doesn't, they'll do it anyway. Under the old doctrine. Because it's *safer.* I did this. I made myself visible, and now she is."

---

Nobody spoke. Outside, a wind had come up over the terraces, and somewhere a shutter slapped against stone in a slow steady rhythm, like a heartbeat.

Caelen sat with it. He was aware of Rook's tense stillness beside him, and of Seraphina, who had come in an hour ago from the House and had said nothing, sitting on the stair with her hands wrapped around her elbows. He was aware of something else too. It was a feeling he had not had since the ridge above Hollow Cut, when a man he loved had chosen to stand where the worst would land.

He had spent nine years learning to spend himself. He had learned to spend his own body, his own hunger, his own careful discipline. He had never learned what it meant to watch another person spend their whole life on his behalf, freely, knowingly, and be unable to stop them.

"You said you'd decided knowingly," he said quietly.

"I did."

"Then I'm going to ask something, and you can say no." He made himself hold Corwin's eyes. "The hall is going to hear us tomorrow, and the day after. Everyone in that room is going to see us. If your sister were in it, in the gallery, where anyone could see her, and she were *there,* with us, she'd be registered in the eyes of the whole Spire. But she'd also be *known.* People don't easily send a face they've seen to a locked ward. They send a name."

"You want me to bring my sister to the hall."

"I want you to decide whether you'd rather she be safe and hidden, or safe and seen." Caelen breathed out. "I don't know which is right. I've only ever been hidden. I'm just beginning to see what it costs."

Corwin looked at him for a long time. Then he laughed, short and cracked and wholly real.

"You know," he said, "I have been so careful my whole life. So very careful. And the one time I do a reckless thing, it turns out to be the only thing I was ever for."

---

They went to the Hall of Canon together the next morning, all of them: Caelen and Rook and Seraphina, Corwin in his good grey coat with the mended cuff, and on his arm, small and pale and very upright in a plain dress, a girl of nineteen whose hands trembled slightly at her sides and whose eyes tracked the great pale columns with wary, wondering attention.

Lisbet had insisted on walking. She would not be carried into the hall like a ward.

The gallery went quiet as they entered. Caelen felt it as a physical thing, a long ripple of attention spreading outward and upward through the tiers, and knew, without needing to look, that every eye in the great vaulted room had turned. Below, in the horseshoe, Custodian Maren looked up. Reth, at his side table, did not look up at all; he only paused, very slightly, in the act of turning a page.

A clerk at the gallery's edge held out a slim book and a pen. It was the witness register.

"Names, please," the clerk said, low. "For the record. The Custodian has ruled that all who speak here speak on the roll, and that no testimony given may be struck from it afterward."

Caelen took the pen. It was a very light thing, and it felt like a great weight.

He had never once, in nine years, signed his name to anything. He had lived by being a rumor. He had been a boy in a tree, a shape on a ridge, a name whispered in the Wastes. To write it now, in a clear hand, in a book in the Hall of Canon, was to become something that could be found.

*Caelen,* he wrote, and after a moment, because there was nothing else, he wrote nothing more.

Rook signed beneath him, a scrawl. Seraphina's hand, steady, formal. Corwin, and then, after a long hesitation and a look at her brother, Lisbet, in a small, careful, clear script.

The clerk blotted the page and closed the book. It was done, and could not be undone, and behind Caelen, in the gallery, a low sound began, not applause, not anything so simple. It was the sound of a great many people drawing a breath and holding it.

He thought of Varrick. Of a scavenger who had kept a boy alive by teaching him to be forgotten. He thought that Varrick would have hated this, and would have understood it, and would have said something dry about arithmetic. And he stood in the light of the high grey windows, among the people who had chosen to stand beside him, and felt the last of nine years' careful invisibility fall away like a coat he hadn't known was too small.

Then he went down into the hall.
