# Chapter Eight — The Central Hall

The Hall of Canon had been built, Corwin said, so that no one who entered it could forget they were small.

Caelen believed him. He stood in the public gallery on the first morning of the hearing with Rook at his shoulder and Corwin a pace behind, and looked down into a chamber that seemed to have no ceiling. Pale columns rose out of a floor of dark stone to a vault so high the light from its clerestory windows arrived diffused and grey, like weather. Below, in a great horseshoe, sat the Custodians, forty-one of them in undyed robes, tier on tier, so quiet and so evenly spaced that they looked less like people than like a decision made in stone.

At the horseshoe's open end stood a single lectern, and behind it, a smaller table, and in the center of the whole vast room a chair of pale wood, larger than the others, empty.

"The Eldest's seat," Corwin murmured. "She has not sat in it for three weeks. They say she is unwell. They also say a great deal else."

"She was at the Vault," Caelen said quietly. "At the door. She looked tired then."

"She looked tired at the last ten hearings," Corwin said. "The difference is that now nobody is sure who is in her chair when she isn't."

---

Malakar and Orell arrived just before the opening bell, slipping into the gallery's back row in plain dark cloaks, hoods drawn. Caelen felt them before he saw them, the small settling of two familiar weights at the room's edge. Malakar's face under the hood was very still. Orell's eyes went to the Wardens' gallery across the hall, a block of grey and brass along the wall, and stayed there a moment before he made himself look away.

"Petra," Malakar said, low, in Caelen's ear. "The tribunal has agreed to produce her as a witness on the fourth day. Kell's desk made the request. It was routine paperwork, and nobody thought to stop it." Something almost like awe crossed his composed voice. "She said she would do one thing that was not a ledger."

Caelen glanced at him. "It worked."

"It has not worked yet. It has merely not been refused."

The bell rang, a single low note that seemed to come from the floor.

---

A man rose from the lectern's side table, and the whole room went quiet in the particular way a room does when it has been waiting for one person.

Chairman Reth was not what Caelen had braced for. He was small, and thin, and plain. He wore a grey coat cut for use rather than display, and carried no papers, and his hair was going white in a way that suggested he had stopped caring about its color long ago. He walked to the lectern without haste and stood there a moment with his hands lightly folded and looked at the Custodians, one by one, and it seemed to Caelen, watching, that he was not looking at their faces. He was looking at where they held their hands.

"Custodians," Reth said. His voice was quiet and dry and very clear. "I will not speak long. I am not a man who is served by length."

He did not speak of the Vault. He did not speak of the Culling, or of Lithoi, or of marrow. He spoke, in the first quarter-hour, of numbers.

"In the eastern territories in the last five years," he said, "eleven hundred and four unlicensed Shifts were formally recorded. Of these, six hundred and eleven ended in death. Four hundred and fifty of the dead were the Shifters themselves. One hundred and sixty-one were bystanders. Of the four hundred and fifty, three hundred and twelve were killed by Warden containment, and one hundred and thirty-eight died of the Shift, or of the drift that followed it, unattended. I have the names." He lifted one hand. A clerk stepped forward with a slim leather book and set it on the lectern. "They are in here. I have read them all. I would ask the hall to remember that they are names. I do not ask it to weep. I ask it to remember that in the disposition of these lives, no single authority is responsible, and therefore no authority is."

Caelen felt something cold settle under his ribs. It was the same thing he'd felt reading the broadside. Reth was not lying. He was not exaggerating. Every word was true, and every word had been placed with the care of a man laying stone.

"The Wardens contain, and kill. The Synod is silent, and lets them drift. The Guild extracts, and licenses unevenly. The fringe, out of love, improvises, and some of it works, and much of it does not, and nobody keeps a count." He let that settle. "I do not accuse any of these of malice. I accuse the arrangement. It is four hands on a single load, each convinced the others are carrying more. I propose that we choose one."

---

He spoke for an hour. He proposed nothing outrageous. A single office, staffed by the Synod's own trained physicians, funded by Guild subsidy, licensed by the hall. A register of Shifters so that none would be missed. A rite of treatment, on the fringe's own model but properly trained. He named it, once, without emphasis: *the archivist's method*. The hall stirred at that. Caelen felt Rook go rigid beside him.

"He's stealing it," Rook whispered. "He's putting Seraphina's name in the mouth of a registry."

"Yes."

"And they're *listening*."

They were. That was what struck Caelen most, looking down at the tiers of quiet robes. They were listening the way a starving man listens to a man who has promised bread. They had lived their whole lives inside a system that let people die without anyone being responsible, and a small plain man with a leather book had just told them, correctly, that it was so.

When Reth finished he simply stopped, and stood, and did not smile or bow. The silence afterward was long and unlike any silence Caelen had heard before.

Then a voice spoke from the horseshoe, low and unsteady, and every head turned.

---

She was young. That was the first thing. She sat in one of the lower tiers, at the horseshoe's near end, in a robe that seemed cut a little too large for her, and she had risen without seeming to decide to. Her hands were pressed flat against her thighs. She looked, to Caelen's eyes, like a woman standing on the edge of something.

"Custodian Maren," said the presiding voice from the empty chair's left, an old man's, dry with age. "The hall recognizes you as acting Eldest, in accordance with the Eldest's stated wishes."

Corwin sucked in a breath beside him. "Maren. They gave it to *Maren*."

"The younger one," Caelen said. "The one after Voss."

"Twenty-nine. She's been a Custodian for four years."

Maren moved to the center of the floor. She stopped a few paces short of the Eldest's empty chair, and looked at it for a long moment, and did not sit in it. Then she turned and faced the hall.

"I have listened to Chairman Reth," she said. Her voice shook, and she let it. "I would like to say, first, that I have not the standing to answer him. I have been a Custodian four years. I inherited a docket I did not choose. I do not know what the Eldest would say."

A murmur ran through the tiers.

"But I know what the hall does not know," Maren said. "I know that it has never, in three generations, heard testimony. Not from the Wardens, not from the fringe, not from those whom the doctrine has touched. It has heard from itself. And I think, Custodians, that is not an accident." She drew a breath. "Chairman Reth has read us the names of the dead. I would like, before we vote on any register, to hear from the living."

Reth, at his lectern, did not move. But his eyes rested on her a moment, mild and grey and attentive, and Caelen understood, with a small cold clarity, that he had just watched the small plain man reassess a piece on the board.

"The hall will hear testimony," Maren said, "over the next five days. From anyone who wishes to speak, and whose speaking is put on the record. The petition is not a mandate. It is a question. I will not rule on it until the hall has heard what it costs." She did not sit. "That is my provisional judgment. I have no authority to make it. I am making it anyway."

She sat down then, all at once, her hands shaking in her lap, and the room broke into low sound.

---

Reth found Caelen on the hall's outer steps at noon, in the blue shadow of a column, when the crowd was still streaming down into the plaza.

He came alone. He had no clerk, no escort. He simply paused beside Caelen and looked out at the plaza and the tall bright city, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"I have read your file," Reth said, without preamble. "All of it. The Guild's, the Warden's, the Synod's, and the small private one Custodian Voss kept in her desk, which I found interesting."

"I've been told."

"I do not hate you, Mister Caelen." He said it in the same dry level tone he'd used to read a death count. "I want that understood before we speak again. I think you are the finest argument for my petition that could be devised. A boy who survived a mountain, who by luck and love and a series of improbable kindnesses became a man who could hold his own hunger at arm's length for nine years. And I think that outcome, in the general run of cases, is one my ledger has never once recorded." He turned his grey, patient eyes on Caelen. "You are the one who lived. I have the names of the others."

Caelen felt the words settle over him like weather. It was not a threat. It was the worst kind of accuracy.

"I'd like to know who kept the list," he said.

Something moved in Reth's face, very briefly, that might have been surprise, or respect. "That," he said, "is the correct question." He inclined his head, once, courteous. "I look forward to your testimony."

He walked away down the steps, small and plain and unhurried, into the bright noon crowd, and Caelen stood a long time in the column's shadow, feeling for the first time in his life the peculiar horror of having met an opponent who was, in every way that counted, not wrong.
