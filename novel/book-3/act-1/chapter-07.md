# Chapter Seven — House Ilvane

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Seraphina. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**SERAPHINA**

The house had not changed, and that was the first cruelty of it.

She had prepared herself for alterations. A new hanging in the vestibule, a rearranged stair, the small violences a household inflicts on a room to make it forget a person. There were none. The white marble ran cool underfoot. The long gallery smelled of beeswax and cut lilies. The clock in the west hall struck the quarter with the same slightly flat note it had struck through her childhood, because her father had never permitted it to be repaired. *A clock is not a person,* he had said once. *It does not require to be perfect in order to be relied upon.*

Halloran walked a pace behind her and did not speak. At the door of the study he bowed, and for a moment his eyes met hers with something in them she could not name, and then he was gone.

Her father stood at the window with his back to the room.

"Seraphina," Lord Ilvane said, without turning. "You've grown thin."

"I have been working." She stood inside the door with her hands folded, and heard the court in her own voice, every contraction gone, every syllable placed. It did not comfort her. It felt like armor she had put on in her sleep. "You have not changed at all, Father."

"I make a point of it." He turned then. He was a tall man, silver at the temple, with a long composed face that had never once, in her memory, arranged itself into anything it had not chosen. "Sit. There is tea. I had it made the way you took it, if you have not changed that either."

She had. She sat anyway.

---

For a while they spoke of nothing. The journey. The weather on the ridge road. Whether the steward had been sufficiently attentive. It was a dance she knew in her bones, and she moved through it without a misstep, and understood as she did that this was what he wanted her to feel: how easily she still knew the steps.

"You did not mention your brother," her father said, at last, "in your reply."

The teacup was very light in her hand. "Nor did you in your letter."

"No." He inclined his head, almost a bow, acknowledging a point scored. "I have been a long time considering how to say his name in this room. I find that I have not learned it."

"You could say it now."

"I could." He turned the ring on his finger, once. "Tomas."

It was a small sound, and it went through her like a blade. Four years. She had forgotten, or had trained herself to forget, that her father's voice could make the name sound like a thing that had been loved. She sat quite still and let it pass through, and waited, and he did not go on.

"That is all I can manage," Lord Ilvane said, quietly. "I would like you to understand that it is not for want of grief."

"Then for what?"

"For want of a House."

---

He told her, then, in the level unhurried voice he used for accounts, and she listened with her hands folded and her heart going hard and steady like a drum in a distant room.

The House of Ilvane had stood for nine generations, he said. It had stood by never once being the subject of a story. The Spire's old families did not survive on wealth or blood. They survived on the absence of anything that might, on a slow afternoon, be whispered. A drifting son was a whisper. A son *returned to the mountains for treatment* was a scandal that could not be lived down in three generations, because it proved that the House had known and had failed, and the whole edifice of marriage and treaty and quiet obligation that held its name upright depended on the belief that Ilvanes did not fail in private.

"I did not send him because I did not love him," her father said. "I sent him because I had two other children and a name that fed forty households, and I could not find a way to keep all of them. I have gone over it every night for four years, and I have not found the way. I wish very much that you could tell me where I erred."

"You could have kept him."

"And watched him become what the drifting become, in my house, in front of the servants? I think not." A flicker of something crossed his face, and was gone. "I had him kept comfortable to the end. I am told he did not suffer. I am told a great many things."

Seraphina set the teacup on its saucer. It made no sound at all. "You knew I'd gone."

"Yes."

"You knew where. You knew the night. You left the postern unwatched."

For a long moment he did not answer, and she watched him decide what kind of man to be. Then, very quietly, in the voice of someone laying down a heavy thing: "The guard at the eastern postern took his supper early that evening. I gave no such order. I merely did not give the other one." He looked at his hands. "I told myself it was mercy. That if I could not keep you, I could at least not be the man who dragged you back. I have never been able to tell mercy from convenience. It is, I fear, a defect of my house."

She had suspected it. She had rehearsed the moment in her head across a hundred nights of the Wastes. She had expected relief, or fury, or some clean thing. She found only a long cold tiredness, like a room she had lived in too long.

"Thank you," she said. And meant it, and hated meaning it.

---

"I did not summon you to apologize," her father said, when the light had begun to lengthen across the floor. "I lack the standing. I summoned you because the hall convenes in eight days, and your rite will be spoken of there, and there is a thing I should like you to consider."

"I thought there might be."

"The House has a position. It is a comfortable one, and I have spent three years constructing it. Our story is that Tomas died abroad of a fever, while traveling for his health, and that you left the Spire in grief to carry out a work of charity in his memory. It is a good story. It has the great virtue of being almost true." He said it without irony. "In the hall, if the House speaks for the rite, the Spire will believe that story. It will also, I think, believe the rite. A House charity. Sanctioned. Quiet."

"And if I do not stand under the House's name."

"Then you stand alone, and the Spire will make its own story of you, and I will not be able to protect you from it." He met her eyes. "I am not threatening you. I want that quite clear. I am telling you what will happen."

She thought of a stone circle. Of a boy with brass tools who had asked for a season and been given one. Of a mother in a doorway with a daughter's hand held tight, saying *better, not cured.* Of a name that had come out of her father's mouth as if it were a stone he had carried in his pocket for four years.

"You want me to help you keep forgetting," she said.

"I want you to help me survive it." The composed face cracked, then, a hair's breadth, at the mouth. "I do not want to forget him, Seraphina. I have never been able to. I want to survive him. There is a distinction, and it is the whole of my life's work. I would like you to see it."

She did see it. That was the terrible thing. She saw it clearly, and she saw the long unlit corridor of the House behind it, generation on generation of people surviving a thing by pretending it had never happened, and she thought, *I could have been so good at this.*

"I will consider it," she said, rising, and heard the court in her own voice like a door closing gently behind her. "That is all I can promise. Not that I will agree. Only that I will not pretend it is a small thing you have asked."

"I would not have wished you to," said Lord Ilvane. He had not risen. He was looking at the window again, at the gold light on the pale terraces below. "It is more than I had hoped for. Your brother would have said the same. He always said you were the one who told the truth in this house."

She stopped with her hand on the door.

"He said that?"

"Often," her father said. "I did not think it was a compliment at the time."

She left before he could see what her face was doing, and it was not until she reached the long gallery, alone under the beeswax light and the smell of lilies, that she let herself lean against the cool wall and breathe. Somewhere overhead the flat-noted clock struck the hour, and she thought of every small thing in that house that was not perfect and had been allowed to remain, and wondered, for the first time in years, whether her father had meant it as a mercy or a confession. And whether, in a family like hers, the two had ever truly been separate.
