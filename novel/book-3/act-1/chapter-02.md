# Chapter Two — The Summons

The courier came on the ninth day after the burial, riding a horse too fine for the track, and Caelen understood before the man had fully dismounted that no one on this ground had ever been meant to see that livery.

It was plain by Spire standards, undyed grey wool with a single thread of dark blue at the collar, and he learned from Seraphina's face how much that plainness was worth. She had come out of the infirmary at the sound of hooves, wiping her hands, and stopped at the threshold with the cloth still in her fingers. The courier looked at her a long moment and bowed, not deeply, in the measured exact degree of a man who had been told precisely how deeply.

"Archivist Seraphina," he said. "I am instructed to place this in your hand and no other. I am instructed also to wait for an answer, if you are inclined to give one today, and if you are not, to wait for one regardless."

The letter was sealed in dark blue wax, pressed with a mark Caelen did not know: a slender tower with a thread wound about its base. He knew it now, though, from the way the color left her knuckles.

"House Ilvane," she said. She did not open it. "You may water your horse, and eat at the fire if you're hungry. You will not be asked anything you are not permitted to answer."

"That is considerate," the courier said, and it might have been sincere.

---

She read it in the forge, of all places, standing at the bench where a half-finished housing had been taken down and set aside, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for whoever would someday be told it fit. Caelen came to stand beside her without being asked. Rook leaned in the doorway. Malakar stood at the room's far edge, arms folded, with the particular stillness he brought to a document he intended to read twice.

"Read it aloud," Rook said, not unkindly. "Or don't. But I'd rather hear it than watch you read it with that face."

Seraphina drew a breath and read, and the careful architecture of her voice settled over the page like a lid.

"*To my daughter, in the second year of her absence from this House. It has come to my attention, by ways I need not trouble you with, that your labors among the fringe settlements have acquired a reputation that no family of consequence can any longer pretend not to have heard. I do not write to reproach you. I write because the House's position, and yours, has altered in ways that would be better discussed in person than by correspondence. I invite you to the Spire. Transport and safe conduct will be provided. The Synod's central hall convenes within the season on matters that touch your work, and I believe you would wish to be present when it does. Your father.*"

She lowered the page.

"That is all of it," she said. "Four sentences of consequence, and the rest is dressing."

"He doesn't say his name," Caelen said.

"He would consider that vulgar. A letter in that seal does not need a signature." Something in her jaw tightened. "But that isn't what you noticed."

"He doesn't say Tomas."

For a moment nobody spoke. The kettle from the day of the burial still sat on the cold hearth, and Caelen found himself looking at it and not at her.

"No," Seraphina said. "He does not. Four years, and a brother, and a House that let him be returned to the mountains for treatment, and in four careful sentences my father has not once written the name. Do you see how well that is done?" Her voice stayed level. It cost her visibly. "He wants me to notice the omission. He has left it there deliberately, the way you leave a door ajar to see who walks through it. If I mention Tomas in my reply, I have accepted that we are speaking of him. If I do not, I have accepted that we are not."

"Then you've got two ways to answer and both of them lose," Rook said.

"Three, in truth. I could not answer at all." She folded the letter along its crease. "That is also an answer. It is the one he has prepared for."

---

"It's a trap," Rook said flatly, after the others had drifted out and the four of them had gathered around the fire, the courier a careful distance away, eating slowly. "Not a knife-in-the-dark one. The kind where the door's open and there's a good dinner on the table."

"It is certainly a maneuver," Malakar said. He had taken the letter without asking and turned it over, studying the seal with an Inquisitor's practiced patience. "The wax is House stock, not couriered stock. Somebody stood over it while it cooled. He wanted it known this came from him and not a clerk." He handed it back. "But a maneuver is not always a trap. Sometimes it is only a man who does not know how to say what he wants."

"You think he wants her back?" Caelen asked.

"I think he wants something back," Malakar said. "The question is whether it is his daughter."

Orell had been quiet through all of it, sitting against a crate with his boots stretched to the fire, the way he'd sat every evening since the crossing. "The central hall convening," he said. "That's the piece I don't like. My contacts at Kesh Hollow have been passing along word for weeks that the Synod's Custodians are in some kind of uproar. Voss gone, a younger one in her chair. Nobody will say what for. Then a letter arrives with the words *matters that touch your work* in it." He shook his head slowly. "I don't believe in coincidence in my line of work, and I doubt you do in yours."

"Meaning what," Rook said.

"Meaning somebody's going to say something about the rite in front of that hall," Orell said. "And her father knows who, or thinks he does. And he'd like his daughter standing where she can be counted."

Seraphina sat very straight, and for the first time in months Caelen watched her reach for something she had once believed she'd put down. It was not the court voice. It was older than that: the child who had learned that a Spire House speaks in the grammar of favors and the only real reply is the one you decide not to give.

"There is something I have never told any of you," she said, quietly. "About how I was permitted to leave. Nearly two years ago I told you I fled. That was true in the way that matters. But no one stopped me at the gate. My father's guards did not follow me into the Wastes. The retrieval party did not find me for weeks, and when it did, it wore my House's colors, but I was never shown an order in his hand." She looked at the letter in her hands. "I have always believed he simply did not know where I'd gone. I am no longer certain that was the case."

The silence that followed was of a different kind than the one in the forge.

"You think he let you go," Caelen said.

"I think it is possible that a man who could not bear to keep me and could not bear to be seen to send me away found a third door and left it open." She let out a breath that was almost a laugh, and not a kind one. "It would be exactly like him. And I cannot say whether it was mercy or convenience. I have never been able to tell those apart in my father. That is the entire difficulty of him."

---

Caelen found her alone at the western marker that night, holding the letter against her chest the way he held a different one, folded soft from carrying.

"You're going," he said.

"I have not decided." She did not turn. "I told the courier I would answer within the week, and he said he would wait as long as required, and I don't doubt him. He has been waiting the better part of two years, in one way or another, for someone to send him home." A pause. "Fenn would have said it was arithmetic. That there is a sum here, and the sum is who gets to write Tomas's name first."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then it is only my father. And I would like, very much, to know whether that is a different problem." She finally looked at him, and the court architecture was entirely gone from her face. "Will you come, if I go?"

"Yes," Caelen said. He had not thought about it. It was the same yes he'd given at the ridge above a scarred country, and at the mouth of a chamber, and at a bedside in a forge. "I don't know what the Spire is, Seraphina. I've never been anywhere I couldn't read the ground. But you came to the Wastes for me once, when nobody had asked you to. I'd rather learn a new country than let you cross that one alone."

She nodded, slowly, and something eased in her shoulders that he had not known was held.

"Then we will need to learn it quickly," she said. "And there is one more thing about the hall you should know. My father did not say it, but I recognize the phrasing. *Matters that touch your work.* That is Guild language, not Synod. Somebody has already put the rite on the docket." She folded the letter once more and tucked it inside her coat, where the shard had once ridden. "I would very much like to know who."

The courier's small fire glowed at the camp's edge in the dark, and behind it, faint and patient on the horizon, the Wastes lay silent, as unconcerned as ever with the names of the people who crossed them.
