# Chapter Six — Corwin

The Spire had rules for entering it, and none of them had been written down for a man like Caelen.

He learned the first at the outer gate, where the road ran under a broad arch of white stone into a plaza of pale flagstones and clean gutters. A steward in the House's grey took Seraphina's arm and guided her toward a lacquered carriage that had been waiting, and Halloran turned to Caelen and Rook with a small stiff bow.

"The House would be honored to lodge you," he said. "Naturally, the Lord will not receive you until he has spoken with his daughter alone. In the interval a room has been kept in the east wing."

"A room the House keeps," said a voice behind them, amused and light, "is a room the House can reach into at three in the morning. I would think very carefully before accepting one."

They turned. A young man had come across the plaza toward them, unhurried, hands loose at his sides. He was perhaps Rook's age, slight, dark-haired, dressed in a coat of good grey cloth that had been mended at one cuff by someone who cared. He smiled at Halloran with perfect friendliness and no trace of deference.

"Corwin of Vael," he said. "Third house of the lower ring, second son, no prospects. I assure you I am quite harmless. Steward, I believe you will find a lodging is already arranged for the Archivist's companions, and that they would prefer it. Some of us have been expecting them for a month."

Halloran's face did not change. "The House does not recall a message."

"No, you wouldn't," Corwin said pleasantly. "It came from the Vael side of the ledger. I'm afraid we're rather poor at reaching the Ilvane clerks." He looked at Caelen, and the friendliness dropped away, and for a moment he was only a young man with tired eyes, holding out a hand. "I am not asking you to trust me. I am asking you to let me show you why you shouldn't trust anyone else. It takes about an hour."

---

His house sat in the lower ring, where the Spire's cleanliness gave way to something that resembled a place people lived. The terraces were narrow, the stairs were steep, and laundry hung between the buildings like small flags. It was a tall thin house of four stories with a green door, and inside it smelled of tea and old paper and a woman's cooking.

"My mother left us this and no money," Corwin said, in the front room, tossing his coat over a chair. "My father spent the money on being a second son. It's a small house and I keep it badly. Sit anywhere that isn't a book."

Caelen sat on a stool by the window and looked out at the stacked tiers of the city, the lit windows climbing toward a pale evening sky, and did not relax.

"You wrote to us," Rook said. He hadn't sat at all. "You said a month."

"I did. A letter to the camp, though I gather the Underlow broker felt it was safer to lose it." Corwin shrugged. "I have a sister." He said it lightly. "Her name is Lisbet. She is nineteen. Four years ago a licensed physician fitted her with a Talon Vector, a perfectly legal, perfectly costly conduit, because our family wanted her to be able to defend herself in the Spire's rougher wards." He paused. "It slipped. The second week. Nobody at the House of Vael had the faintest idea what to do. The Synod's physicians told us to wait and see. We waited. We saw."

"She drifted," Caelen said, softly.

"Yes. And for two years the Synod's own physicians told us it was a nervous condition. That it would settle. That we ought not to cause a scandal." Corwin turned from the window, and his hands were shaking very slightly, the way a man's hands shake when he has held them steady for too long. "She's alive. She's better than she was, as of this spring. Do you know why? Because somebody sent me a letter smuggled out of a settlement in the east. About an archivist, and a rite, and a season's grace for someone who wasn't supposed to have one. I wrote back. Nobody answered. So I decided to wait for you here."

"You didn't wait for us," Rook said. "You're standing between us and the House of Ilvane."

"I thought it would be a useful thing to be," Corwin said, and there was a genuine rueful humor in it. "I have been terribly useless for twenty-four years. It seemed a fair time to try."

---

He taught them what he could, that evening and the next morning, in the tall thin house.

He taught Caelen how a Spire drawing room worked: that one never refused the first offer outright, only delayed it into a courtesy; that a request phrased as a question was usually an order; that a man who said *how good of you to come* had already decided how you'd leave. He taught Rook how to hold a cup so nobody thought he'd stolen it. He laughed at Rook's expression and Rook, after a moment, laughed too, reluctantly, and something loosened in the room.

"There's a language under the language," Corwin said. "The words are all polite. Everything that matters is in what you don't say and who you don't look at. The Ilvane family are masters of it. Seraphina's father hasn't raised his voice in forty years. He's never had to."

"What does he want?" Caelen said.

"That's the question, isn't it." Corwin sat down finally, on the arm of a sofa, and rubbed his forehead. "I've been trying to work it out for months. The House Ilvane has spent three years pretending its daughter simply went abroad. That story works so long as she stays abroad. She's a walking hole in it. The rite made her famous, which made the hole visible. Either he closes it, or he makes it a virtue." He let out a breath. "I'd guess the second. Fathers, in my experience, will do a great deal to avoid the first."

Rook glanced at Caelen, and Caelen understood the look. A house of that kind did not welcome a lost daughter home. It restructured its history around her.

"And the hall?" Caelen said. "The petition?"

"Ah." Corwin's mouth tightened. "That I know a great deal more about, and I'm afraid it's worse. Chairman Reth arrived two weeks ago. He has a house in the fourth ring, a very small one, and two clerks, and has spoken to no one of importance. He's been reading. Do you know what he reads? The Custodians' own case files. Every one, going back thirty years, including the ones the Synod believed were sealed." His voice dropped. "He's not lobbying the hall. He's studying it. He's learning which Custodians have doubts and which have certainties, and which of them would prefer not to have to choose. By the time the hearing opens, he'll know what each of them is afraid of."

"And you know this how?"

"My clerk is his clerk's cousin," Corwin said, and shrugged. "The Spire is very small, really, once you learn where to stand."

---

He walked them to the edge of the lower ring at dusk, where a low wall overlooked the plain beyond the city.

The Wastes were not visible from here. Somewhere far to the south they lay silver and scarred under the last light, and the wall looked out only at fields. Caelen stood with his hands on the cool stone and felt, for the first time since the ridge, homesick, in a way he had no name for.

"I keep thinking I should be afraid," he said. "And I am. But it's a different kind. In the Wastes if something wanted me, it came in the dark, and I knew its shape. Here they smile at you. They tell you they're honored."

"That's the Spire," Corwin said. "It doesn't kill you. It notices you, until you can't move without being seen." He hesitated. "I ought to say something honest, since you'll find it out. If my family learns I've taken you in, there'll be consequences. Not violence. Nothing so honest. My allowance, my seat at my father's table. And Lisbet." He looked at his hands. "I've thought about that a great deal, and I've decided I can carry it. I wanted you to know I've decided knowingly."

"You don't owe us that," Caelen said.

"Perhaps not." Corwin smiled, small and tired and entirely sincere. "But I've watched a great many people in this city spend their whole lives never once deciding anything knowingly. It seemed a shame to waste the opportunity."

Behind them, in the dusk, the great tower rose into the cloud, pale and bright and unhurt, and far above, faint as a held breath, a single bell began to ring the hour.
