# Chapter Four — What the Network Is Worth

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Malakar. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**MALAKAR**

He had counted them so often that the number had stopped being a figure and become a kind of weather. Thirteen names. Thirteen people scattered through the garrisons of the eastern territories, each one holding a small refusal, misfiling an order, delaying a rotation, treating a case quietly that the doctrine required be reported. Thirteen was a dozen and one, and a dozen and one was not an army. Malakar knew precisely what it was, because he had spent eleven years learning to read exactly how much weight a piece of paper could bear.

He set the count down on the table in the back room of the surgeon's house at Kesh Hollow, beside a candle and a plate of cold mutton nobody had touched, and looked at the faces around it.

The surgeon, whose name was Halvane, sat with his hands folded, tired-eyed and patient, a man who had spent thirty years learning not to be noticed. Teague, the line Warden from the eastern crossing, sat with his back to the wall and his eyes on the door, which he had done since the day he'd let three travelers through on a dark road and hadn't been able to stop watching doors since. Orell sat at the table's foot. Three others had come, sworn to secrecy and shaking with the effort of being here at all.

"The petition," Malakar said, "contains a provision for hearings. The hall will take testimony on the conduct of the Warden order in cases of unlicensed Resonance. Reth's petition wants that testimony for its own reasons. It builds the case that the Wardens have failed, and therefore that a central register must replace them. But the provision is real. A witness who stands in that hall speaks on the record, in front of the Custodians, and cannot afterward be quietly made to have said something else."

"You want us to testify," Teague said.

"I want to know whether any of you can," Malakar said. "That is different. I will not ask a man to put his neck in a noose because I have decided the noose is worth the view."

---

Halvane broke the silence first, dry, gentle. "You realize what you are asking is not the same as what you were asking a year ago. A year ago you asked us to be small. To lose a form, delay a patrol. Nobody was ever going to arrest a clerk for a form misplaced in the right month. Testifying is a name. A face. A garrison commander who has read my file for thirty years will read it again, differently."

"Yes," Malakar said.

"And the clerk," said Teague. "Petra. You said she was in the Kesh Hollow cells."

The room went very still.

Malakar had not planned to say it in that order. He had carried it for four months, the way he carried every fact he'd chosen not to spend, and it came out of his mouth now in the exact flat voice he'd once used to read a sentence into a record. "Petra's cousin's husband is a turnkey at the tribunal cells. She is being held there on charge of falsifying garrison orders. The tribunal has been deferring her case for sixteen months, and I do not believe that is mercy. It is the order's habit with a problem it does not want to name. If she is a witness in that hall, her case becomes public. If her case becomes public, the tribunal must either hear it or explain why it does not."

"Or she disappears," Orell said quietly. "And a woman who was a clerk becomes a woman who was never there."

"Yes," Malakar said again. He made himself look at each of them. "That is the entire difficulty. I cannot tell you which it will be. I have spent a year learning to read the order's instincts, and I have found that in a case like this the order has two, and they do not agree with each other. It can protect itself by hiding a thing, or it can protect itself by burying the person who knows it. Publicity can save her. Publicity can also be the last thing that hands her to the second instinct."

---

They argued for three hours, and Malakar let them, because he had learned that a conscience settled by an argument was sturdier than one settled by an order. Two of the newest, a young quartermaster and an aging armorer, said plainly they could not, and Malakar thanked them for saying it, because a man who says no honestly is worth ten who say yes and mean it only until morning. Halvane said he would think, which from Halvane was a great deal. Teague said nothing for a long time, and then said that he had let three travelers through on a dark road for reasons he had never been able to explain to his own captain, and that he was tired of not explaining it.

When the room had emptied Malakar stayed at the table with Orell and the candle.

"You've made a decision," Orell said.

"I made it in a lodging room, with the file," Malakar said. "The night I first read Petra's name. I have only been arranging the paperwork ever since." He rubbed his eyes. "It is a peculiar thing, Orell. I spent eleven years perfecting the art of having no fingerprints. I documented every force parameter so that no tribunal could say I had acted beyond doctrine. And the first useful thing I have done since leaving the order is the exact opposite. I am going to put my own name on a record and say: *I did this, and I believed it, and I was wrong, and here is the proof.*"

"That's not the opposite," Orell said. "That's the same skill. You just finally aimed it at the right document."

Malakar almost smiled, and noticed how easily it came. A year among people who said hard things plainly had done that. He was not sure he would have recognized himself from the office at Kesh Hollow.

"There is one more thing," he said. "The hall will call for the order's own account of Greyfen. Someone will have to answer for it who was there in a position to know. I have made inquiries. The officer who signed the containment report is a man named Roshe, and he has since been promoted." He let that sit. "I should like you to be there when I say his name."

"I'll be there," Orell said. "I think I've been waiting a year to be somewhere I can say his."

---

He walked back to his lodging late, through streets he knew by heart and did not belong to. A patrol crossed the square ahead, grey and brass, and he stopped in a doorway and let it pass, and felt, watching the disciplined unhurried tread, the old lurch: the part of him that still leaned toward that formation the way a plant leans toward a window it grew up beside.

He did not resent it. He had learned to let it be there, as he had let the stalled clause finally finish itself. It was not loyalty. It was only the shape of a life he had spent eleven years fitting himself to, and it would take longer than a year to unlearn the shape of a room.

In his lodging he lit a lamp and drew a sheet toward him and began, in his exact and careful hand, a document he had never written before. Not a report. Not a recovery order. A statement of testimony.

*I am Malakar, formerly Inquisitor-Commander of the Warden order, Resonance Enforcement, Eastern Territories. I speak in my own name.*

He read the sentence twice. It contained no clause. It contained no armor. It was the first sentence in his adult life that could not be defended by any doctrine, and he found, looking at it, that the pen did not hesitate at all.
