# Chapter Twelve — The Glass Wastes, Still

Winter came early that year, the first true cold Caelen had felt settle over the camp since Fenn's own season had first begun its slow narrowing, and he found himself, on the solstice evening, climbing the rise above the camp alone, the way he had a dozen times since a mountain first taught him what it meant to stand at a threshold and choose to keep walking regardless of what waited past it.

The others found him there, one by one, the way they always did — Rook first, settling in without a word; then Seraphina, the tethering rite's fragments now carried in a satchel considerably fuller than the one she'd first unfolded at a stranger's fire; then Malakar and Orell together, still an unlikely pairing, still somehow exactly right; and last, slowest, leaning on a stick he'd only started needing this season, Fenn, breath fogging thin in the cold, alive, present, and entirely unashamed of how much effort the climbing had cost him.

"Wasn't going to miss the view," Fenn said, easing himself down onto the same flat stone where Varrick had once sat, "on account of a little cold and a body that's running short on complaints worth making about it."

---

They stood together at the ridge's edge, looking out over the Glass Wastes the way Caelen had looked out over them at the end of one book already, and the end of another act before that — the same scarred silver plate, the same rusted rig-bones standing like the ribs of something enormous that had died on its feet and never fallen the rest of the way down, the same patient hiss of ash drifting over a wound that had never once, in all the years any of them had known this ground, fully closed.

It had not healed. Caelen understood that now with a clarity two books' worth of hard-won cost had finally, fully settled into him. It was not going to heal, not in his lifetime, not perhaps in any lifetime this particular territory had left to spend. The Guild would come back, wearing some new director's careful patience. The Synod would keep arguing with itself, slow and internal, tipping by degrees rather than all at once. Kell's ledger would find some new angle. Voss's replacement would inherit an argument three generations old and no closer to resolved than it had ever been.

And the rite would keep spreading anyway, slow and careful and entirely unlicensed, because some mercies were worth the danger regardless of how patiently the danger waited.

"No clean victory," Rook said, echoing words nine years old now, worn smooth with repetition the way the small stone of a name gets worn smooth from handling.

"No," Caelen agreed. "Never was. I don't think there's a version of this life that was ever going to end that way, and I've stopped waiting on one." He looked around at the gathered family — smaller by one irreplaceable weight, differently shaped, and entirely, stubbornly still standing. "I think that's actually the whole of what we've built, these two books' worth of hard cost. Not a victory. Just a way of carrying the unfinished thing together, instead of alone."

---

Fenn was quiet a long while, watching the horizon with the particular stillness of a man doing an arithmetic he'd long since made his peace with.

"I keep thinking about what happens after," he said, finally. "Not to me — I've settled that account plainly enough, and I don't need any of you fussing over the settling of it further than you already have. I mean after all of it. After Kell's ledger and Voss's doctrine and whatever comes next wearing some new face neither of us has met yet. Does it ever actually stop? Or is this just what a life looks like, once you've decided the small shape of it is worth choosing over and over?"

"I don't know," Caelen said, honest, because honesty had cost him less than the alternative for as long as he could remember choosing between them. "I used to think there'd be an ending to it. A door that finally stayed shut. A debt that finally closed clean." He touched the letter, still folded, still carried close against his chest months after the words themselves had gone soft with handling. "I don't think that's how it actually works anymore. I think it's just choosing, over and over, for as long as there's breath left to spend choosing with. Varrick taught me that, the whole time, without either of us ever quite saying it plain until the very end."

He looked out at the Wastes one final time, scarred and silver and entirely, stubbornly unhealed, the same wound in the land that had first taught a starving boy what it meant to stand at a threshold and choose the harder ground, and understood, standing there with the whole found family gathered close around him, that whatever came next — Kell's return, Voss's replacement, some new door neither of them had found yet, some new cost none of them could see coming — they would meet it the same way they had met everything else this life had ever handed them.

Together. Badly, at first. For exactly as long as there was still time left to carry it in.

"All right," he said, and felt Rook's shoulder settle against his own, and Seraphina's quiet resolve beside him, and Malakar and Orell's hard-won partnership a few careful steps behind, and Fenn's narrowing, unashamed presence steady on the stone where an old man had once taught all of them what it actually meant to spend a life well. "Then let's keep walking."

He did not know what waited past this particular horizon. He had stopped needing to know, somewhere across two books' worth of hard, costly choosing, because the not-knowing had never once, in the whole of this life, been the thing that actually mattered.

What mattered was who was still standing beside him when he stepped down off the ridge into whatever came next.
