# Chapter Ten — The Debt Renewed

They walked south for six days, and Caelen understood, somewhere in the third, that the grief had stopped arriving in waves the way it had at the annex and had simply, quietly, moved in — a permanent resident now, the way the small warm stone of his own name had once been, except this stone offered no comfort at all, only weight.

Fenn met them at the camp's western marker, thinner than Caelen remembered, the particular permanent hitch in his shoulders settled deeper than a single season should have earned, and Caelen understood, seeing him, that whatever the letter had spared them of the full account, Fenn had carried the whole terrible weight of it alone for six days, waiting.

"He asked me to tell it to you plain," Fenn said, before Caelen had found any words of his own. "Not soften it. Said a man who'd spent his whole life teaching honesty costing less than the alternative would rather his own dying get the same treatment."

---

They sat together that evening around the fire that had, across two books and nine hard years, become the one constant thread running through every version of found family Caelen had ever built, and Fenn told it the way Varrick would have wanted it told — plainly, without softening, and without pretending the hard parts weren't hard.

"Four nights before your letter reached you, he woke me in the small hours, breathing wrong in a way I'd learned, by then, to recognize as different from his usual wrong." Fenn's voice held steady, though his hands, working a length of brass wire he didn't seem to notice he was still holding, betrayed the cost of the telling. "Didn't want the surgeon fetched. Said plainly there wasn't a fetching left that would change the arithmetic, and he'd rather spend his last clear hours talking than being poked at by someone with no real answer to offer him regardless."

"What did he talk about," Rook asked, wrecked, needing the answer the way a person needs water.

"You, mostly. All of you." Fenn's hands finally went still. "Told me about a boy in a tree who wouldn't come down for kindness but came down for honest arithmetic instead. Told me about a fast-talking kid who shared his only decent loaf of bread with a stranger nobody else in that whole settlement would've fed. Told me, near the end, about a debt he said he'd started calling arithmetic because the truth of it frightened him considerably more than any ledger ever could." Fenn looked at Caelen directly. "Said he wanted you to know, plainly, that the frightening was worth it. Every single year of it. Said if that's the last true thing you remember him saying, he'd count the whole dying as a fair trade."

---

They buried him the following morning, on the rise above the camp where he'd once stood with Caelen and Rook and Seraphina, watching the Wastes stretch out scarred and silver and entirely, stubbornly alive despite everything mined out of it — the same rise, Caelen understood, where a starving boy had once been told, plainly, that this was where whatever came next would have to happen.

There was no formal rite. Varrick had never once, in nine years, shown patience for ceremony he hadn't personally judged worth the trouble, and Caelen understood that honoring him meant honoring that particular stubbornness above every other consideration.

"I don't do charity," Rook said, into the quiet, his voice cracking on the old familiar words, "and I don't do speeches, so I'll just say the true thing plain, the way he taught all of us to. You found a starving kid in a tree and told him it was arithmetic. Spent nine years proving that was the biggest lie you ever told, and never once admitted it until you were dying, because admitting it earlier might've meant somebody had to be brave about loving you back properly instead of sideways." He wiped his face, unashamed. "We loved you back anyway, old man. Sideways and plain both. I hope that arithmetic finally comes out even, wherever you've gone to do the reckoning."

Seraphina spoke next, quiet, the careful court architecture she still sometimes reached for entirely absent. "You told me, once, that wanting things selfishly and wanting them true weren't as separate as doctrine liked to pretend. I've carried that further than you'll ever know now, and I intend to keep carrying it, in your name, for every family this rite still manages to reach."

Malakar, precise even in grief, offered simply: "You extended a Warden the same terms you once extended a strata child. Careful. Provisional. Entirely, unexpectedly generous underneath the caution. I don't believe I ever properly thanked you for that particular kindness, and I regret, now, that I waited this long to say it."

Orell, who had known him only in the telling, said nothing at all, only stood with his head bowed, a stranger's grief offered plainly on behalf of people he'd chosen to become family to at considerable cost to himself.

---

Caelen spoke last, the letter still folded and carried close against his chest, and found the words arrived the way the hardest true things always had in this life — slow, and plain, and entirely without armor.

"You told me, at a fire once, that some debts don't close just because the ledger's owner walked away from the table." He looked out at the Glass Wastes, scarred and silver and unhealed, the same wound in the land that had first taught him what it meant to choose ground nobody wanted badly enough to watch too closely. "I don't think this one closes either. I think I'm going to carry it the rest of my life, the way I've carried everything else you ever handed me — the terms, the arithmetic, the sideways kindness dressed up as practicality. I think that's what you actually meant, that last night, telling Fenn the frightening was worth it. Not that the debt gets paid off. Just that it's worth carrying, however heavy it gets, because of who you get to carry it with."

He knelt, and pressed his palm flat against the fresh-turned earth, the way he'd once pressed it flat against an ancient sealed door, and felt no answering pulse this time, no vast buried certainty — only ordinary ground, ordinary loss, and the whole unglamorous, entirely worthwhile weight of a debt renewed rather than closed.

"Rest easy," he said, quiet, to a man who had never once, in nine years, made resting look easy for himself. "We've got it from here. However badly, however long it takes. Together, the whole way through."

The found family stood together on the rise a long while afterward, smaller now by one irreplaceable weight and differently shaped for the carrying of it, watching the Wastes stretch out scarred and silver toward a horizon that offered, as it always had, no clean answers at all — only the plain, stubborn, entirely worthwhile fact of still being there, together, to keep walking toward whatever came next.
