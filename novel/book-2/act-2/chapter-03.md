# Chapter Three — Orell's Choice

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Malakar. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**MALAKAR**

He reached Orell the way he'd once reached every difficult truth in eleven years of service — carefully, on paper first, because paper rarely lied to him the way frightened men did, and a supply requisition slipped through the annex's own provisioning chain, addressed to a name only one man serving there would recognize, was as close to paper as this particular truth was going to allow.

Orell found him before dawn, at the drainage cut a half-mile out where Malakar had watched Seraphina walked toward a sealed seam the day before, and he came alone, the same as the ruin all those months ago, though something in his bearing now carried considerably less of that meeting's careful uncertainty.

"You read my requisition," Malakar said.

"I read considerably more than that," Orell said, low, crouching beside him in the grey pre-dawn. "I've spent three weeks inside those walls, Malakar, provisioning an operation I was told was routine reliquary maintenance and watching it become, day by day, something I don't have a doctrine left that adequately excuses my continued participation in."

---

"Tell me what you've seen," Malakar said, and understood, asking it, that this was the whole of what the last eight months had been building toward — not a plan, not yet, but the plain accounting a man owed another man before either of them decided how much trust the moment could actually bear.

"The archivist," Orell said. "Your Seraphina. They've had her at the seam three times now, each attempt shorter than the last because each one costs her considerably more than the working itself accounts for. Custodian Voss doesn't call it torture. I don't have a gentler word for watching someone forced back to a working that leaves them shaking and half-conscious, on a schedule set by someone else's deadline rather than their own body's capacity to survive it." His jaw tightened, and Malakar recognized, watching him, the particular strain of a man cataloguing his own complicity in real time. "I've delayed what I could. Misfiled a provisioning order here, slowed a guard rotation there — small things, the same small things your clerk at Long Reach was doing before she got caught at it. I don't know how much longer I can keep doing it without drawing the same attention she did."

"And the three names I gave you," Malakar said, quiet. "The clerk. The line Warden. The surgeon."

"The clerk's already lost to whatever's happening to her now, and I couldn't protect her from that, however much I tried." Orell's voice carried real grief in it, plain and unhidden. "The line Warden I've kept working, carefully, at the eastern crossing — he's the one who let you through two nights back, though I imagine you'd already worked that out for yourselves. The surgeon I've used sparingly, only for what I couldn't risk documenting through official channels." He looked at Malakar directly. "I carried your names forward, Inquisitor. Not to a superior. To exactly the place you asked me to carry them — my own conscience, one careful test at a time, until I ran out of ways to keep calling what I was watching inside those walls anything but what it actually is."

---

"Then I need to ask you for something considerably larger than three careful names," Malakar said, and laid the whole shape of it out plainly — the second seal, the new-moon deadline, Seraphina's forced readings, the rescue three exhausted travelers had no real chance of managing against twelve disciplined guards without help from inside the walls.

Orell was quiet a long while, the grey light strengthening slowly around them, and Malakar understood, watching him weigh it, that this was the exact moment eleven years of service and eight months of careful, provisional sympathy finally arrived at the place they could no longer avoid choosing between.

"If I help you," Orell said, finally, slow, "there's no careful misfiling that survives it. No delayed rotation, no requisition quietly lost in transit. I open a gate, or I falsify a schedule, or I simply fail to report what I've clearly seen — any of it, and I'm exactly what you became eight months ago. Renounced. Hunted. Everything I've built in eleven years, gone in a single night's choosing."

"Yes," Malakar said, because he had promised himself, walking back from that first ruin, never to make this particular choice sound smaller to another man than it had actually cost him to make it himself.

"I keep thinking about the boy," Orell said, quiet, unexpected. "The strata child. I read his file the same night you did, all those years ago, and I remember thinking, clearly, that the order had no business hunting a nine-year-old for surviving something none of us understood. I told myself, at the time, that thinking it was enough. That doing my job carefully, precisely, was its own kind of decency, even inside doctrine that frightened me." He looked north, toward the annex's low grey silhouette just beginning to show against the strengthening light. "I don't believe that anymore. I haven't believed it, if I'm honest with myself, since Greyfen. I think I've simply been waiting, these three weeks, for someone to finally ask me to prove it."

---

"There's a supply gate on the annex's eastern face," Orell said, and Malakar understood, hearing the shift in his voice, that the choosing had already happened, somewhere in the saying of the last few sentences, and everything now was simply the shape of acting on it. "Guard rotation thins there between the second and third watch, because command's always assumed the drainage cut makes a direct approach too exposed to bother reinforcing properly. I can be posted to that gate tomorrow night, if I request it plainly enough that nobody thinks to question the asking. I can't open it wide, and I can't guarantee I'll be able to explain my own absence afterward without considerable cost. But I can make sure it's unlatched, and unwatched, for exactly as long as three careful people need it to be."

"That's considerably more than I came here asking for," Malakar said.

"I know." Orell rose, the decision settling over him visibly, some old weight lifting even as a new one, heavier, took its place. "I'd ask you one thing in return, Inquisitor, since I imagine it's the last favor I'll have standing enough to ask before my own name joins yours on whatever ledger tracks men like us now." He held Malakar's gaze, steady. "When this is over, whatever's left of it — I'd like to know the boy made it. Whatever else this costs any of us. I think I'd like to carry that forward, at least, as proof the choosing was worth what it took."

"I'll see to it personally," Malakar said, and meant it entirely, and watched Orell withdraw the same unhurried way he always had, though the tread carried, this time, the particular lighter weight of a man who had finally, after three long weeks of careful small delays, chosen the whole of what he actually believed rather than the safest fraction of it.

Malakar sat alone at the drainage cut a while longer, watching the sun finally clear the horizon over ground that had cost, by his own careful count, considerably more than either of them had budgeted for when a ruined weigh-station first taught them both what this particular kind of choosing actually required. Tomorrow night. The second and third watch. A gate left unlatched by a man who had finally stopped waiting to be asked.

It would have to be enough.
