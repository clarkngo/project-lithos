# Chapter Six — What Fenn Chooses

*A note on point of view: this chapter departs from Caelen's perspective, following Fenn — a new controlled exception, matching the discipline established for Malakar, Seraphina, and Custodian Voss elsewhere in this book. See [style-guide.md §4](../../style-guide.md).*

---

**FENN**

The season Seraphina had bought him was down to weeks now, not months, and Fenn had learned, in the quiet stretch since the others rode north, to measure the narrowing the same careful way he'd once measured a housing's brass — by feel, by the particular weight of his own hands, by how long he could hold a fitting steady before the tremor started.

He did not tell Varrick how close the weeks had gotten. He had learned that particular silence from the old man himself, watching him fold a bloodied rag away three separate times since the others left, each time insisting, with the same dry stubbornness, that it was nothing worth anyone's worry.

"You're doing the thing I do," Varrick said, on the ninth evening, not looking up from the fire he could no longer easily crouch to tend himself. "Folding something away quiet because you've decided the folding costs less than the telling."

"Learned from the best," Fenn said, and found, saying it, that some of the old brass-worker's wry humor had survived even this — a small mercy, offered sideways, the way every true thing in this life seemed to arrive.

"Learned wrong, then," Varrick said, gentle. "I've had cause to regret my own folding, lately. Wouldn't wish the habit on a man who's got even less time to waste on it than I do."

---

The strangers came on the twelfth night, three of them, moving with a discipline Fenn recognized immediately as considerably more dangerous than Corin's clumsy testing had been weeks before — no fumbled cover story this time, no attempt to pass as desperate family. They came for the camp itself, torches doused, blades already drawn, and Fenn understood, waking to the particular wrong silence of a night gone entirely too still, that whoever had sent them had stopped caring whether their purpose stayed hidden.

"Guild," one of them said, low, to the others, spotting the camp's crate-tin structures. "Director's orders. The old man's the leverage. Take him alive if it's clean, don't risk the operation if it isn't."

Fenn understood, hearing it, exactly what kind of night this was about to become.

He had no anchor. No Caelen holding a banked coal steady across from him, no Seraphina's murmured working smoothing the edges of whatever came next. He had only himself, and a body that had spent the last twelve days narrowing toward its own private ending, and an old man asleep twenty strides distant who had spent nine years teaching a starving boy how to be worth the trouble of protecting.

Fenn had never once, in his whole ruined season, chosen to Shift on purpose. Every flare before now had happened to him, arrived and passed like weather he had no say in. He understood, standing at the edge of the camp's outer marker with three armed strangers closing on the only home he had left, that tonight was going to be different, and that the difference was going to cost him considerably more than any of the seasons he had left to spend.

He let it in on purpose.

---

The Shift arrived the way Caelen had once described his own worst ones — not power, not a gift, but a tearing, his skin drawing wrong-tight across shoulders that had never been fitted for this size, his hands lengthening with a grinding pressure that stole every sound but a low wet roar from his own straining throat. He felt the old patient hunger surge up to meet the invitation eagerly, delighted, the way it had always been delighted whenever he'd let it close the distance even a little, and he understood, mid-tearing, that there was no small warm stone of his own name steady enough in him yet to promise he'd find his own way back from this one.

He fought anyway. Not the Shift — he had chosen that, deliberately, the one true choosing his whole ruined season had ever actually offered him. He fought to stay *himself* inside it, the way Caelen had once described holding a coal banked and steady even at its largest measure, and found, to his own considerable surprise, that a season of watching someone else do exactly that had taught him more than he'd realized he was learning.

He put the first stranger down before the man's blade cleared its sheath. The second got a blow in, a hot line of pain across Fenn's ribs that some distant, still-present part of him catalogued and set aside for later, because later was a thing worth surviving toward. The third turned to run at the sight of what the camp's supposedly easy leverage had actually become, and some vast, patient, hungry part of Fenn wanted, badly, to chase him down and finish the arithmetic properly.

He didn't. He stood, instead, shaking, enormous, wrong-shaped, and watched the man flee into the dark, and understood that letting him go was its own kind of choosing, the exact same choosing Caelen had once described making at a burning weigh-station nine years before either of them had known the other existed.

---

The cold arrived the way it always did, all at once, a door thrown open onto winter, and Fenn came back to himself on his knees in the scarred dirt, smaller again, wrong in the opposite direction, shaking so hard his own teeth cracked together.

Varrick was there before the shaking had fully passed, easing himself down with visible effort, wrapping what blankets the camp still had around Fenn's spent shoulders with hands that trembled from more than just the cold night air.

"You shouldn't have," Varrick said, and Fenn heard, underneath the words, something that was not quite scolding at all. "Whatever season you had left, boy, you didn't have room to spend it on an old man who's already halfway through paying his own debt regardless."

"Wasn't your debt I was protecting," Fenn managed, wrecked, honest. "Wasn't just arithmetic either, before you go telling me it was foolish arithmetic same as you always do about your own choices. I chose it. The way Caelen taught me choosing actually works — not knowing in advance if you can hold, just finding out, badly, in the moment it's asked of you." He looked at Varrick, and found, even spent, even shaking, that something in him had settled, finally, into a shape he recognized as his own. "I've got fewer weeks now than I did this morning. I know that. I did the arithmetic on the way into it, same as I did with mother's medicine, and this time it came out considerably more even than that one ever did."

Varrick was quiet a long while, watching him with an expression Fenn had never seen on the old man's face before — not pity, not even pride exactly, but something closer to recognition, one debt-payer seeing another finally understand the particular terrible math of it.

"Then I owe you an apology," Varrick said, finally, "for ever telling Caelen, or telling myself, that this kind of spending was a thing to be avoided at all costs. Watching you just now, I don't think it is. I think it's just the cost of loving people enough to stand where the worst of it lands, same as I've watched every single one of you do for each other since a mountain first taught a boy what that kind of standing actually costs."

They sat together at the camp's ruined edge as the first grey light began finding its way over the horizon, an old man spending down his own last weeks and a young one who had just, deliberately, spent a considerable portion of his, and neither of them said anything further, because some things, both of them had learned now, the hard way, were not meant to be answered. They were only meant to be carried, together, for exactly as long as there was time left to carry them in.
