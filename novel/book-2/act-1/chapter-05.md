# Chapter Five — Varrick's Ledger

Caelen noticed the blood before Varrick managed to hide the rag it had come from, and understood, in the single suspended second before Varrick's dry composure reasserted itself, that this was not new — only newly witnessed.

"It's nothing," Varrick said, folding the rag away with the same unhurried economy he did everything with now, though his breath, Caelen noticed, had gone thin and careful in a way that cost him something to keep steady. "Cold morning air catching an old man's chest wrong. Happens to every scavenger who lives long enough to complain about it."

"It's not nothing." Caelen did not raise his voice. He had learned, across nine years, that Varrick's careful deflections answered gentleness considerably better than confrontation ever did. "How long."

Varrick was quiet a long moment, weighing, Caelen understood, not whether to lie but how much of the truth actually needed spending on a boy — a man, nine years a man now — who had already spent so much of his own life learning to carry hard things honestly.

"Since the winter," he said, finally. "Started as an ache in the cold. Been a considerably more interesting conversation with my own lungs since the thaw." He looked at Caelen, and something in his dry composure, just for a moment, let the true weight of it show. "The Vault wound never healed the way we let ourselves hope, boy. I don't imagine it's going to start now."

---

Seraphina examined him that evening in the low structure that had become, without any of them formally deciding it, the camp's version of an infirmary, her hands moving over Varrick's ribs with the same careful precision she brought to the rite's fragile fragments, and Caelen watched her face for the verdict before she offered it plainly.

"It's not Drift," she said, at last, quiet, packing her instruments away with hands that had gone, Caelen noticed, less than perfectly steady. "I want that said first, because I know all three of us have spent the last eight months learning to hope every hard thing has a rite waiting somewhere to answer it. This isn't that kind of wound. The strut that struck him cracked something deeper than the rib itself, something that never set properly, and it's been working its slow damage ever since. I don't have a fragment for this. Nobody's ancient methodology sealed away an answer for an old man's lung finally giving up the fight it's been losing quietly for the better part of a year."

"Then there's nothing," Rook said, from the doorway, his voice gone flat in a way Caelen recognized immediately, with a lurch, as the particular flatness of fear arriving in a register none of them had built any defenses against.

"There's time," Seraphina said, careful. "Managed carefully — rest, warmth, considerably less walking than any of you are inclined to let him do — there's time. I won't pretend to know how much. I've learned, this past season, not to promise numbers I can't stand behind."

Rook did not say anything further. He simply turned and walked out into the grey evening, and Caelen understood, watching him go, that this was not the same walking-away that had once, nine years gone, carried a frightened boy four careful feet from a fire he loved. This was a different kind of running — toward nothing, away from nothing, only the particular aimless motion of a person whose body needed to do something with a fear that had no shape he could fight.

---

Caelen found him an hour later at the camp's western marker, the same spot where they'd stood together the morning Fenn's message first arrived, staring out at a horizon that offered, as it always did, no answers at all.

"I keep waiting for it to be something I can hit," Rook said, without turning, his voice wrecked in a way that had nothing to do with any Shift or rite. "A Warden line. A Guild survey crew. Even the drift, back when I was still frightened enough of you to run from it — at least that had a shape. Something a knife or a choosing or a stubborn refusal to leave could actually push back against." He was quiet a moment, and when he spoke again his fast rhythm had gone entirely, uncharacteristically still. "This is just going to keep happening. Slow. Quiet. No cordon to stand against, no Guild ledger to argue with. Just time, doing the one thing none of us have ever once managed to talk our way out of."

"I know," Caelen said, because he did, because the same cold understanding had settled into him the moment he'd seen the rag folded too quickly away. "I don't have anything to fix this with, Rook. I've spent nine years learning to spend myself against exactly the kind of danger that has an edge to push back against. This isn't that. I can't Shift my way into buying him more time. I can't choose the small shape of anything here. There's no drift to hold back, no rite to anchor." He stood with that admission a long moment, letting it be as plain and unarmored as everything else this year had asked him to be. "All I've got is the same thing you've got. Time spent close, instead of time spent afraid to be close. That's the whole of what either of us can actually offer him."

Rook was quiet a long while, and when he finally spoke again, something in his voice had settled, not healed, but settled, the way grief settles when it finally stops looking for a fight it can win. "Nine years ago I told you I didn't know how long it'd take before there was only the one part of me left — the part that stays instead of the part that runs." He looked at Caelen directly, eyes wet in the failing light. "I already know the answer this time. There's no waiting on it. I'm staying. However long this takes, however it ends. I just — " His voice caught, plain, unguarded. "I just don't know how to stay through something I can't do anything about."

"Neither do I," Caelen said. "I don't think that's a thing either of us gets to know in advance. I think it's just a thing we find out, the same way we've found out everything else — by doing it, badly at first, together."

---

He found Varrick alone by the fire later that night, the old man's breath still careful and thin in the cold, watching the flames with an expression Caelen had rarely seen on him — not fear, not quite, but something close to it, an old man doing an accounting he'd clearly been running privately for considerably longer than the rag had made visible to the rest of them.

"You've been carrying this alone," Caelen said, sitting beside him.

"Man doesn't do charity, and he doesn't do burdening people either, if he can help it," Varrick said, dry, though the dryness had gone thin at its edges, the way it always did now when something true was pressing close behind it. He was quiet a while, watching the fire. "I found a starving boy in a tree nine years ago, Caelen, and told myself plainly I wasn't doing it out of kindness. Told you the same, in fact, first night at the shelter — terms, not charity, a debt to be worked out as we went." He looked at Caelen, and whatever careful armor had held his voice steady all these years finally, entirely, set itself down. "I want you to know, before this particular debt comes fully due, that I was lying to both of us the whole while. Might have started as arithmetic. Didn't stay that way past the first winter, and I think some stubborn, unshowy part of me has known it every day since."

"I know," Caelen said, and found his own voice unsteady in a way he did not try to hide. "You've never once needed to say it plainly for me to know it."

"Good." Varrick's hand found his shoulder, the same gesture Rook had once offered him at the edge of losing himself entirely, steady despite everything. "Then here's the last thing I'll ask of you, and I'll only ask it the once, because I've never been a man who repeats himself for effect. Whatever this costs, however long it takes — you don't spend yourself foolish trying to fix a thing that was never yours to fix. You've done that enough times already for people this life had no business asking it of you. Let this one just be what it is. Let me be an old man who got considerably more good years out of a bargain with a starving boy than either of us had any right to expect, and let that be enough, when the ledger finally closes, without you carrying guilt on top of the grief."

Caelen sat with him a long while by the fire, saying nothing further, because some things, he had learned across nine hard years of learning it, were not meant to be answered. They were only meant to be carried, together, for exactly as long as there was still time left to carry them in.
