# Chapter Six — Fenn

Fenn came back on the fortieth day, which Caelen understood, watching him make the last stretch of the scarred track under his own power, was considerably sooner than the season Seraphina had bought him should have run out.

He was thinner than he'd been the first time, and the permanent hitch in his shoulders had settled deeper, but he was carrying something — a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, held with the particular careful pride of a man who had not come only to take.

"Brought something," Fenn said, before Caelen could ask why he'd returned early, setting the bundle down and unwrapping it to reveal a set of small, well-made tools, worn from use but honestly kept. "Was a housing-fitter, before. Not a Resonant myself by trade — just good hands for brass work, which is how I ended up doing unlicensed fitting for men who couldn't afford the Guild's proper rates. Figured a camp doing what this one does could use somebody who knows his way around a conduit's actual mechanics, even a broken one that never held a core right to begin with." He looked at Caelen, something wry surfacing through the exhaustion. "Didn't come back early to take another season off your archivist's back, if that's what you're bracing for. Came back because I didn't have anywhere better to spend the season I've already got."

---

He stayed a week, and Caelen found, working alongside him at the small forge Rook had rigged from salvage near the camp's edge, that the shape of Fenn's story arrived the way most true things did in this life — sideways, in pieces, offered only when the offering cost less than the silence would have.

He had grown up in a fringe settlement not unlike Hollow Cut had once been, the youngest of four, and had learned brass work at his father's knee the same unhurried way Rook had once learned a knife's honest use from Ansel. When the Guild's licensing fees for a proper conduit fitting outpaced what an honest brass-worker's trade could cover, and his mother's lungs had gone bad the way lungs went bad in settlements built too close to old extraction burns, Fenn had taken work nobody licensed would touch — hauling salvage through poisoned ground for a subcontractor who paid well precisely because the ground was poisoned, using an unlicensed housing fitted by his own careful hands, because a fitter who couldn't trust his own work had no business calling himself one.

"Wasn't reckless," he said, quiet, turning a small brass fitting over in scarred fingers. "Wasn't chasing a feeling, the way I hear some Spire sons do it. Was just arithmetic. Mother needed medicine the Guild's own extraction had helped make necessary in the first place. I had hands that could fit a housing and a body willing to spend itself. Didn't seem like much of a choice at the time." He was quiet a moment. "Didn't feel like one, looking back, either. Just felt like the only sum that came out even."

"How many Shifts," Caelen asked, careful, the way he'd learned to ask hard questions without demanding they be answered.

"Lost count somewhere past the second year." Fenn's hands had gone still on the fitting. "Mother passed anyway, in the end. Medicine bought her eight extra months, maybe. I keep doing the arithmetic different ways, trying to make the eight months worth what it cost. Some days it comes out even. Most days it doesn't."

---

The flare came on the fourth day, without warning, while they were working the forge together in the late afternoon heat.

Caelen felt it before he saw it — the particular wrongness in the air that he recognized, with a lurch of old instinct, from every dangerous moment his own body had ever taught him to read first through sound and smell and the hair rising at the back of his neck. Fenn's breath had gone low and wet, his shoulders drawing up past their permanent hitch into something considerably more wrong-shaped, his eyes fixing on nothing at all with the particular unblinking stillness Caelen remembered, sick, from his own worst nights.

"Fenn," he said, low, already moving, already letting the coal behind his sternum light — not for strength this time, but for the same banked, deliberate stillness he'd held for Seraphina's rite. "Fenn, look at me. Not through me. At me."

Fenn's eyes did not find him immediately. Something older and more patient than fear had begun surfacing behind them, the same vast, wordless hunger Caelen recognized from the Vault's chamber, from his own worst edges at the Sinking Fields — except here, in Fenn, it wore a face Caelen had spent a week learning to care about, hands that had spent that same week fitting brass with careful, honest pride, and the sight of the hunger rising up through all of that, threatening to unmake it, frightened Caelen considerably more than anything the vast lonely chamber had ever offered him directly.

He did not have Seraphina's fragments. He did not have the full working, the murmured guidance, the careful ritual architecture she'd built across months of study. He had only what nine years had taught his own body, and he spent it now the way he'd once spent it holding a wheel free or a cart steady — banked, deliberate, a fixed point offered without ceremony.

"Stay with me," he said, quiet, steady, holding his own coal lit and still the way he'd have held it for any patient in Seraphina's stone circle. "Not the arithmetic. Not the eight months, not the medicine, not any of it. Just this. Just the forge, and the brass in your hands, and me, standing here, not going anywhere."

It took long minutes — longer, Caelen understood distantly, than he had any right to expect an improvised anchor to hold against a flare this severe — but slowly, the wrongness in Fenn's shoulders eased, his breath finding its ordinary register again, his eyes clearing, focusing, finding Caelen's face and holding there, present, human-shaped, ashamed.

"Sorry," Fenn said, wrecked, shaking. "Didn't mean to bring that near your fire."

"You didn't choose it," Caelen said, quiet, and understood, saying it, that he meant the words for both of them at once. "Nobody chooses the flare. I've spent nine years learning that the choosing only ever happens after, in what you do once it's passed. You did that part. You came back to me instead of staying lost in it. That's the whole of what any of us can actually manage, most days."

---

They sat together afterward at the forge's cooling edge, the late light going gold and then grey around them, and Fenn was quiet a long while before he spoke again, his voice steadier now but carrying something rawer underneath it than the brass-work banter of the days before.

"I keep thinking about what I'd have been," he said, "if nobody'd ever told me there was a rite at all. If I'd just kept spending myself the way I was, hauling salvage, chasing the same arithmetic that never quite came out even, until there wasn't enough of me left to notice it stopped." He looked at Caelen, something raw and searching in his exhausted face. "You've never gone that far. I can tell, somehow — the way you held that flare back just now, careful, like a man who's stood at that particular edge himself and knows exactly how close is too close. But you've never gone all the way over it. Have you."

"Once," Caelen said, quiet, honest, because he had learned across nine years that honesty was the only currency that had ever actually bought him anything worth keeping. "Stood right at the edge of it, in a chamber I won't ask you to imagine, with something vast and old and considerably more seductive than anything either of us has ever chased through a housing's brass. I chose the small shape of my life over it. Chose Varrick, and Rook, and the particular unglamorous work of staying who I already was." He held Fenn's gaze steady. "I don't say that to make what you're carrying sound smaller than it is. I say it because I don't think the difference between us is courage, or strength, or anything the Synod's doctrine would put a clean name to. I think it's luck, mostly, and a debt or two I got to pay down before it came due entirely. You didn't get the same luck. That's not a judgment on what you are. It's just the truth of it."

Fenn was quiet a long while, watching the forge's last coals settle to ash, and when he finally spoke again, his voice had found something Caelen recognized, with a private ache, as the same worn, hard-won steadiness Rook had once found at a fire in the Wastes.

"Figure I'll stay on, if your camp'll have me," he said. "Not for the rite. I understand now it's not going to hand me back what the arithmetic already spent. Just — for this. The forge. The company. Somebody who knows what it costs to stand this close to the edge and not go all the way over, even on the days I'm not entirely certain I managed it myself."

"Stay," Caelen said, simple, and found, saying it, that he meant it the same unarmored way Ansel once had, offering stew to a starving strata child without asking a single question that wasn't his to ask. "We'll figure the rest of it out together, same as everything else."
