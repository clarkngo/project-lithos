# Chapter Ten — What's Left to Spend

They buried the four raiders at dawn, unmarked as they'd died, because Caelen understood, standing over ground that had already taken so much of this life's cost without complaint, that a man's silence about who paid him didn't excuse the living from the small mercy of putting him properly into the earth regardless.

"No papers," Malakar said, methodical even now, a fresh bandage stark against the dried blood at his temple, going through the last of the dead men's effects with the same precision he'd once brought to a Warden case file. "No House marks, no Guild ledger tokens, nothing beyond the one bootlace Seraphina managed to call out before they silenced her. Professional to the last detail. Whoever commissioned this understood exactly how much a lack of evidence would cost us in the search that follows."

"Voss's colors," Rook said, flat, still bloodied from the fight, his fast rhythm gone entirely quiet in a way that had settled into him, Caelen understood, the same way it had settled after Ansel, and after the Sinking Fields, and after every hard cost this life had ever handed him to carry.

"Voss's colors on one dead man's bootlace," Malakar said, careful, "which could mean Voss commissioned this directly, or could mean someone wanted us to believe exactly that while a different ledger did the actual paying. I don't say this to muddy what feels, I know, like the one clear thread we've got. I say it because I spent eleven years learning that the clearest thread in a case file is often the one somebody left for you to find on purpose."

"Then we find out which," Caelen said. "Properly. Not guessing at a fire, arguing over one bootlace until the argument itself becomes an excuse not to move."

---

The question of the camp itself came next, and it was Fenn, unexpectedly, who raised it plainly while the others were still circling it sideways.

"You can't keep treating people here," he said, quiet, from where he sat nursing a burn along one forearm that would join the rest of his body's honest accounting of what this life kept asking of him. "Not this season. Maybe not for considerably longer than that. Whoever did this knows exactly where to find you now, knows the camp has no real walls beyond the ones you've built out of each other's willingness to fight for it. Bringing more families here right now isn't kindness. It's handing Voss, or Kell, or whoever's actual hand is on this, a second chance at the same leverage."

"He's right," Varrick said, before Rook could argue it, his voice carrying the particular flat authority he reserved for things he'd already decided were beyond debate. "The rite doesn't stop because we're frightened. It pauses because pausing is the only honest arithmetic left, until we know enough to promise the next family walking up that track something better than a raid in the night for their trouble."

Seraphina would have argued it, Caelen understood, watching the decision settle over the rest of them — would have stood exactly where Fenn now sat and insisted, plain and furious, that every paused season cost someone their own eight months. The fact that she wasn't there to make that argument herself was its own kind of grief, small and specific, folded into the larger one.

"When we bring her home," Caelen said, "it starts again. That's not a pause we're choosing lightly. It's a debt we're promising to pay back, the same as everything else."

---

The harder question waited until the practical ones had been settled, and Caelen found he could not make himself raise it directly, so it was Varrick, again, who set it down plainly on the table between them.

"I'm not coming," he said, into the quiet, before Caelen had found a way to ask it gently. "I want to. I want that said honestly, because I've spent nine years teaching you both that honesty costs less than the alternative, and I won't break my own rule now out of pride. But I can't keep pace with whatever ground this chase is going to cover, and a man who slows down a rescue is a man who costs the person being rescued considerably more than his own presence is worth." He looked at Rook, and something in his dry composure, for once, did not try to armor itself at all. "I need you to go. Both of you know that. I need you to also know I'm not sending you off alone in the sense that frightens you. Fenn's staying. So is the sympathizer network Malakar's been building, whichever of them can reach us fastest if trouble finds this camp again before you're back."

"I'll stay too, if you'd rather," Rook said, and Caelen heard, underneath the offer, how much it cost him to make it — the same impossible arithmetic he'd stood inside once already this book, weighed now against a version of it with Seraphina's name attached instead of Varrick's.

"No," Varrick said, gentle but final. "You go get her back. That's not me being brave, boy, whatever it sounds like. That's me understanding, plainly, that I've got considerably less use to any of you standing here dying slowly than you've got use for each other out there, moving fast. Let me spend what's left of me on being glad you went, instead of guilty that you stayed."

Fenn caught Caelen's eye across the fire, and nodded once, steady, the particular nod of a man who had learned, across one hard season, exactly what it meant to be trusted with something this heavy. "I'll keep him fed," he said, quiet. "Measured portions. I've had considerable practice lately at looking after somebody who won't ask for help until it's nearly too late to give it."

"I heard that," Varrick said, dry, and something in the fire's low light, for just a moment, let the whole exhausted camp remember how to almost smile.

---

They set out at first light the next morning, Caelen and Rook and Malakar, the last of Malakar's dried rations and Fenn's careful map-work — routes toward the Synod's outer holdings, sketched from half-remembered fragments of reliquary geography Seraphina herself had once traced for them — folded into a single worn satchel between them.

Caelen looked back once, from the same broken ridge where he'd once stood as a boy leaner and harder than the child who'd fallen from a mountain, and found Varrick and Fenn both still visible at the camp's edge, small against the scarred grey horizon, watching them go the way Varrick had once watched him choose, at a ridge just like this one, to walk forward into ground that offered no promise of safety.

"No clean read on who took her," Malakar said, quiet, falling into step beside him. "No clean read on where she's being held, or why, or what either Kell or Voss actually mean to do with her once whatever bargain they're building comes fully due. I want that said plainly before we've gone a single mile further, because I'd rather you walk into this with the shape of the uncertainty clear than let hope smooth it over into something simpler than it actually is."

"I know," Caelen said, and found, saying it, that the words carried none of the fear he might once have expected them to. Only the same old cold certainty that had carried him through the mountain, the shelter, the weigh-station, the Vault's vast lonely chamber, and every hard threshold since. "We don't need a clean read. We just need to start walking, and find out the rest of it the way we've found out everything else in this life. Badly, at first. Together, the whole way through."

He stepped down off the ridge, into ground he did not yet know the shape of, Rook and Malakar falling in beside him without a word needing to be said about it, and did not look back a second time, because he had long since stopped needing to check whether the people who mattered were still coming.
