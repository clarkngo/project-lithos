# Chapter Two — Every Family That Asks

By the third week after Fenn, the camp had stopped being able to pretend it was still the quiet fixed point it had been eight months building itself into.

They came in twos and threes now, not ones — a mother and her Shifted son, both gaunt with the particular gauntness of a family that had spent everything they had on unlicensed housings and had nothing left over for food; a Guild factor's widow who'd heard the rumor third-hand and walked eleven days on the strength of a story she half didn't believe; two brothers who arrived separately, a week apart, each convinced the other was already lost and neither willing to say so first. Word did not travel the way rumors were supposed to travel, slow and thinning with distance. It traveled instead the way desperate things always seemed to, fast and whole and utterly indifferent to how many people a small stone-ringed clearing could actually hold.

"We turned two away yesterday," Seraphina said, over the cold remains of a breakfast none of them had finished, her voice carrying none of its old careful architecture at all anymore, worn down past performance by simple exhaustion. "A man from the eastern settlements, and a girl no older than twelve traveling with her uncle. I told them to come back in a month. I don't know if either of them has a month left to spend waiting on my schedule."

"You can't hold every debt in this territory on your own two hands," Varrick said, gentler than his usual dryness, though Caelen caught the small effort it cost him to lean forward over the fire, a stiffness in his ribs that eight months had not eased the way any of them had let themselves hope. "Man who tries to carry every weight offered him ends up carrying none of them properly. That's not cruelty, girl. That's arithmetic, same as everything else this life's ever taught any of us."

"Then help me build the arithmetic properly," Seraphina said, and there was no argument in it, only plain exhausted request. "Because right now I am doing it by instinct and guilt in roughly equal measure, and neither of those is a policy."

They built one, that morning, the four of them and Malakar besides, in the flat unglamorous way real policies always got built — not with ceremony, but with a list scratched onto the back of a salvage manifest. Severity first: the further gone, the sooner seen, regardless of how long they'd waited or how far they'd walked. A season's worth of slots, no more, because Seraphina's own body could only pay the rite's cost so many times before her own reserves ran as thin as any patient's. And a rule none of them liked saying aloud but all of them understood was necessary now: nobody came within sight of the actual clearing until Malakar or Caelen had spoken to them first, alone, away from Seraphina entirely.

"You think somebody's going to come pretending," Rook said, flat, understanding the shape of the rule before anyone explained it.

"I think," Malakar said, precise even now, eight months out from every doctrine he'd once built his whole life around, "that a rumor this valuable, spreading this fast, is exactly the kind of rumor an interested party tests before they act on it. I would rather we find their tester before their tester finds us worth acting against."

---

The tester came four days later, and Caelen understood, watching him approach the interview post Malakar had set up at the camp's outer marker, that he would not have caught the wrongness himself if Malakar hadn't spent the last four days teaching him, patiently, exactly what to watch for.

The man gave his name as Corin, and his story was good — a sister, he said, Shifted young and Shifted often, hidden now in a settlement two days south because the family couldn't risk a Warden patrol catching sight of her. He had the details a real supplicant would have: the particular exhausted grief in his voice, the careful way he avoided naming which settlement exactly, as though protecting her even now. Caelen, watching from a few strides off the way Malakar had positioned him, found himself believing it for the better part of ten full minutes.

It was Malakar who caught the seam.

"Which Vector," Malakar asked, mild, midway through a question that had sounded, until that moment, like ordinary sympathetic follow-up. "Your sister's conduit. You said she's Shifted young and often — a family watching that closely would know the Form Vector by now, even an unlicensed housing. What shape does she take."

Corin's answer came a half-beat too fast, and a half-beat too confident — a term Caelen recognized, distantly, from Seraphina's own reliquary vocabulary, not the kind of word a frightened brother from a fringe settlement would reach for smoothly under pressure. "Talon," he said. "A Talon Vector."

"That's the second most common answer to that question in the whole licensed trade," Malakar said, still mild, though something in his stance had shifted, weight settling low and ready in a way Caelen had only seen in him during actual danger. "Convenient, if a man were guessing rather than grieving. Who sent you, Corin. And I'd advise you answer that plainly, because the next man who asks you that question may not extend you the same courtesy of asking twice."

Corin ran.

He did not get far — Rook, positioned exactly where Malakar's four days of preparation had put him, brought him down before he'd cleared the second rise, and Caelen arrived a breath later to find the man face-down in the scarred dirt, Rook's knee planted between his shoulder blades, all pretense of grief entirely gone from his voice now, replaced by something flatter and considerably less rehearsed.

"I don't know who," Corin said, muffled, genuine fear finally surfacing under the fear he'd been performing. "That's God's honest truth, I don't know the name behind it. I was paid, through a broker in Underlow, to confirm the rumor and count what I could — how many structures, how many people, how often patients come and how far they've traveled to reach here. That's the whole of it. I was never meant to get near enough to be caught. Whoever's paying clearly didn't credit you lot with a Warden's own eye watching the gate."

---

They let him go, in the end — Malakar's judgment, over Rook's open objection, that a dead or imprisoned tester would only confirm to whoever sent him that the rumor was worth escalating over, while a frightened one sent back with nothing useful to report might buy them a season of the same uncertainty their patients were paying to escape.

"He wasn't lying about not knowing the name," Malakar said, once Corin's retreating shape had shrunk to nothing against the horizon. "I've questioned enough men who genuinely didn't know to recognize the particular shape of that ignorance. Which means whoever's paying is being considerably more careful than a Guild factor or a garrison Warden usually bothers to be. That narrows the field to precisely the two kinds of interest I was hoping, against my own better judgment, it might not be."

"Guild," Caelen said, quiet, "or Synod."

"Or both, working separately, neither one aware yet that the other's doing the same counting," Malakar said. "Which is, I'll admit, the possibility that troubles me most. Two patient appetites converging on the same rumor rarely stays a coincidence for very long."

Seraphina had come to stand at the camp's outer marker by then, having heard enough of the shape of it to understand without needing every detail spelled out plainly, and Caelen watched something settle behind her eyes that was not quite fear and not quite resolve, but the particular exhausted alloy of both that he recognized, by now, as her permanent working state.

"I am not stopping," she said, before anyone had asked her to consider it. "I want that said plainly, the same way I said it to my own House three years ago. Whoever's counting us, whatever ledger they're building — every name on it is also a name of someone who walked here believing there was nothing left to try. I will not let a stranger's arithmetic decide, on my behalf, that their walking was for nothing."

"Nobody's asking you to stop," Varrick said, dry, though the dryness carried, as it always did now, a weight underneath it he no longer bothered fully hiding. "We're asking you to let the rest of us build a wall around the stopping-you-from-stopping, so the whole thing doesn't come apart the way Hollow Cut did the last time somebody decided watching from a distance wasn't information enough for them anymore."

Caelen looked out at the flat silver horizon where Corin's shape had finally vanished entirely, and felt the same old cold certainty settle into him that had carried him through the mountain, the shelter, the weigh-station, the Vault's vast lonely chamber — the understanding, plain and unavoidable, that whatever peace the camp had built across eight quiet months had just, formally, ended.

"Then we build the wall," he said. "And we keep seeing every family that asks, for as long as we've got a season left to give them." He looked at Seraphina, and at Rook, and at Varrick easing himself back down by the fire with a stiffness none of them yet said aloud, and at Malakar already turning, precise and unhurried, to consider the camp's outer approaches with a Warden's old eye for exactly where a next tester might come from. "That's the arithmetic now. We just have to figure out how to keep paying it before somebody bigger than Corin decides to collect."
